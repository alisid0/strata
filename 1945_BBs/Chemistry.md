## 1. Atomic structure

### The science of exceptions
*Card 33 · chemistry · foundations*

**Idea.** Physics loves clean rules: gravity always pulls down, a straight line is always straight. **Chemistry** is the science of exceptions.

It's messy. Elements misbehave, reactions run backward, molecules change shape at random. Getting a pattern-based science out of that chaos was a real achievement.

**Concrete.** But it had to be done, because mastering this messy science is how we survive.

Without it there's no medicine to cure disease, no rust-proofing for aircraft, no way to filter water or freeze-dry food for orbit. Chemistry is an engine of survival.

**Definition.** To get chemistry, you have to grasp scale, just how small things go.

It's smaller than you think. A single grain of sand is a mountain next to what we're dealing with. And here's the magic: in that smallness sits an enormous effect on the large world.

**In action.** It takes billions of atoms to make a speck of dust, yet their tiny shapes decide everything above.

Move one atom in a large molecule and a life-saving medicine can turn into a poison. The microscopic structure controls the visible world.

**Deeper.** Which leaves scientists a problem.

To build a bridge you count steel beams. To build a medicine you'd need to count atoms. How do you measure and count particles you can't see at all?

### The chemist's dozen
*Card 34 · chemistry · stoichiometry*

**Idea.** How do people count things? With grouping words.

At a bakery you ask for a **dozen** eggs, not twelve. Lincoln said "four **score**," not eighty years. A hundred years is a **century**.

**Concrete.** Dozen, score, century: just words standing for numbers, to make counting big things easier.

Chemists need to count atoms to mix their recipes. But atoms are so small that asking for a dozen of them is useless. You couldn't see a trillion.

**Definition.** So they invented a new grouping word, the biggest in history: the **mole**.

Just as a dozen always means 12, a mole always means about 602,200,000,000,000,000,000,000 things. Just a number, nothing mystical.

**In action.** To feel how huge it is against how small atoms are: a mole of marbles would bury the Earth's surface miles deep. But a mole of water molecules fits in a single shot glass.

That's the microscopic scale. The number is vast, the particles almost ghosts.

**Deeper.** That number is the translator between the invisible world and the visible one.

1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particlesThe bridge between counting atoms and weighing grams.Because of it, a chemist can put a cup of powder on an ordinary scale and know exactly how many trillions of atoms are inside, without counting one.

### The cosmic menu
*Card 35 · chemistry · periodic table*

**Idea.** The whole world is made from just 118 ingredients.

Think of everything you've ever seen: every star, every ocean, every building, the screen you're reading on, the eyes you're reading with. All of it is built from the same short recipe list.

**Concrete.** Before you say "what about dark matter, or the God particle?" — yes, there are stranger, deeper mysteries out there. We'll keep those for later.

For the physical, touchable universe, the stuff you can weigh and handle, those 118 ingredients are everything.

**Definition.** These ingredients are called **elements**, and they're laid out on one famous chart: the **periodic table**.

Don't read it as a poster to memorise. Read it as the inventory of reality, the complete list of what the universe is made of.

**In action.** Just as a baker mixes flour, butter and sugar into hundreds of pastries, nature mixes these elements.

Take three, carbon, hydrogen, oxygen. Combine them one way and you get the sugar in your coffee. Another way, rocket fuel. Another, a tree trunk. The magic isn't endless ingredients, it's how they link up.

**Deeper.** So what makes element 8 (oxygen) behave so differently from element 79 (gold)?

To answer that, things are about to get small. Smaller than you can easily imagine. We have to look inside the ingredients themselves.

### The 80th cut
*Card 36 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** Take a piece of iron, a 100-gram chunk about the size of a golf ball, held between two fingers.

Now take a magically sharp knife and start cutting it in half. How many cuts until you reach the smallest possible piece of iron? Millions? Billions? Let's find out.

**Concrete.** First cut: two halves, 50 grams each. Again: 25 grams. Cut three: 12.5. Cut four: 6.25. It shrinks fast. By cut 10 it's smaller than a grain of sand.

But we keep going. We're chasing the very bottom of matter.

**Definition.** By cut 30 it's invisible to the eye. By cut 50, smaller than a virus. By cut 70, we're past the DNA inside a cell.

We cut until the knife simply won't divide it any further. It can't stay iron and get smaller. And the number of cuts it took is surprisingly small: exactly **80**.

**In action.** After 80 halvings you're left with a single dot of matter, the very basement of the universe.

You've reached **the atom**, the building block of all 118 ingredients. Cut this final dot and it stops being iron at all, breaking into something else entirely.

**Deeper.** The word atom comes from the Greek *atomos*. It means **uncuttable**.

the atomThe smallest recognisable piece of any element on the periodic table.Gold, oxygen, carbon, iron: every element is just a collection of these uncuttable dots. But what makes an iron atom different from a gold one? We have to look inside the dot.

### The three puzzle pieces
*Card 37 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** Here's a twist. That "uncuttable" atom? Not quite true. It isn't the smallest thing after all.

In the early 1900s scientists cracked the atom open, and what they found changed everything. The atom isn't a solid dot. It's a container.

**Concrete.** Inside are just three smaller pieces: **protons, neutrons and electrons**.

This is the real plot twist. An iron atom and a gold atom look nothing alike in the world, but zoom in and they're built from the exact same three parts. There's no "gold proton" or "iron electron." A proton is a proton, everywhere.

**Definition.** How are they arranged? Protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge) are packed tightly in a heavy clump at the dead centre, the **nucleus**.

The electrons (negative charge) are tiny, and they swarm around the outside at blinding speed, like a cloud.

**In action.** At this scale, the "type" of matter stops existing. There's only counting.

The only thing making an atom behave like gold, or oxygen, or iron is the *number* of these pieces inside. The universe isn't a kitchen with 118 ingredients. It's a Lego set with three kinds of brick.

**Deeper.** The key brick is the proton. Its count is an element's ID card. Exactly 26 protons in the centre means iron. 79 means gold.

atomic number (Z) = number of protonsChange the proton count and you change the element itself.That's what the periodic table is really sorting: it starts at hydrogen (1 proton) and counts up, one at a time.

### Isotopes: same element, different weight
*BB 151 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** Not every atom of the same element weighs the same. Carbon, the element of life, comes in at least three versions — identical in every chemical way, but with different masses. The only difference is the number of neutrons in the nucleus. These variants are isotopes.

**Concrete.** Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons — 12 particles in the nucleus. Carbon-13 has 6 protons and 7 neutrons — 13 particles. Carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons — 14 particles. All three are carbon. They all have 6 protons, so they all have 6 electrons arranged the same way, so they all form the same bonds and make the same compounds. Chemically, they are indistinguishable. The only difference is mass — and for carbon-14, instability: it slowly decays, which is how radiocarbon dating works.

**Definition.** **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element — same number of protons, same atomic number Z — but with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different **mass numbers** A (where A = protons + neutrons). Isotopes are identified by their mass number: carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14. Most elements exist as a mixture of isotopes. The chemical properties of isotopes are nearly identical because chemical behaviour is determined by electrons, not neutrons.

**In action.** The atomic masses on the periodic table are not whole numbers because they are weighted averages of all naturally occurring isotopes. Chlorine's atomic mass is 35.5 — not because any chlorine atom has a half-neutron, but because chlorine in nature is about 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Isotopes are also why the standard kilogram was once defined by a single platinum-iridium cylinder in Paris — a physical artefact whose mass could change, unlike the isotopic-constant-based definition adopted in 2019.

### Atomic number and mass number: the two-number ID
*BB 152 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** Every atom carries two key numbers. The atomic number Z tells you what element it is — the number of protons, fixed and non-negotiable. The mass number A tells you which isotope it is — the total of protons and neutrons. Z is the identity. A is the heft.

**Concrete.** An atom with Z = 11 is sodium, always and forever. Change the proton count and you change the element — sodium to magnesium, oxygen to fluorine. The mass number A can vary without changing the element: sodium-23 has 11 protons and 12 neutrons (A = 23). The numbers are written as ²³₁₁Na — mass number on top, atomic number below, symbol to the right. The atomic number is often omitted because the symbol already implies it — Na is always Z = 11.

**Definition.** The **atomic number** Z is the number of protons in the nucleus. It uniquely identifies the element and determines its position on the periodic table. In a neutral atom, Z also equals the number of electrons. The **mass number** A is the total number of nucleons — protons plus neutrons. The number of neutrons is A − Z. Notation: ᴬ₂X, where X is the element symbol. The periodic table lists elements in order of increasing Z; the atomic mass listed is the weighted average of isotopic masses.

**In action.** Z answers "what is it?" A answers "how heavy is this version?" Together they let you calculate the number of each subatomic particle in any atom: protons = Z, electrons = Z (neutral atom), neutrons = A − Z. For ²³₁₁Na: 11 protons, 11 electrons, 12 neutrons. For ³⁵₁₇Cl: 17 protons, 17 electrons, 18 neutrons. This two-number system — Z for identity, A for mass — is the universal ID card for every atom in the universe.

### Electron shells: where the electrons live
*BB 153 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** Electrons don't orbit the nucleus like planets around a sun — that's a useful picture, not the true one. But it captures something real: electrons are organised in layers, like the rungs of a ladder. Each rung — each shell — can hold only so many electrons before the next one starts to fill. And it's the outermost shell that decides almost everything about how an atom behaves.

**Concrete.** Sodium has 11 electrons. They fill from the inside out: 2 in the first shell (the closest to the nucleus), 8 in the second shell, and 1 left over in the third shell — written as 2,8,1. That lone electron in the outermost shell is restless. It wants to leave. Chlorine has 17 electrons: 2,8,7. Its outer shell is almost full — it desperately wants one more. This is why sodium and chlorine react so violently together: one wants to give an electron away, the other wants to take it.

**Definition.** **Electron shells** (or energy levels) are regions around the nucleus where electrons are found, labelled n = 1, 2, 3, ... from innermost outward. Each shell has a maximum capacity: shell 1 holds 2 electrons, shell 2 holds 8, shell 3 holds 18 (but is stable at 8 for the first 20 elements), shell 4 holds 32. The **valence shell** is the outermost occupied shell — its electrons, the **valence electrons**, determine the atom's chemical properties and bonding behaviour.

**In action.** The shell model explains the shape of the periodic table. Elements in the same column have the same number of valence electrons — that's why they behave similarly. Lithium (2,1), sodium (2,8,1), and potassium (2,8,8,1) all have one valence electron — all are soft, reactive metals that explode in water. The periodic table isn't a random grid; it's a map of electron configurations, and the rows and columns follow the filling of shells. The octet rule — atoms tend to want 8 valence electrons — is the single most useful rule in predicting chemical behaviour.

### Electronic configuration: the address of each electron
*BB 154 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** Shells are the neighbourhoods. But within each shell, electrons don't all live at the same address. Each shell is divided into subshells — s, p, d, f — each with a different shape and capacity. The electronic configuration of an atom is the full postal code of every electron: which shell, which subshell, how many.

**Concrete.** Carbon has 6 electrons. They fill in order of increasing energy: 1s gets 2, 2s gets 2, 2p gets the remaining 2. Written as 1s² 2s² 2p². Oxygen has 8: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. Sodium has 11: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. The superscript is the electron count in that subshell. The order of filling is not always the obvious one — 4s fills before 3d because 4s is actually lower in energy for those elements. The Aufbau principle ("building up") gives the full filling order.

**Definition.** **Electronic configuration** is the distribution of electrons among the shells and subshells of an atom. Subshells: s (holds 2, spherical), p (holds 6, dumbbell-shaped, three orientations), d (holds 10, five orientations), f (holds 14, seven orientations). The filling order follows the **Aufbau principle**, **Pauli exclusion principle** (max two electrons per orbital, opposite spins), and **Hund's rule** (electrons occupy separate orbitals within a subshell before pairing up). The noble gas shorthand compresses the inner configuration: Na = [Ne] 3s¹.

**In action.** The electronic configuration is the real reason the periodic table has the shape it does. The s-block (two columns on the left) fills the s subshell. The p-block (six columns on the right) fills the p subshell. The d-block (ten columns in the middle) fills the d subshell — these are the transition metals. The f-block (the two rows usually shown detached) fills the f subshell — the lanthanides and actinides. The table is not a design choice; it's a direct map of quantum mechanics. The configuration is why copper is coloured, why iron is magnetic, and why gold doesn't tarnish.

### Ions: atoms that have lost or gained electrons
*BB 155 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** An atom is neutral — protons and electrons balance. But remove an electron and the balance tips — the atom becomes a positively charged ion. Add an electron and it becomes negatively charged. An ion is just an atom with an imbalance. And that imbalance changes everything about how it behaves.

**Concrete.** Sodium has 11 protons and 11 electrons — neutral. Lose one electron and it becomes Na⁺, with 11 protons but only 10 electrons — a net charge of +1. Chlorine has 17 protons and 17 electrons. Gain one electron and it becomes Cl⁻, with 17 protons and 18 electrons — a net charge of −1. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have opposite charges, so they attract — they form the ionic compound NaCl, table salt. The neutral sodium atom explodes in water; the sodium ion dissolves harmlessly. Losing one electron transforms the atom completely.

**Definition.** An **ion** is an atom or molecule that has gained or lost one or more electrons, giving it a net electric charge. A **cation** (positive ion) forms when an atom loses electrons — metals tend to form cations (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻). An **anion** (negative ion) forms when an atom gains electrons — non-metals tend to form anions (Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻). The charge is written as a superscript: Na⁺, Ca²⁺, O²⁻, Cl⁻. Ions of opposite charge attract each other with an electrostatic force — this is the basis of ionic bonding.

**In action.** Atoms form ions to achieve a stable electron configuration — typically the configuration of the nearest noble gas (8 valence electrons, a full outer shell). Sodium (2,8,1) loses one to become Na⁺ (2,8) — the same configuration as neon. Chlorine (2,8,7) gains one to become Cl⁻ (2,8,8) — the configuration of argon. The drive toward a full outer shell is the engine of ion formation, and by extension, of all ionic bonding. Ions are not just charged atoms — they are atoms that have solved their instability by giving up or taking on electrons.

### The noble gases: already full, already happy
*BB 156 · chemistry · atomic structure*

**Idea.** At the far right of the periodic table sits a column of elements that don't react with anything. They don't bond, don't burn, don't combine. They are complete. Their outer shells are already full — they have nothing to gain, nothing to give, and no reason to engage with any other atom. These are the noble gases.

**Concrete.** Helium has 2 electrons — its only shell is full (capacity 2). Neon has 10 electrons: 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second — both shells full. Argon has 18: 2,8,8 — full. Each noble gas has a complete outer shell with no room and no vacancies. A sodium atom desperately wants to be like neon (hence Na⁺). A chlorine atom desperately wants to be like argon (hence Cl⁻). The noble gases are the destination — every other atom is trying to get there.

**Definition.** The **noble gases** (Group 18 / VIIIA) — helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon — are characterised by having a completely filled outer electron shell. This full-shell configuration is exceptionally stable. As a result, noble gases are chemically **inert** under most conditions — they do not readily form compounds. The **octet rule** — the observation that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve 8 valence electrons — is named after this noble gas stability. "Octet" = eight electrons in the outer shell.

**In action.** The noble gases are not just a curiosity — they are the framework for understanding all chemical bonding. Every bond type — ionic, covalent, metallic — is fundamentally about atoms rearranging electrons to achieve noble gas configurations. The octet rule, with exceptions (hydrogen wants 2, boron is comfortable with 6, elements beyond period 3 can expand their octet), is the single most predictive rule in introductory chemistry. The noble gases are the baseline. Everything else is a departure — and a return.

## 2. Chemical bonding

### Why atoms bond: the drive to fill the shell
*BB 157 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Two hydrogen atoms meet in space. Neither has a full shell — each has one lonely electron, wanting one more to reach helium's stability. So they share. They move close enough that their electron clouds overlap, and each nucleus now feels the pull of both electrons. The two atoms become one molecule — H₂ — and the arrangement is more stable than either atom alone. This is bonding: atoms sacrifice independence for stability.

**Concrete.** A sodium atom (2,8,1) and a chlorine atom (2,8,7) are both unstable in their own way. Sodium's lone valence electron is weakly held; chlorine's almost-full shell exerts a strong pull for one more. Sodium gives its electron to chlorine. Both achieve noble gas configurations: Na⁺ (2,8) like neon, Cl⁻ (2,8,8) like argon. The resulting oppositely charged ions attract — an ionic bond. The energy of the Na⁺Cl⁻ pair is lower than the energy of Na + Cl separately. The difference is released as heat. Bonding releases energy because the bonded state is more stable.

**Definition.** Atoms bond to achieve a lower, more stable energy state — almost always by attaining a full outer electron shell (the noble gas configuration). The three main strategies: **give and take** (ionic bonding — one atom transfers electrons to another), **share** (covalent bonding — atoms pool electrons), and **communal pool** (metallic bonding — electrons delocalise across many atoms). All bonding is electrostatic at root: the attraction between positive nuclei and negative electrons, arranged in a configuration that minimises total energy.

**In action.** Bonding is not a choice atoms "want" to make — it's a consequence of the laws of electrostatics and quantum mechanics. When the total energy of a bonded system is lower than the total energy of the separated atoms, the bond forms spontaneously. The energy released when a bond forms is the **bond energy**; the same energy must be supplied to break the bond. This energy accounting — bonds forming releases energy, bonds breaking absorbs energy — is the foundation of thermochemistry and the reason fuel burns and food sustains us.

### Ionic bonding: give and take
*BB 158 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Sodium gives an electron. Chlorine takes it. They are now oppositely charged, and opposite charges attract — hard. This is ionic bonding in one sentence: one atom transfers an electron to another, the resulting ions stick together electrostatically, and a compound is born.

**Concrete.** Hold a piece of sodium metal. Soft, silvery, you can cut it with a knife. Drop it in chlorine gas — a pale green poison — and it erupts into a brilliant yellow flame. What's left is a white crystalline powder: sodium chloride, table salt. The sodium atoms each gave an electron to a chlorine atom. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions packed together in a regular, repeating 3D grid — an **ionic lattice** — each sodium surrounded by six chlorides, each chloride by six sodiums. The substance that emerges — brittle, high-melting, dissolving in water to conduct electricity — shares almost no properties with either parent element.

**Definition.** **Ionic bonding** is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when one or more electrons are transferred from a metal atom (low electronegativity) to a non-metal atom (high electronegativity). The resulting **ionic compound** is a crystalline lattice — not individual molecules, but an extended, repeating arrangement of ions. Properties: high melting and boiling points (strong electrostatic forces throughout), brittle (ions of like charge repel if layers shift), conduct electricity when molten or dissolved (ions are free to move), do not conduct as solids (ions are locked in place).

**In action.** Ionic bonding is not just NaCl. Magnesium oxide (MgO) — Mg loses two electrons to become Mg²⁺, O gains two to become O²⁻. The double charges mean a stronger attraction — MgO melts at 2,852°C, used to line furnaces. Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) — the ionic lattice that makes up limestone, chalk, and seashells. The formula of an ionic compound is not a molecular formula but an **empirical formula** — the simplest whole-number ratio of ions that balances the charges: Na⁺Cl⁻ (1:1), Mg²⁺O²⁻ (1:1), Ca²⁺(Cl⁻)₂ (1:2). There is no "molecule" of NaCl — just a lattice of alternating ions.

### Covalent bonding: sharing is the strategy
*BB 159 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** What if neither atom is willing to fully give up an electron? They share instead. Each atom contributes one electron to a shared pair, and both nuclei are attracted to both electrons — the pair acts as the glue. This is covalent bonding, and it's how the vast majority of substances in your body are held together.

**Concrete.** Two hydrogen atoms, each with one electron. Neither can rip an electron from the other — they're equally matched. So they overlap. The two electrons now occupy the space between the two nuclei. Each nucleus is pulled toward the electron pair, and the pair is pulled toward both nuclei. The result is H₂ — a single covalent bond. Two fluorine atoms, each with 7 valence electrons, share one pair to form F₂ — both achieve a full octet. Oxygen atoms share two pairs (a double bond) to form O₂. Nitrogen atoms share three pairs (a triple bond) to form N₂ — one of the strongest bonds in nature.

**Definition.** **Covalent bonding** is the sharing of one or more electron pairs between two atoms, typically non-metals. A **single bond** shares one pair (H−H, F−F). A **double bond** shares two pairs (O=O). A **triple bond** shares three pairs (N≡N). Covalent bonds form discrete **molecules** — finite groups of atoms with a fixed formula — unlike ionic lattices. Bond strength increases from single to triple: a triple bond is shorter and stronger. **Lewis structures** (dot-and-cross diagrams) show how valence electrons are shared.

**In action.** Covalent bonding is the chemistry of life. Water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), DNA — all are covalent molecules. The carbon atom, with 4 valence electrons, forms 4 covalent bonds — allowing it to build chains, rings, and frameworks of almost infinite variety. This is why carbon is the backbone of organic chemistry and of life itself. The shapes of covalent molecules — linear (CO₂), bent (H₂O), tetrahedral (CH₄) — are determined by the number of electron pairs around the central atom repelling each other into the arrangement that minimises repulsion. Shape determines function, in chemistry as in biology.

### Metallic bonding: a sea of electrons
*BB 160 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Metals don't transfer electrons and they don't share them in pairs. Instead, every metal atom in the lattice releases its valence electrons into a communal pool — a sea of electrons that flows freely between the positive metal ions. The ions are fixed in place; the electrons are not. This is metallic bonding, and it explains why metals conduct, bend, and shine.

**Concrete.** Take a copper wire. The copper atoms are arranged in a regular lattice, but their outer electrons are not tied to any specific atom — they wander freely through the entire structure. Apply a voltage and the electrons drift — electric current. Hammer the wire and the layers of ions slide past each other — the sea of electrons flows around them and the metal bends instead of shattering. Shine light on the surface and the mobile electrons absorb and re-emit photons — the metal gleams. All three properties — conductivity, malleability, lustre — come from the same delocalised electrons.

**Definition.** **Metallic bonding** is the electrostatic attraction between a regular lattice of positive metal ions and a sea of **delocalised electrons** — valence electrons that are not bound to any specific atom but move freely throughout the structure. This model explains: (1) **Electrical conductivity** — mobile electrons carry charge. (2) **Thermal conductivity** — mobile electrons transfer kinetic energy. (3) **Malleability and ductility** — layers of ions can slide without breaking the bonding. (4) **Lustre** — mobile electrons interact with light. (5) Variable melting points — the strength of the metallic bond depends on the number of delocalised electrons and the size of the ions.

**In action.** Metallic bonding covers the whole periodic table's left and centre. Sodium (one delocalised electron per atom) is soft enough to cut with a knife, melts at 98°C. Tungsten (many delocalised electrons, small ions) is hard enough to drill through steel, melts at 3,422°C — the filament in incandescent light bulbs. Alloys exploit metallic bonding: adding a small amount of carbon to iron disrupts the iron lattice just enough to prevent layers sliding, producing steel — harder, stronger, but still bendable. The sea of electrons is why a bent copper pipe doesn't break, why a gold ring can be resized, and why aluminium foil exists.

## 3. Reactions & molecular architecture

### The mole in practice: from formula to grams and back
*BB 235 · chemistry · stoichiometry*

**Idea.** Card 34 introduced the mole as the chemist's dozen — 6.022 × 10²³ things. Now we use it. The mole is the bridge between the atomic world (which you can't weigh) and the lab bench (which you can). One equation — n = m/M — converts grams to moles and back. The periodic table gives you M (molar mass in g/mol). The balance gives you m (mass in grams). The mole number n is what you actually use in equations.

**Concrete.** How many moles in 80 g of NaOH? Na = 23, O = 16, H = 1. M(NaOH) = 23+16+1 = 40 g/mol. n = m/M = 80/40 = 2 moles. How many formula units? 2 × 6.022×10²³ = 1.204×10²⁴. Now the reverse: you need 0.5 moles of H₂SO₄. What mass? M(H₂SO₄) = 2(1)+32+4(16) = 98 g/mol. m = nM = 0.5×98 = 49 g. Weigh out 49 g on the balance and you have exactly 0.5 moles. The balance reads grams; the equation reads moles. n = m/M is the translator.

**Definition.** The fundamental mole equation: **n = m / M**, where n = amount in moles, m = mass in grams, M = molar mass in g/mol (numerically equal to the relative atomic/formula mass from the periodic table). For gases at RTP (room temperature and pressure, ~20°C, 1 atm): 1 mole of any gas occupies **24 dm³** (or 24,000 cm³). For solutions: **n = cV**, where c is concentration in mol/dm³ and V is volume in dm³. These three equations — n = m/M, n = V/24 (gases), n = cV (solutions) — cover the vast majority of mole calculations.

**In action.** Mole calculations are the daily arithmetic of chemistry. A pharmacist calculates the moles of active ingredient per tablet. An environmental chemist calculates the moles of CO₂ released per litre of fuel. A materials scientist calculates the moles of monomer needed to make a polymer of a target molecular weight. Every balanced equation is read in moles — "2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O" means "2 moles of H₂ react with 1 mole of O₂ to produce 2 moles of H₂O." The coefficients in the equation ARE mole ratios. Convert everything to moles first, then use the ratio, then convert back to the unit you need. This workflow never changes.

### Empirical and molecular formulas: what the numbers really mean
*BB 236 · chemistry · stoichiometry*

**Idea.** The molecular formula (C₆H₁₂O₆ for glucose) tells you the exact number of each atom in one molecule. The empirical formula (CH₂O) tells you the simplest whole-number ratio. They are related by a whole-number multiplier: molecular = empirical × n. The empirical formula comes from experiment — you burn the compound, measure the CO₂ and H₂O produced, and work backwards to the ratios of C, H, and O.

**Concrete.** A compound is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Assume 100 g: 40.0 g C, 6.7 g H, 53.3 g O. Convert to moles: n(C) = 40.0/12 = 3.33 mol, n(H) = 6.7/1 = 6.7 mol, n(O) = 53.3/16 = 3.33 mol. Divide by smallest (3.33): C = 1, H = 2, O = 1. Empirical formula: CH₂O. If the molar mass is found to be 180 g/mol, the molecular formula multiplier n = 180 / 30 = 6. Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂O₆ — glucose. The empirical formula (CH₂O) is the blueprint; the molecular formula (×6) is the actual building.

**Definition.** The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The **molecular formula** is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. Procedure: (1) Convert mass percentages (or masses) to moles using n = m/M. (2) Divide all mole values by the smallest to get the simplest ratio. (3) If the ratios are not whole numbers (e.g. 1.5), multiply all by a factor to clear fractions. (4) The result is the empirical formula. (5) To get the molecular formula, divide the compound's molar mass by the empirical formula mass — the result is the multiplier n. Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ.

**In action.** Empirical formula determination is the detective work of chemistry. An unknown white powder — what is it? Burn it, measure the combustion products, calculate the empirical formula, measure the molar mass, deduce the molecular formula, then search the database for a match. This is how new compounds are identified and how purity is verified. For ionic compounds, the empirical formula IS the formula (NaCl, CaCl₂) because ionic lattices don't have discrete molecules — the formula gives the ion ratio, not a molecular unit. The term "molecular formula" only strictly applies to covalent molecules. For ionic compounds, we use "formula unit."

### Relative atomic mass: why chlorine is 35.5
*BB 237 · chemistry · stoichiometry*

**Idea.** Look at the periodic table. Chlorine's atomic mass is 35.5 — not a whole number. But you can't have half a proton or half a neutron. The reason: chlorine in nature is a mixture of two isotopes — 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. The mass on the table is the weighted average. No single chlorine atom weighs 35.5; that's the average you'd get if you scooped up a handful.

**Concrete.** Natural chlorine: 75.77% Cl-35 (mass = 34.97 u) and 24.23% Cl-37 (mass = 36.97 u). Weighted average: (0.7577 × 34.97) + (0.2423 × 36.97) = 26.50 + 8.96 = 35.46 ≈ 35.5. Bromine: 50.7% Br-79 and 49.3% Br-81. Average: (0.507 × 79) + (0.493 × 81) ≈ 79.9. The periodic table mass listed for each element is this weighted average of its naturally occurring isotopes. The unit is the unified atomic mass unit (u), defined as 1/12 the mass of one carbon-12 atom — so by definition, carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12 u.

**Definition.** **Relative atomic mass** A_r of an element is the weighted average mass of its naturally occurring isotopes relative to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It is dimensionless (a ratio) but numerically equal to the molar mass in g/mol. For an element with isotopes of masses m₁, m₂, ... and fractional abundances a₁, a₂, ...: A_r = a₁m₁ + a₂m₂ + ... where a₁+a₂+... = 1. **Relative molecular mass** M_r is the sum of the A_r values of all atoms in the molecular formula. **Relative formula mass** is the same concept applied to ionic compounds.

**In action.** The non-integer masses on the periodic table are not rounding errors — they are evidence of isotopes. Mass spectrometry measures isotopic ratios with extraordinary precision, and from these the weighted average is computed. The same principle applies to forensic science: the isotopic ratio of carbon (¹³C/¹²C) in a sample can reveal whether a material came from a plant using C3 or C4 photosynthesis — tracing the origin of drugs, food, or fibres. The decimal places on the periodic table are not imperfections; they're fingerprints of nuclear physics preserved in the chemistry of the Earth's crust.

### Ionic lattices: the giant structure and its consequences
*BB 238 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** An ionic bond is not a single Na⁺–Cl⁻ pair floating alone. It's a giant, repeating 3D lattice of alternating positive and negative ions, extending in all directions. A grain of salt contains billions of ions in a single continuous structure. There are no "molecules" of NaCl — just a crystal. And this giant structure dictates every property: high melting point, brittleness, and the ability to conduct when molten.

**Concrete.** Sodium chloride melts at 801°C. Why so high? Every Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions, and every Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺ ions. To melt the crystal, you must overcome the electrostatic attraction between every ion and all its neighbours simultaneously — a colossal energy requirement. Magnesium oxide (MgO) melts at 2,852°C because Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have double the charge, so the attraction is four times stronger. Now strike a salt crystal with a hammer: it shatters. Why? The blow shifts a layer of ions so that like charges (Na⁺ next to Na⁺) align — they repel violently and the crystal cleaves. Ionic solids are brittle because their strength is also their weakness: the rigid lattice tolerates no misalignment.

**Definition.** An **ionic lattice** is a giant, regular, repeating 3D arrangement of oppositely charged ions held together by strong electrostatic forces throughout the structure. Properties arising from the lattice: (1) **High melting/boiling points** — strong electrostatic forces require large energy to overcome. (2) **Hard but brittle** — hard because the forces are strong; brittle because a small shift brings like charges together. (3) **Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved** — free-moving ions carry charge; as solids, ions are fixed and cannot conduct. (4) **Soluble in water** (many, not all) — water molecules surround and separate the ions.

**In action.** The lattice model explains why we write NaCl, not Na₁Cl₁, Na₂Cl₂, or NaₙClₙ — the formula gives the simplest ion ratio, not a molecular count. It explains why salt water conducts electricity (ions are free) but solid salt does not (ions locked in place). It explains why MgO lines furnaces (enormous lattice energy = enormous melting point). It explains the octahedral shape of NaCl and the different crystal habits of different ionic compounds. The properties of the substance are not in any single ion pair — they emerge from the cooperative behaviour of the entire lattice.

### Covalent networks: diamond, graphite, and giant molecules
*BB 239 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Some covalent substances form molecules (H₂O, CO₂ — small, discrete). Others form giant covalent networks — every atom bonded to neighbouring atoms in a continuous 3D framework. Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. One is the hardest natural substance on Earth; the other is soft enough to write with. The atoms are identical; the bonding pattern is everything.

**Concrete.** In **diamond**, each carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds to four other carbons, arranged in a rigid tetrahedral 3D network. Every electron is locked in a bond — no free electrons. Result: hardest natural substance, doesn't conduct electricity, extremely high melting point (~3500°C), transparent when pure. In **graphite**, each carbon forms three bonds in a flat hexagonal sheet. The fourth electron from each carbon becomes delocalised — free to move between the sheets. The sheets are held together by weak van der Waals forces and slide easily. Result: soft (sheets slip), conducts electricity (delocalised electrons), high melting point (strong in-plane bonds), opaque and shiny. Same element. Two bonding patterns. Opposite properties.

**Definition.** **Giant covalent structures** (network solids) are continuous 3D frameworks where atoms are joined by covalent bonds throughout the entire structure. There are no discrete molecules — the whole crystal is one giant molecule. Examples: diamond (C, tetrahedral, each C bonded to 4 others), graphite (C, hexagonal layers, each C bonded to 3), silicon dioxide SiO₂ (each Si bonded to 4 O, each O bonded to 2 Si). Properties: very high melting points (strong covalent bonds throughout), generally hard, do not conduct electricity (except graphite — delocalised electrons), insoluble in water. **Allotropes** are different structural forms of the same element — diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon.

**In action.** The diamond/graphite dichotomy is the most dramatic example of structure determining properties in all of chemistry. Diamond's hardness makes it an industrial abrasive and cutting tool. Graphite's slipperiness makes it a dry lubricant and pencil "lead." Graphite's electrical conductivity makes it useful for electrodes and brushes in motors. The recently discovered allotropes — graphene (single graphite sheet, strongest material known, superb conductor) and fullerenes (C₆₀ football-shaped molecules) — extend the lesson: carbon's bonding flexibility produces an entire materials library from one element.

### Molecular shapes: VSEPR — the electrons decide the geometry
*BB 240 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Molecules are not flat drawings on paper. They are 3D objects with definite shapes — linear, bent, tetrahedral, pyramidal. The shape is not arbitrary. It is determined by one rule: electron pairs repel each other, and they arrange themselves around the central atom to be as far apart as possible. This is VSEPR — Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion — and it predicts the geometry of almost any molecule.

**Concrete.** Carbon dioxide (CO₂): carbon has two double bonds to two oxygens — two regions of electron density around C. They repel to opposite sides: the molecule is **linear**, 180°. Water (H₂O): oxygen has two bonds to hydrogen AND two lone pairs — four regions of electron density. A tetrahedral arrangement is 109.5°, but the lone pairs squeeze the bonds closer together — the H-O-H angle is 104.5°, and the molecule is **bent**. Methane (CH₄): four bonds, no lone pairs — perfect tetrahedron, 109.5°. Ammonia (NH₃): three bonds, one lone pair — the lone pair pushes the three N-H bonds down, giving a **trigonal pyramidal** shape with H-N-H angle ≈ 107°. In every case: count the electron regions (bonds + lone pairs), and the shape follows.

**Definition.** **VSEPR theory** states that electron pairs (both bonding pairs and lone pairs) around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves to minimise repulsion, determining the molecular geometry. Key geometries: 2 electron pairs → **linear** (180°). 3 pairs → **trigonal planar** (120°). 4 pairs → **tetrahedral** (109.5°). With lone pairs: 4 pairs with 1 lone pair → **trigonal pyramidal** (~107°). 4 pairs with 2 lone pairs → **bent** (~104.5°). 5 pairs → **trigonal bipyramidal**. 6 pairs → **octahedral**. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs (they occupy more space), compressing bond angles slightly.

**In action.** Shape determines function — in chemistry as in biology. The bent shape of water makes it polar, which makes it an extraordinary solvent. The tetrahedral shape of methane makes it non-polar, which is why oil and water don't mix. The planar shape of a drug molecule determines whether it fits into a receptor like a key into a lock. The double helix of DNA is a specific shape held by specific bond angles. VSEPR gives you the shape from the formula — a sketch on paper that becomes a 3D object, and that 3D object's shape determines what it can and cannot do.

### Polarity: when sharing isn't equal
*BB 241 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** In a covalent bond, electrons are shared — but not always equally. Oxygen pulls electrons harder than hydrogen. In an O-H bond, the electrons spend more time near the oxygen, giving it a partial negative charge (δ−) and leaving hydrogen with a partial positive charge (δ+). This is a polar bond — a covalent bond with unequal sharing. If the molecule's shape allows these bond polarities to add up rather than cancel out, the whole molecule is polar.

**Concrete.** Electronegativity increases across the periodic table (left → right) and decreases down a group. Fluorine (4.0) is the most electronegative element; caesium (0.7) the least. In HF, the difference is 4.0−2.1 = 1.9 — a very polar bond. In CH, the difference is 2.5−2.1 = 0.4 — nearly non-polar. Water (H₂O): O-H bonds are polar, and the bent shape prevents the bond dipoles from cancelling — the molecule has a net dipole moment. Carbon dioxide (CO₂): C=O bonds are polar, but the linear shape makes the two bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel exactly — CO₂ is non-polar despite having polar bonds. Shape decides whether bond polarities add up or cancel out.

**Definition.** **Electronegativity** is the ability of an atom to attract the shared electrons in a covalent bond (Pauling scale, 0.7–4.0). A **polar bond** forms when the electronegativity difference ΔEN > 0.4 (approximately). The atom with higher EN gets δ−; the other δ+. When ΔEN > ~1.7, the bond is considered **ionic** rather than polar covalent. A **polar molecule** has a net dipole moment — an asymmetrical distribution of charge. A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if the symmetry cancels the bond dipoles (e.g. CO₂, CCl₄ — tetrahedral symmetry cancels four polar C-Cl bonds).

**In action.** Polarity governs solubility: "like dissolves like." Polar water dissolves polar and ionic substances (salt, sugar, alcohol). Non-polar solvents (hexane, oil) dissolve non-polar substances (grease, wax). It governs boiling points: polar molecules attract each other more strongly, requiring more energy to separate. It governs how drugs cross cell membranes (non-polar molecules pass through; polar ones need transport proteins). It governs chromatography: polar substances stick to polar stationary phases and move slowly. The polarity of a molecule is a single number (dipole moment, measured in Debyes) that encodes how asymmetrically its electrons are distributed — and that asymmetry dictates its interactions with everything around it.

### Intermolecular forces: the weak attractions that shape the world
*BB 242 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Covalent and ionic bonds hold atoms together within a molecule. Intermolecular forces hold molecules together. They are far weaker — tens of kJ/mol vs hundreds — but they are the reason water is liquid at room temperature, why geckos can walk on ceilings, and why DNA strands stay paired. There are three kinds, and every molecule experiences at least one.

**Concrete.** Compare methane (CH₄, M_r = 16) and water (H₂O, M_r = 18) — similar masses. Methane boils at −161°C; water at +100°C. The 261°C difference is entirely due to intermolecular forces. Methane is non-polar — the only forces between its molecules are weak **London dispersion forces** (instantaneous dipoles from randomly shifting electrons). Water has **hydrogen bonding** — the strongest IMF — plus dipole-dipole forces and dispersion forces. The IMFs in water are orders of magnitude stronger than in methane. Compare the halogens: F₂ (gas, −188°C), Cl₂ (gas, −34°C), Br₂ (liquid, +59°C), I₂ (solid, +184°C). All non-polar, so only dispersion forces — but dispersion forces increase with the number of electrons. Iodine has 106 electrons; fluorine has 18. More electrons = larger instantaneous dipoles = stronger dispersion forces = higher boiling point.

**Definition.** The three types of **intermolecular force** (IMF), in order of increasing strength: (1) **London dispersion forces** (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole) — present between ALL molecules; increase with molecular size (more electrons). (2) **Dipole–dipole forces** — between polar molecules; permanent δ+ and δ− attract. (3) **Hydrogen bonding** — a special, strong dipole–dipole force when H is bonded to N, O, or F (the three most electronegative elements). IMF strength: ~1–40 kJ/mol. Compare: covalent bond ~150–800 kJ/mol. IMFs are weak per interaction but additive — in giant molecules (polymers, DNA), their sum is enormous.

**In action.** IMFs explain the physical properties of everything. Why does butter melt at ~35°C but diamond at ~3500°C? Butter molecules are held by weak van der Waals forces; diamond atoms are held by strong covalent bonds throughout. Why does a longer hydrocarbon chain have a higher boiling point? More electrons, stronger dispersion forces. Why does water bead on a waxed car? Water's strong hydrogen bonds keep water molecules together (cohesion); wax is non-polar and offers only weak dispersion forces (poor adhesion) — the water minimises contact and beads up. The living world runs on IMFs: protein folding, DNA base pairing, cell membrane formation — all governed by the interplay of these weak, additive, and exquisitely selective forces.

### Hydrogen bonding: the special force that defines life
*BB 243 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** When hydrogen is covalently bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine — the three most electronegative elements — the bond is so polar that the hydrogen carries a strong δ+ charge. This δ+ hydrogen is then strongly attracted to a lone pair on an N, O, or F atom on a neighbouring molecule. This is a hydrogen bond — about 10 times stronger than ordinary dipole–dipole forces, but still about 10 times weaker than a covalent bond. It's the Goldilocks force: strong enough to matter, weak enough to break and re-form at biological temperatures.

**Concrete.** Hydrogen bonds between water molecules: each water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds — two via its hydrogens (donating), two via its lone pairs (accepting). This tetrahedral network is why water has such a high specific heat capacity (absorbing heat breaks H-bonds rather than speeding up molecules), why ice floats (the H-bond network holds molecules further apart than in liquid water — ice is less dense), and why water has a high surface tension (H-bonds at the surface pull inward). In DNA, hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (A=T with two H-bonds, G≡C with three) hold the double helix together — strong enough for stability, weak enough to unzip for replication. In proteins, H-bonds between C=O and N-H groups along the backbone create the α-helix and β-sheet secondary structures.

**Definition.** A **hydrogen bond** is a strong intermolecular attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F — the donor) and a lone pair of electrons on another electronegative atom (the acceptor). H-bond energy: ~10–40 kJ/mol (compare: O-H covalent bond ~460 kJ/mol). Requirements: H must be bonded to N, O, or F; the acceptor must have a lone pair. H-bonds are directional (strongest when donor-H···acceptor are collinear) and specific. They can be intermolecular (between molecules — water, DNA base pairs) or intramolecular (within the same molecule — protein folding).

**In action.** Without hydrogen bonding, water would boil at about −80°C — Earth's oceans would be gas. DNA would not form a stable double helix. Proteins would not fold into their functional shapes. Cellulose (plant fibre) and nylon derive their strength from hydrogen bonds between polymer chains. The reason your cotton T-shirt stays together is hydrogen bonding between cellulose chains. The reason a spider's silk is stronger than steel by weight involves hydrogen-bonded β-sheet nanocrystals. Hydrogen bonding is the most important non-covalent interaction in chemistry and biology — it's the scaffold on which the molecules of life are assembled.

### Why water is weird: hydrogen bonds explain everything
*BB 244 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** Water is the most familiar substance on Earth, and also the strangest. It expands when it freezes (almost everything else contracts). It has an enormous heat capacity. Its solid form floats on its liquid form. It dissolves more substances than any other liquid. Every single one of these anomalies traces back to the same cause: the hydrogen-bonded network that gives water its structure and its stubbornness.

**Concrete.** The density anomaly: most liquids get denser as they cool — the molecules slow down and pack tighter. Water does too — until 4°C. Below 4°C, the hydrogen bonds begin forcing molecules into an open, hexagonal arrangement (the ice lattice). At 0°C, water freezes into this open structure — ice is about 9% less dense than liquid water, so it floats. If ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, killing all life. The high specific heat capacity (4.18 J/g·°C): to raise water's temperature, you must first break hydrogen bonds — the energy goes into restructuring the network, not just speeding up molecules. This makes water an excellent coolant (sweat, car radiators) and a climate moderator (oceans buffer temperature swings).

**Definition.** Water's anomalous properties arise from its extensive **hydrogen-bonded network**: (1) **Maximum density at 4°C** — below this, H-bond-directed open structure begins to dominate. (2) **Solid less dense than liquid** — ice floats. (3) **High specific heat capacity** — 4.18 J/g·°C, among the highest of any liquid. (4) **High latent heat of vaporisation** — 2260 J/g, making evaporative cooling highly effective. (5) **High surface tension** — H-bonds create a strong "skin." (6) **Universal solvent** — polar nature and H-bonding ability dissolve ionic and polar substances. All trace to the same molecular feature: two H-bond donors + two H-bond acceptors per molecule.

**In action.** Water's weirdness is why life uses it. The floating ice insulates aquatic ecosystems through winter. The high heat capacity stabilises our body temperature and the Earth's climate. The high latent heat makes sweating an effective cooling mechanism — evaporating 1 g of water removes 2260 J of heat. The solvent power allows blood (mostly water) to carry nutrients, ions, and waste. The surface tension allows water to rise through plant xylem by capillary action. In the search for extraterrestrial life, "follow the water" is not a slogan — it's a recognition that water's unique hydrogen-bonding chemistry makes it almost irreplaceable as a medium for biochemistry.

### Properties from structure: predicting how a substance will behave
*BB 245 · chemistry · bonding*

**Idea.** You're handed an unknown white solid. You've never seen it before. But you can predict its properties — melting point, conductivity, solubility, hardness — by answering one question: what kind of bonding and structure does it have? Ionic lattice, giant covalent, metallic, or simple molecular? The structure type plus a few rules gives you a complete property profile. No experiment required.

**Concrete.** Four substances. Substance A: high melting point, conducts when molten but not solid, brittle, dissolves in water. → Ionic lattice (like NaCl — giant ionic, ions fixed when solid, free when molten/dissolved). Substance B: very high melting point, doesn't conduct, extremely hard, insoluble. → Giant covalent (like diamond — 3D network of strong covalent bonds, no free charges). Substance C: conducts as solid, malleable, shiny, high melting point. → Metallic (like copper — delocalised electrons, layers of cations can slide). Substance D: low melting point, doesn't conduct, soft, may dissolve in non-polar solvents. → Simple molecular (like wax — strong covalent bonds inside molecules, weak IMFs between molecules). The structure type is the master switch — flip it and every property changes together.

**Definition.** **Structure–property relationship decision framework:** (1) Identify the bonding type: metallic → metallic bonding. Non-metal + non-metal → covalent. Metal + non-metal → ionic. (2) Identify the structure: giant ionic, giant covalent, giant metallic, or simple molecular. (3) Predict properties: **Melting point** — giant >> simple (you're breaking strong bonds vs weak IMFs). **Electrical conductivity** — free charges (delocalised electrons or mobile ions) required. **Hardness** — giant structures are hard; simple molecular are soft. **Solubility** — ionic → polar solvents; covalent → non-polar solvents; metals and giant covalent → insoluble. (4) Exceptions exist (graphite conducts as a covalent structure), but the framework catches 95% of substances.

**In action.** This is the practical payoff of bonding theory. A materials engineer choosing a furnace lining needs a substance with high melting point and low conductivity — giant ionic (MgO). An electrical engineer needs a conductor that can be drawn into wire — metallic (Cu). A packaging designer wants something lightweight, flexible, and waterproof — simple molecular polymer. A jeweller wants hard, shiny, and corrosion-resistant — metallic (Au, Pt). Bonding theory is not abstract classification — it's the predictive toolkit that tells you what a material can and cannot do before you ever touch it.

### What is a chemical reaction?
*BB 246 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** A chemical reaction is not magic — it's rearrangement. Atoms don't appear or disappear. Bonds break and new bonds form, and the atoms that were in the reactants are all still there in the products, just connected differently. A reaction is a reshuffling of the same deck of atoms into a new hand.

**Concrete.** Methane burns in oxygen: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Count atoms. Before: 1 C, 4 H, 4 O. After: 1 C (in CO₂), 4 H (in 2 H₂O), 4 O (2 in CO₂, 2 in 2 H₂O). Every atom is accounted for — nothing is lost, nothing is created. What happened: the C-H bonds in methane and the O=O bonds in oxygen broke; C=O bonds (in CO₂) and O-H bonds (in H₂O) formed. Energy was released because the bonds formed are stronger than the bonds broken (exothermic). The atoms didn't change identity — carbon stayed carbon, hydrogen stayed hydrogen. Only their partnerships changed. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms; nuclear reactions (next topic, not this one) change atoms into different elements.

**Definition.** A **chemical reaction** is a process in which one or more substances (reactants) are transformed into one or more different substances (products) through the breaking and forming of chemical bonds. Atoms are **conserved** — the number and type of atoms is the same before and after. Mass is conserved. A reaction is represented by a **chemical equation**: reactants → products. Evidence of a reaction: colour change, gas evolved, precipitate formed, temperature change, light emitted. Reactions can be classified by type (synthesis, decomposition, combustion, displacement, acid–base, redox) — covered in subsequent BBs.

**In action.** The distinction between physical change and chemical reaction matters. Ice melting is physical — H₂O molecules are still H₂O, just reorganised. Electrolysis of water (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂) is chemical — the H₂O molecules are torn apart and reassembled into entirely different substances. Cooking an egg: chemical — the proteins denature and re-form new bonds; the change is irreversible. Dissolving salt in water: physical — the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are separated but unchanged; evaporate the water and you get the same salt back. The line: if new substances with new properties form, it's a chemical reaction. If the same substance just changes state or mixes, it's physical.

### Balancing equations: atoms in must equal atoms out
*BB 247 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** A chemical equation is a sentence in the language of chemistry. Like any sentence, it must be grammatically correct. The grammar is conservation of mass: the number of each type of atom on the left must equal the number on the right. If the atoms don't balance, the equation is wrong — no matter how plausible the products look. Balancing is the first skill of reaction chemistry. Without it, nothing else works.

**Concrete.** Balance the combustion of propane: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Step 1 — carbon: 3 C on left → need 3 CO₂. C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + H₂O. Step 2 — hydrogen: 8 H → need 4 H₂O. C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Step 3 — oxygen: right side has 3×2 + 4×1 = 10 O atoms → need 5 O₂ on left. C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Check: C: 3=3 ✓, H: 8=8 ✓, O: 10=10 ✓. Balanced. The coefficients (1, 5, 3, 4) are the stoichiometric coefficients — they tell you the mole ratios: 1 mole of propane reacts with 5 moles of oxygen to produce 3 moles of CO₂ and 4 moles of H₂O.

**Definition.** A **balanced chemical equation** has equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides, satisfying the **law of conservation of mass**. Procedure: (1) Write the skeleton equation with correct formulas. (2) Balance elements one at a time using coefficients (whole numbers placed BEFORE formulas). (3) Start with elements that appear in only one reactant and one product. (4) Save elements that appear in multiple places (often O and H) for last. (5) Check all elements. (6) Reduce coefficients to the simplest whole-number ratio. Never change subscripts — that changes the compound's identity. Only adjust coefficients.

**In action.** Balancing is not busywork — it's stoichiometry. A balanced equation gives the mole ratios, and from mole ratios come all quantitative predictions: how much product from how much reactant, which reactant runs out first (limiting reagent), what the yield should be, and what's left over. An unbalanced equation is worse than useless — it leads to wrong masses, wrong volumes, and in industrial chemistry, wrong orders of magnitude in cost and waste. Every stoichiometric calculation starts with a balanced equation. If the equation isn't balanced, stop. Nothing after it can be trusted.

### Acids and bases: proton donors and acceptors
*BB 248 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor. A base is a proton acceptor. This is the Brønsted-Lowry definition, and it covers almost every acid–base reaction you'll encounter. HCl gas meets NH₃ gas and they react instantly to form NH₄Cl — a white smoke. HCl donated a proton to NH₃. That's the entire transaction. Acids give; bases receive. The proton is the currency.

**Concrete.** Hydrochloric acid (HCl) in water: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). HCl donated a proton to water — HCl is the acid, H₂O is the base. The products: H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion) and Cl⁻. Ammonia in water: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). Ammonia accepted a proton from water — NH₃ is the base, H₂O is the acid this time. Water is amphoteric — it can act as both acid and base depending on what it meets. Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) donate protons completely — the reaction goes to completion. Weak acids (CH₃COOH, carbonic acid) donate only partially — an equilibrium mixture results.

**Definition.** **Brønsted-Lowry theory:** An **acid** is a proton (H⁺) donor. A **base** is a proton acceptor. Acid–base reactions are **proton transfer** reactions. When an acid loses a proton, it forms its **conjugate base** (HCl → Cl⁻). When a base gains a proton, it forms its **conjugate acid** (NH₃ → NH₄⁺). Strong acids have weak conjugate bases; weak acids have stronger conjugate bases. **Common strong acids**: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (first proton), HClO₄. **Common strong bases**: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂. **Common weak acids**: CH₃COOH (acetic), H₂CO₃ (carbonic), HF. **Common weak bases**: NH₃, amines.

**In action.** The Brønsted-Lowry definition is broader than the older Arrhenius definition (which restricted acids to substances producing H⁺ in water and bases to substances producing OH⁻). Brønsted-Lowry works in any solvent, or even without solvent — HCl gas + NH₃ gas react directly in the air. It explains why salts can be acidic or basic: NH₄Cl dissolves to give NH₄⁺ (a weak acid — donates protons to water, making the solution acidic) and Cl⁻ (an extremely weak conjugate base — does nothing). It underpins buffer chemistry: a weak acid and its conjugate base resist pH changes by absorbing added H⁺ or OH⁻. Every biological fluid is buffered — blood pH is maintained at 7.40±0.05 by the H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ buffer system. The Brønsted-Lowry framework turns acid–base chemistry from a list of substances into a unified proton economy.

### The pH scale and neutralisation
*BB 249 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** The acidity of a solution is measured by its pH — a number from 0 (strongly acidic) to 14 (strongly alkaline), with 7 as neutral. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Each unit of pH represents a factor of 10 in hydrogen ion concentration. pH 3 is ten times more acidic than pH 4, a hundred times more than pH 5. The scale is logarithmic — because the range of [H⁺] in everyday solutions spans 14 orders of magnitude.

**Concrete.** Stomach acid: pH ~1.5 ([H⁺] ≈ 0.03 mol/dm³). Lemon juice: pH ~2.5. Vinegar: pH ~3. Black coffee: pH ~5. Pure water: pH = 7 ([H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷). Blood: pH 7.4. Baking soda solution: pH ~8.5. Household ammonia: pH ~11.5. Drain cleaner: pH ~14 ([OH⁻] ≈ 1 mol/dm³). Neutralisation: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l). The H⁺ from the acid and OH⁻ from the base combine to form water. If exactly equal moles of acid and base are mixed, the resulting solution has pH 7 — neutral. The salt (NaCl) stays dissolved. The net ionic equation for ANY strong acid–strong base neutralisation is always the same: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

**Definition.** **pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**, where [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/dm³. For pure water at 25°C, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷, so pH = 7 — **neutral**. pH 7: alkaline (basic). **pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]** and pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C). **Neutralisation** is the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water. The **equivalence point** is when exactly enough base has been added to neutralise all the acid (or vice versa). **Indicators** (litmus, phenolphthalein, universal indicator) change colour at specific pH ranges.

**In action.** The logarithmic nature of pH is easy to misunderstand. A solution at pH 6 is not "slightly acidic compared to pH 7" — it has ten times the H⁺ concentration. A small pH shift in blood (from 7.4 to 7.2) is a medical emergency — it means [H⁺] has increased by ~60%, disrupting every pH-dependent enzyme in the body. In swimming pools, agriculture (soil pH), and food preservation, pH monitoring is constant. Neutralisation is the basis of antacids (CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ — the fizz), of treating acidic industrial waste, and of titrations — the lab technique that determines the concentration of an unknown acid or base by neutralising it with a known base or acid. The pH scale reduces an enormous concentration range to a simple 0–14 number — the logarithm at its most practical.

## 4. Redox, electrochemistry & kinetics

### Types of reaction: the five patterns
*BB 281 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** Most chemical reactions fall into a handful of patterns. Recognise the pattern, and you can predict the products without memorising each reaction individually. Synthesis: A + B → AB. Decomposition: AB → A + B. Combustion: fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Displacement: A + BC → AC + B. Precipitation: two solutions mix → solid drops out. Five patterns cover the majority of reactions you'll encounter.

**Concrete.** **Synthesis:** 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s) — a brilliant white flame as magnesium ribbon burns. Two elements combine into one compound. **Decomposition:** CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) — limestone heated, carbon dioxide driven off, quicklime left behind. **Combustion:** C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g) — the gas in your stove, burning with a blue flame. Always O₂ in, CO₂ + H₂O out for hydrocarbons. **Displacement:** Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s) — grey zinc metal in blue copper sulphate solution; copper plates out as reddish-brown solid. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one. **Precipitation:** AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq) — two clear solutions mix and a white cloud of solid silver chloride appears. The solid that forms is the precipitate.

**Definition.** The five fundamental reaction types: (1) **Synthesis** (combination) — two or more substances form one product: A + B → AB. (2) **Decomposition** — one substance breaks into two or more: AB → A + B (often requires heat, light, or electricity). (3) **Combustion** — reaction with oxygen, producing oxides; hydrocarbon combustion always yields CO₂ + H₂O. (4) **Single displacement** — one element replaces another in a compound: A + BC → AC + B (driven by the reactivity series). (5) **Double displacement** (precipitation or neutralisation) — ions swap partners: AB + CD → AD + CB. Two additional overarching types — acid–base (proton transfer) and redox (electron transfer) — can overlap with these patterns.

**In action.** Pattern recognition in reaction types saves enormous mental effort. Given reactants, identify the pattern → predict products → balance the equation. Rusting: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ — synthesis (and redox). Baking soda + vinegar: NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂ — double displacement (and acid–base). Bleaching a stain: NaClO → NaCl + [O] — decomposition. The reactivity series governs displacement: a metal higher in the series (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, Cu, Ag, Au) displaces any metal below it from its compounds. This is why zinc displaces copper but copper won't displace zinc — the series encodes the relative tendency to lose electrons, which is also the redox activity order.

### Oxidation: losing electrons
*BB 282 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** Historically, oxidation meant "reacting with oxygen." That's how the name stuck. But the modern definition is broader and more powerful: oxidation is the loss of electrons. Iron rusts (Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻) — the iron loses electrons to oxygen. Magnesium burns (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻) — the metal loses electrons. Any process where an atom, ion, or molecule gives up electrons is oxidation, whether oxygen is involved or not.

**Concrete.** Place an iron nail in copper sulphate solution. The nail turns reddish-brown as copper metal plates onto it. What happened? Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ — the iron lost electrons (oxidised). Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) — the copper ions gained electrons (reduced). The iron atoms gave electrons to the copper ions — one substance was oxidised, the other reduced. Oxidation and reduction always happen together. In a blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. The iron in Fe₂O₃ goes from Fe³⁺ to Fe(0) — it gains electrons (reduced). The carbon in CO goes from C²⁺ to C⁴⁺ (in CO₂) — it loses electrons (oxidised). Carbon monoxide is the reducing agent; iron oxide is the oxidising agent.

**Definition.** **Oxidation** is the loss of electrons. The substance that loses electrons is **oxidised**. The substance that accepts those electrons is the **oxidising agent** (it causes oxidation and is itself reduced). In terms of oxidation number (see BB-NEW-239), oxidation is an increase in oxidation number. In organic chemistry, oxidation often involves gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen. The OIL RIG mnemonic: **O**xidation **I**s **L**oss of electrons; **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain of electrons.

**In action.** Oxidation is everywhere — literally. Rusting (iron → iron oxide) costs the global economy billions per year in structural damage. The brown colour of a cut apple is enzymatic oxidation. Bleach works by oxidising coloured compounds to colourless ones. A car battery delivers power because lead is oxidised at one electrode (Pb → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻) while lead dioxide is reduced at the other. Respiration — the process powering your cells — is the controlled oxidation of glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. The oxygen you breathe is the terminal electron acceptor in a chain of redox reactions that powers every cell in your body. Oxidation is not a niche lab phenomenon — it is the chemistry of energy, decay, and life itself.

---

### Reduction: gaining electrons
*BB 283 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** Reduction is the gain of electrons. The name is historical — metal ores are often oxides, and "reducing" the ore to metal involves removing oxygen. Aluminium, for example, was once more precious than gold because reducing Al₂O₃ to Al metal was nearly impossible. Today, the Hall-Héroult process uses enormous electric currents to force the reduction — and aluminium is cheap enough for drink cans. Reduction is the chemical act of turning a compound back into its elemental form.

**Concrete.** Aluminium extraction: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al(l) — each aluminium ion gains three electrons to become aluminium metal. The electrons are supplied by a massive direct current passed through molten aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite. In a hydrogen fuel cell: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O — oxygen is reduced to water, gaining electrons (and protons) at the cathode. In photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ — carbon in CO₂ (oxidation state +4) is reduced to carbon in glucose (average oxidation state 0). The carbon gains electrons. Photosynthesis is a massive reduction process powered by sunlight.

**Definition.** **Reduction** is the gain of electrons. The substance that gains electrons is **reduced**. The substance that supplies those electrons is the **reducing agent** (it causes reduction and is itself oxidised). In terms of oxidation number, reduction is a decrease in oxidation number. In organic chemistry, reduction often involves gaining hydrogen or losing oxygen. Metals are extracted from their ores by reduction — the metal ion gains electrons to become the neutral metal. Common reducing agents: carbon (in blast furnaces), hydrogen, reactive metals (Na, Mg, Al), and electrons themselves (in electrolysis).

**In action.** Reduction is the chemical basis of metallurgy — every metal above gold in the reactivity series exists in nature as a compound and must be reduced to its elemental form. The Iron Age began when humans learned to reduce iron oxide with charcoal (carbon). The Hall-Héroult process (1886) made aluminium an industrial material instead of a scientific curiosity. In biology, reduction is how energy is stored: NAD⁺ → NADH is a reduction, and NADH carries those high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain. Reduction and oxidation are not two topics — they are two halves of every redox reaction, and you cannot have one without the other.

### Redox: oxidation and reduction are one story
*BB 284 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** You can't have oxidation without reduction. Electrons don't vanish — when one substance loses them, another must gain them. A redox reaction is the full story: who gave, who received, and how many electrons changed hands. The substance that is oxidised is the reducing agent. The substance that is reduced is the oxidising agent. The terminology sounds backwards, but once you see the transaction, it's clear: the reducing agent reduces the other by giving electrons; the oxidising agent oxidises the other by taking electrons.

**Concrete.** Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). Zinc loses 2 electrons — oxidised. Copper ions gain 2 electrons — reduced. Zinc is the **reducing agent** (it gave electrons, causing reduction of Cu²⁺). Cu²⁺ is the **oxidising agent** (it accepted electrons, causing oxidation of Zn). The electron transfer is direct — zinc atoms touch copper ions and hand over electrons. Now the thermite reaction: 2Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → Al₂O₃(s) + 2Fe(l). Aluminium loses electrons (Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻) — oxidised. Iron gains electrons (Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Fe) — reduced. Al is the reducing agent; Fe₂O₃ is the oxidising agent. The reaction is so exothermic it produces molten iron — used to weld railway tracks.

**Definition.** A **redox** (reduction–oxidation) reaction involves the transfer of electrons from one species to another. The species that loses electrons is **oxidised** and acts as the **reducing agent**. The species that gains electrons is **reduced** and acts as the **oxidising agent**. The total number of electrons lost equals the total number gained. A redox reaction can be split into two **half-reactions**: oxidation half (electrons on the right) and reduction half (electrons on the left). A **disproportionation** reaction is a redox where the same element is both oxidised and reduced (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ — oxygen goes from −1 to −2 and 0).

**In action.** Redox is the unifying framework for an enormous range of chemistry. Rusting: Fe → Fe₂O₃ (iron oxidised by O₂). Bleaching: coloured compounds oxidised to colourless ones. Batteries: redox reactions separated into two half-cells, electron flow through an external circuit. Electroplating: metal ions reduced onto a surface. Respiration: glucose oxidised. Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide reduced. Metabolism: thousands of coupled redox reactions, each enzyme catalysing one electron transfer step in a chain. The ubiquity of redox is why the OIL RIG mnemonic is worth memorising — it opens the door to understanding energy in chemistry, biology, and industry.

### Oxidation numbers: keeping score in the electron game
*BB 285 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** In a redox reaction, electrons move. But in a covalent molecule like CO₂, no electron is fully transferred — they're shared. How do you tell who's oxidised and who's reduced when there's no obvious electron transfer? You assign oxidation numbers — a bookkeeping score that pretends all bonds are ionic. If the number goes up, it's oxidation. If it goes down, it's reduction. The rules are simple, and they turn every reaction into a scoreable game.

**Concrete.** In MnO₄⁻ (permanganate ion): oxygen is −2 (rule: oxygen is −2 except in peroxides). Four oxygens = −8 total. The ion's charge is −1. So Mn + (−8) = −1 → Mn = +7. In SO₄²⁻: four oxygens = −8. S + (−8) = −2 → S = +6. Now the reaction: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O. Mn goes from +7 to +2 — decrease of 5 → reduction. Fe goes from +2 to +3 — increase of 1 → oxidation. Five Fe²⁺ are oxidised (total electron loss = 5) to match one MnO₄⁻ being reduced (electron gain = 5). The oxidation numbers balance — as they must. This is how you identify redox reactions when no isolated charges are visible.

**Definition.** **Oxidation number** (oxidation state) is the hypothetical charge an atom would have if all bonds were fully ionic. Rules (in order of priority): (1) Free element = 0 (Na, O₂, Fe). (2) Simple ion = charge (Na⁺ = +1, Cl⁻ = −1). (3) Fluorine in compounds = −1. (4) Hydrogen in compounds = +1 (except metal hydrides = −1). (5) Oxygen in compounds = −2 (except peroxides = −1, OF₂ = +2). (6) Group 1 metals = +1, Group 2 = +2. (7) Sum of oxidation numbers = overall charge of species. In a redox reaction, the element whose oxidation number increases is **oxidised**; the element whose oxidation number decreases is **reduced**.

**In action.** Oxidation numbers reveal hidden redox reactions. Is this a redox? CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Oxidation numbers: Ca = +2 throughout, C = +4 throughout, O = −2 throughout — no changes. This is NOT a redox; it's a decomposition. Is this a redox? 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. H goes from 0 to +1 (oxidised); O goes from 0 to −2 (reduced). Yes — redox. Oxidation numbers are the universal redox test. They also allow you to balance complex redox equations (like permanganate oxidising iron) by balancing the electron gain and loss before adding H⁺/OH⁻ and H₂O. Titration calculations in analytical chemistry depend entirely on oxidation number changes to determine the equivalence point.

### Half-equations and electrochemistry: redox with a circuit
*BB 286 · chemistry · reactions*

**Idea.** A redox reaction can be physically split in two. Put the oxidation in one beaker, the reduction in another, connect them with a wire and a salt bridge, and electrons flow through the wire — an electric current. This is an electrochemical cell. Reverse it — force current through a cell — and you drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction: electrolysis. Half-equations are the language for both. Each half tells one side of the electron story.

**Concrete.** A zinc–copper cell (the Daniell cell): Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ — oxidation at the anode (negative terminal). Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) — reduction at the cathode (positive terminal). Electrons flow through the external wire from Zn to Cu. The salt bridge (filter paper soaked in KNO₃) allows ions to migrate, completing the circuit. Cell voltage ≈ 1.1 V. For electrolysis of molten NaCl: at the anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻ (oxidation, green chlorine gas bubbles off). At the cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na(l) (reduction, molten sodium metal collects). This is how sodium metal and chlorine gas are manufactured — the same reaction that gives table salt, but in reverse, driven by electricity. Electrolysis of brine (NaCl solution) gives different products: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ at anode; 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ at cathode (water is reduced instead of Na⁺ because H₂O is more easily reduced).

**Definition.** A **half-equation** shows either the oxidation or reduction part of a redox reaction, with electrons explicitly shown. Oxidation half: electrons on the right (lost). Reduction half: electrons on the left (gained). Multiplying half-equations so electron counts match and adding them gives the full redox equation. An **electrochemical cell** (galvanic/voltaic cell) converts chemical energy to electrical energy by separating the half-reactions. **Electrolysis** uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. **Anode** = where oxidation occurs. **Cathode** = where reduction occurs (mnemonic: AN OX, RED CAT).

**In action.** Electrochemistry is the bridge between chemistry and electricity. Every battery — from a 1.5 V AA to a lithium-ion pack powering a phone — is an electrochemical cell. The voltage depends on which two half-reactions you pair. Electrolysis produces aluminium (Hall-Héroult), chlorine and sodium hydroxide (chlor-alkali process), and pure copper (electrorefining). Electroplating deposits a thin layer of metal (silver on cutlery, chromium on car parts). In a hydrogen fuel cell, H₂ is oxidised at the anode and O₂ is reduced at the cathode — the only product is water. Electrochemistry is not a niche — it's the industrial backbone of the metal, chemical, and energy-storage industries.

### Exothermic and endothermic: energy out or energy in
*BB 287 · chemistry · energetics*

**Idea.** Some reactions release energy — they get hot. These are exothermic. Others absorb energy — they get cold. These are endothermic. The difference is whether the bonds being formed are stronger (exothermic — the extra energy is released as heat) or weaker (endothermic — energy must be absorbed from the surroundings to break stronger bonds and form weaker ones). Every reaction has an energy signature. The sign tells you whether the reaction is a heat source or a heat sink.

**Concrete.** Burning methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. The bonds formed (2 C=O at ~800 kJ/mol each, 4 O-H at ~460 each) are collectively stronger than the bonds broken (4 C-H at ~410 each, 2 O=O at ~498 each). The excess energy — about 890 kJ per mole of methane — is released as heat. The flame is hot. This is exothermic. Now dissolve ammonium nitrate in water: NH₄NO₃(s) → NH₄⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq). Breaking the ionic lattice absorbs energy; hydrating the ions releases energy — but the net is absorption. The beaker gets cold. This is endothermic — the reaction pulls heat from the water. Cold packs use this: ammonium nitrate and water in separate compartments; break the seal, they mix, and the pack turns icy cold.

**Definition.** An **exothermic** reaction releases energy to the surroundings (ΔH 0). The products have more enthalpy than the reactants. The surroundings get colder. Examples: photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate, thermal decomposition of limestone. In an **energy profile diagram**, exothermic reactions show products lower than reactants (energy released); endothermic show products higher (energy absorbed). The difference is the **enthalpy change** ΔH.

**In action.** The exo/endo distinction determines how reactions are used. Exothermic reactions are energy sources — fuels, explosives, heat packs. Endothermic reactions are energy sinks — cold packs, cooling systems, and the most important endothermic reaction on Earth: photosynthesis, which absorbs 2800 kJ of sunlight per mole of glucose produced. A reaction that is spontaneous at one temperature can become non-spontaneous at another — the sign of ΔH alone doesn't tell you spontaneity (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, the Gibbs free energy, decides that). But the exo/endo classification is the first cut — it tells you which direction the heat flows, and whether you need to supply energy or can extract it.

---

### Enthalpy: measuring the heat of reaction
*BB 288 · chemistry · energetics*

**Idea.** Enthalpy (H) is the total heat content of a chemical system. You can't measure H directly — but you CAN measure the change in H, ΔH, when a reaction occurs. ΔH is the heat absorbed or released at constant pressure — the number you get from a calorimetry experiment. A negative ΔH means the reaction released heat (exothermic). A positive ΔH means it absorbed heat (endothermic). The magnitude tells you how much.

**Concrete.** Measure the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol. Burn 0.46 g of ethanol (0.01 mol) in a spirit burner under a copper calorimeter containing 200 g of water. The water temperature rises from 20.0°C to 34.3°C. Energy absorbed by water: q = mcΔT = 200 × 4.18 × 14.3 = 11,960 J ≈ 12.0 kJ. This came from 0.01 mol ethanol. So ΔH_combustion ≈ −1200 kJ/mol (actual value: −1367 kJ/mol — the difference is heat loss to the surroundings). The negative sign means exothermic. In a neutralisation: 50 cm³ of 1 M HCl + 50 cm³ of 1 M NaOH. Temperature rises by 6.8°C. Total solution mass ≈ 100 g. q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.8 = 2842 J. Moles of water formed = 0.05. ΔH_neut ≈ −2842/0.05 = −56,800 J/mol ≈ −57 kJ/mol. Strong acid–strong base neutralisation always gives about −57 kJ per mole of water formed — because the net reaction is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

**Definition.** **Enthalpy change** ΔH is the heat absorbed or released by a reaction at constant pressure, measured in kJ/mol. Standard enthalpy changes (ΔH°) are measured under **standard conditions**: 298 K (25°C), 100 kPa (1 atm), all solutions at 1 mol/dm³. Types: ΔH_combustion (burning 1 mole in excess O₂), ΔH_neutralisation (acid + base → 1 mol H₂O), ΔH_formation (forming 1 mole of compound from its elements in standard states), ΔH_reaction (any reaction as written). **Calorimetry** measures ΔH using q = mcΔT, where q = heat, m = mass, c = specific heat capacity, ΔT = temperature change. Divide q by moles reacted to get ΔH in kJ/mol.

**In action.** Enthalpy data is the bedrock of chemical engineering. The ΔH_combustion of a fuel determines its value as an energy source. Methane: −890 kJ/mol. Hydrogen: −286 kJ/mol. Octane (petrol): −5470 kJ/mol. Per gram, hydrogen delivers about 2.5× the energy of petrol — which is why it's attractive as a fuel, and why rockets use liquid hydrogen. ΔH_formation data (tabulated for thousands of compounds) lets you calculate ΔH for ANY reaction using Hess's Law — you don't need to measure every reaction directly. The enthalpy of a reaction is a single number that encodes the net energy consequence of breaking all the reactant bonds and forming all the product bonds.

---

### Hess's Law: the energy route doesn't matter
*BB 289 · chemistry · energetics*

**Idea.** You want to know the enthalpy change for a reaction you can't measure directly — maybe it's too slow, or side reactions interfere. Hess's Law says: it doesn't matter. The overall enthalpy change depends only on the initial and final states, not the route. If you can find a path from reactants to products using reactions you DO know, just add up their ΔH values. The answer is the same as if you'd measured the direct reaction.

**Concrete.** Find ΔH for: C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g) — the incomplete combustion of carbon. You can't easily stop carbon burning at CO; it tends to go all the way to CO₂. But you can measure two reactions: (1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₁ = −394 kJ/mol. (2) CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₂ = −283 kJ/mol. Hess's Law: the path C → CO₂ (direct, route 1) is equivalent to C → CO → CO₂ (route A then route 2 reversed). So: ΔH₁ = ΔH_A + ΔH₂ → −394 = ΔH_A + (−283) → ΔH_A = −111 kJ/mol. The enthalpy of formation of CO is −111 kJ/mol — found without ever isolating CO in a calorimeter. The cycle: C + ½O₂ → CO (wanted, ΔH_A). C + O₂ → CO₂ (measured, −394). CO + ½O₂ → CO₂ (measured, −283). Arrow manipulation: ΔH_A = −394 − (−283) = −111.

**Definition.** **Hess's Law** states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken — it depends only on the initial and final states. This is a direct consequence of enthalpy being a **state function**. Practical application: construct an **enthalpy cycle** (often a triangle or Born–Haber cycle), write known ΔH values on the arrows, and solve for the unknown. Alternative routes must sum to the same total. This works because energy is conserved — the net energy change around any closed cycle must be zero. Hess's Law allows calculation of ΔH for reactions that are impossible or impractical to measure directly.

**In action.** Hess's Law is the accounting system of thermochemistry. The standard enthalpy of formation ΔH_f° of every compound is tabulated. For any reaction: ΔH° = Σ(ΔH_f° of products) − Σ(ΔH_f° of reactants) — multiply each by its stoichiometric coefficient. This means you can calculate ΔH for millions of hypothetical reactions from a database of a few thousand formation enthalpies. Born–Haber cycles use Hess's Law to calculate lattice energies — the enthalpy change when gaseous ions form a solid lattice — which cannot be measured directly. Every ΔH value you see quoted for a reaction that wasn't directly measured was found using Hess's Law. The cycle is the tool; the conservation of energy is the guarantee.

### Bond enthalpies: breaking costs, making pays
*BB 290 · chemistry · energetics*

**Idea.** Every chemical bond has a price — the bond enthalpy, measured in kJ/mol. Breaking a bond always costs energy (endothermic). Making a bond always releases energy (exothermic). The net ΔH of a reaction is the sum: energy to break all reactant bonds minus energy released making all product bonds. If the products have stronger bonds overall, the reaction is exothermic. If they have weaker bonds, it's endothermic. Bond enthalpies reduce every reaction to an energy balance sheet.

**Concrete.** Estimate ΔH for: H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g). Bonds broken: 1 H-H (436 kJ) + 1 Cl-Cl (243 kJ) = 679 kJ absorbed. Bonds formed: 2 H-Cl (2 × 431 = 862 kJ) released. Net: ΔH ≈ +679 − 862 = −183 kJ/mol (per mole of reaction as written — producing 2 HCl). The actual value is −184 kJ/mol — bond enthalpy estimates are remarkably close. For methane combustion: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Bonds broken: 4 C-H (4×413=1652) + 2 O=O (2×498=996) = 2648 kJ. Bonds formed: 2 C=O (2×799=1598) + 4 O-H (4×463=1852) = 3450 kJ. Net: +2648 − 3450 = −802 kJ (actual: −890 kJ). The estimate is slightly off because bond enthalpies are averages — the C-H bond in methane isn't exactly the same as C-H in other molecules. But the estimate is within 10%.

**Definition.** **Bond enthalpy** (bond dissociation energy) is the energy required to break one mole of a specific bond in the gaseous state. **Mean bond enthalpy** is the average value for a given bond type across many compounds. Key values (kJ/mol): H-H 436, C-H 413, O-H 463, C-C 347, C=C 614, C≡C 839, O=O 498, C=O 799, Cl-Cl 243, H-Cl 431. ΔH_reaction ≈ Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed). Breaking bonds = endothermic (+). Making bonds = exothermic (−). This is an estimation method — it assumes all bonds of a given type are identical, which they aren't.

**In action.** Bond enthalpies explain why some substances are stable and others aren't. N₂ has an N≡N triple bond (944 kJ/mol) — one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. This is why nitrogen gas is almost inert, and why explosives containing nitrogen (TNT, nitroglycerine) release so much energy when the stable N₂ triple bond forms. The weakness of the O-O single bond (146 kJ/mol) in peroxides makes them reactive bleaching and disinfecting agents. The strength of the C-F bond (485 kJ/mol) makes PTFE (Teflon) incredibly resistant to chemical attack. Bond enthalpies turn the qualitative idea of "strong bonds" and "weak bonds" into numbers — and those numbers predict reactivity, stability, and energy output.

### Activation energy: the barrier every reaction must cross
*BB 291 · chemistry · energetics*

**Idea.** Petrol doesn't spontaneously ignite at room temperature — even though its combustion is highly exothermic. Why? Because before bonds can form, existing bonds must begin to break. This takes energy — the activation energy, E_a. It's the hump in the energy profile that reactants must climb before they can slide down to products. A reaction can be thermodynamically favoured (negative ΔH) but kinetically frozen (high E_a). The match provides the spark; the spark provides the E_a.

**Concrete.** A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature sits unchanged for years. Apply a spark — the spark provides the activation energy to break a few H-H and O=O bonds. Those atoms recombine, releasing far more energy than the spark supplied. That energy provides E_a for neighbouring molecules, which react and release more energy — a chain reaction. The mixture explodes. The overall ΔH is −286 kJ/mol (exothermic), but the E_a is around 200 kJ/mol. Without that initial 200 kJ push, nothing happens. Catalysts work by providing an alternative pathway with lower E_a. A catalytic converter uses platinum to lower the E_a for 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ and 2NO → N₂ + O₂, allowing these reactions to proceed at exhaust temperatures instead of requiring a flame.

**Definition.** **Activation energy** E_a is the minimum energy colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur. On an energy profile diagram, it's the difference between the reactants' energy and the **transition state** (activated complex) — the highest-energy arrangement along the reaction path. E_a is always positive (even for exothermic reactions). It is measured in kJ/mol. The **Arrhenius equation** relates E_a to the rate constant: k = A e^(−E_a/RT). High E_a → slow reaction at a given temperature. Low E_a → fast reaction. A **catalyst** lowers E_a without being consumed, providing an alternative mechanism.

**In action.** Activation energy is why the world isn't on fire. Thermodynamics says a mixture of oxygen and almost anything organic should react exothermically — but kinetics, governed by E_a, keeps those reactions frozen until triggered. It's why food doesn't spontaneously combust in your cupboard. It's why diamonds don't turn into graphite (the thermodynamically stable form) at room temperature — the E_a for rearranging all those strong C-C bonds is enormous. It's why enzymes are essential for life — biochemical reactions that would take millennia at body temperature happen in milliseconds because enzymes lower the E_a by factors of a million or more. E_a is nature's safety catch: it separates the thermodynamically possible from the kinetically actual.

### Reaction rate: how fast?
*BB 292 · chemistry · kinetics*

**Idea.** Some reactions are over in microseconds (an explosion). Others take millennia (diamond → graphite). The rate of a reaction measures how quickly reactants disappear or products appear. Rate = change in concentration ÷ time. It's the speedometer of chemistry. And unlike a car's speed, a reaction's rate is usually fastest at the start (when concentrations are highest) and slows as reactants are consumed.

**Concrete.** Marble chips (CaCO₃) react with hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Monitor the rate by measuring the volume of CO₂ gas evolved every 30 seconds. At t=0: 0 cm³. At t=30s: 24 cm³. At t=60s: 40 cm³. At t=90s: 50 cm³. At t=120s: 55 cm³. The rate is fastest at the start (0–30s: 24/30 = 0.8 cm³/s) and slows down (90–120s: 5/30 = 0.17 cm³/s) as the acid is consumed and the marble chips shrink. The average rate over the whole reaction is total volume/total time. The instantaneous rate at any moment is the gradient of the tangent to the volume–time curve at that point. At t=0, the initial rate is the steepest tangent.

**Definition.** The **rate of reaction** is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time: rate = −Δ[reactant]/Δt = +Δ[product]/Δt (units: mol/dm³/s). Rate can be followed by monitoring: gas volume, mass loss, colour change (using a colorimeter), pH change, or conductivity. The **rate equation** (rate law) expresses rate in terms of concentrations: rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where k is the **rate constant**, and m and n are the **orders** of reaction with respect to A and B (determined experimentally, not from the stoichiometric coefficients). The **overall order** = m + n.

**In action.** Reaction rate is not a single number — it's a function of concentration. The initial rate is often quoted for comparison. Rate data is analysed graphically: a straight line when concentration is plotted against time means zero order (rate is constant, independent of concentration). A constant half-life means first order (exponential decay of reactant). Plotting 1/[A] against time giving a straight line means second order. These graphical methods reveal the rate law, which in turn reveals the mechanism — the sequence of elementary steps by which the reaction actually occurs. The stoichiometric equation tells you what reacts and what's produced; the rate law tells you HOW it happens, which molecules collide in which step, and which step is the bottleneck.

### Factors affecting rate: temperature, concentration, surface area
*BB 293 · chemistry · kinetics*

**Idea.** For a reaction to happen, particles must collide with enough energy (≥E_a) and the right orientation. Anything that increases the frequency of successful collisions increases the rate. Three factors do this: higher temperature (faster particles, more with E≥E_a), higher concentration (more particles in the same volume = more collisions), and greater surface area (more exposed particles available to collide). These are the three levers of chemical kinetics.

**Concrete.** **Temperature:** The reaction between sodium thiosulphate and HCl produces a sulphur precipitate that turns the solution cloudy. At 20°C, the cross drawn on paper beneath the flask disappears in 60 seconds. At 40°C: 15 seconds. At 60°C: 4 seconds. A 10°C rise roughly doubles the rate for many reactions at room temperature. **Concentration:** Marble chips with 2 M HCl produce CO₂ twice as fast as with 1 M HCl — more acid particles per unit volume means more frequent collisions with the marble surface. **Surface area:** A large marble chip takes 5 minutes to fully react. The same mass of powdered marble reacts in 30 seconds — the powder has enormously more surface area exposed to the acid. A spark can ignite flour dust in air, causing a devastating explosion — the fine powder has huge surface area for oxidation.

**Definition.** **Collision theory** states that for a reaction to occur, particles must collide with energy ≥ E_a and with the correct orientation. Rate ∝ (collision frequency) × (fraction with E ≥ E_a). The three controllable factors: (1) **Temperature** — increases the average kinetic energy of particles, so more collisions exceed E_a; also increases collision frequency slightly. (2) **Concentration** (or pressure for gases) — more particles per unit volume, more collisions per second. (3) **Surface area** — for solids, only surface particles can react; powdering increases the surface-to-volume ratio enormously. A **catalyst** (see BB-NEW-249) provides an alternative pathway with lower E_a.

**In action.** Kinetic control is everywhere in industry and daily life. Refrigerators slow food spoilage (lower T means slower reaction rates for bacterial metabolism). Pressure cookers raise the boiling point of water, increasing the temperature and dramatically speeding cooking. Catalytic converters use enormous surface area (honeycomb structure coated with platinum/rhodium nanoparticles) to maximise contact between exhaust gases and catalyst. In a flour mill or coal mine, dust explosions are a constant hazard — controlled by ventilation (reducing concentration) and avoiding sparks (ignition source). The same principles govern drug dosing: concentration in the bloodstream must stay in the therapeutic window — too low (no effect), too high (toxic). Kinetics determines the dosing interval.

### The Boltzmann distribution: who has enough energy?
*BB 294 · chemistry · kinetics*

**Idea.** In a gas or liquid, not all particles have the same energy. Some are slow, some are fast, most are somewhere in the middle. The distribution of energies is given by the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve — a skewed bell shape that tails off to the right. Only the particles in the high-energy tail (those with E ≥ E_a) can react. The area under the tail is tiny at low temperature. Raise the temperature, and the whole curve flattens and shifts right — the tail area grows enormously. This is why a small temperature rise can double the rate.

**Concrete.** At 298 K (25°C), the fraction of molecules with E ≥ 50 kJ/mol in a typical gas is very small — perhaps 1 in 10⁹. Raise the temperature to 308 K (35°C) — only a 10°C increase. The average kinetic energy increases by just 3.3% (proportional to T). But the fraction with E ≥ 50 kJ/mol roughly **doubles**. The rate doubles because the number of successful collisions doubles — even though the total number of collisions barely changes. The high-energy tail is exquisitely sensitive to temperature. At 600 K, a much larger fraction crosses the barrier. At 1000 K, most molecules do. The Boltzmann distribution explains why a match lights a fire: the match raises a tiny region to ~1000 K, enough molecules in that region cross E_a, the reaction releases heat, neighbouring molecules are heated, and the reaction propagates.

**Definition.** The **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** describes the distribution of kinetic energies among molecules in a gas at a given temperature. Key features: (1) No molecules have zero energy. (2) The curve passes through a maximum (most probable energy). (3) Mean energy > most probable energy. (4) The curve is asymmetric with a long tail to the right. (5) The area under the curve = total number of molecules. (6) The area under the tail beyond E_a = number of molecules with enough energy to react. (7) Increasing temperature flattens the curve, shifts the peak right, and dramatically increases the tail area beyond E_a. The distribution applies rigorously to ideal gases but qualitatively to liquids and solutions as well.

**In action.** The Boltzmann distribution is the statistical foundation of chemical kinetics. It explains why reaction rates increase exponentially with temperature (the Arrhenius equation, k = Ae^(−E_a/RT), falls out of the fraction of molecules with E ≥ E_a). It explains why catalysts are so effective — lowering E_a from 100 to 50 kJ/mol can increase the reactive fraction by a factor of millions at room temperature. It explains why enzymes have optimal temperature ranges — too cold and the tail is too small; too hot and the enzyme denatures (unfolds). It explains why a fever of just 2°C above normal speeds up immune responses — and why a fever of 5°C above normal can denature proteins and become life-threatening. The Boltzmann distribution turns the qualitative observation "heat speeds reactions" into a quantitative, predictive tool.

### Catalysts: lowering the barrier without being consumed
*BB 295 · chemistry · kinetics*

**Idea.** A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up. It does this by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. More molecules can cross the lower barrier at the same temperature — the rate increases, sometimes by factors of millions. The catalyst emerges unchanged at the end, ready to catalyse another reaction cycle. It's the chemical equivalent of a facilitator — it lowers the entry fee but doesn't pay it.

**Concrete.** Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). Uncatalysed, this is slow — a bottle of H₂O₂ lasts for months. Add a pinch of manganese dioxide (MnO₂) powder and the solution erupts in a fountain of oxygen. The MnO₂ provides a surface where H₂O₂ molecules adsorb, bonds weaken, and the reaction occurs with a much lower E_a. Filter the MnO₂ out afterward and it's chemically unchanged — the same mass, the same composition. In the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃), an iron catalyst lowers the temperature needed from ~1000°C to ~450°C, saving enormous energy and making the process economically viable — producing the ammonia that feeds half the world's population through fertilisers. Without the iron catalyst, the world's food supply would be a fraction of what it is.

**Definition.** A **catalyst** is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, without being permanently consumed. **Heterogeneous catalysis**: catalyst is in a different phase from reactants (typically solid catalyst, gaseous/liquid reactants) — e.g., iron in Haber process, platinum in catalytic converters, MnO₂ in H₂O₂ decomposition. **Homogeneous catalysis**: catalyst is in the same phase — e.g., acid-catalysed ester hydrolysis. **Enzymes** are biological catalysts — proteins with active sites that bind specific substrates with exquisite selectivity. A catalyst does NOT change ΔH, equilibrium position, or the yield — it only changes the rate. It lowers E_a for BOTH forward and reverse reactions equally.

**In action.** Catalysis is one of the most economically important fields in chemistry. The Haber–Bosch process (iron catalyst) produces ~180 million tonnes of ammonia annually. Catalytic converters (Pt, Rh, Pd) clean car exhaust. Ziegler–Natta catalysts (titanium compounds) produce polyethylene and polypropylene — the plastics of modern life. Enzymes catalyse every biochemical reaction in your body: carbonic anhydrase speeds up CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ by a factor of ~10⁷, essential for respiration. Catalysts do not violate thermodynamics — they cannot make an endergonic reaction (ΔG > 0) occur spontaneously. They can only accelerate reactions that are already thermodynamically favoured. But that acceleration, from geological to industrial timescales, is what makes modern chemistry possible.
