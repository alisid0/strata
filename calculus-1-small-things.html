Chapter one

Small things

Most people who give up on calculus give up on the notation rather than the ideas. This chapter deals with the symbols first, then with the one fact everything else is built on: that some small quantities are so much smaller than others that they stop counting. Nothing is assumed beyond multiplying and adding.

1. The symbols

There is one letter to get past, and it is d. It is not a number, and it is not something you multiply by. It is a label, and it means a little bit of.

So dx is a little bit of x. That is the entire translation. Everything else in the notation is built from it.

2. Two kinds of small

Now the fact that makes calculus work, and it is the part most courses hurry past.

Take a small number and square it. The result is not just smaller. It is smaller in a different league, and the gap widens the smaller you started.

The new area is made of three pieces added to the original: a strip up the right side, a strip along the top, and a small square in the corner where the two strips meet. Each strip is x long and h wide. The corner is h by h.

(x + h)² = x² + 2xh + h²the original, two strips, and the corner

That is ordinary algebra, and you may have multiplied it out at school without anyone mentioning that the three terms are three pieces of a picture. Now put numbers to it.

5The corner

And the corner square, which is 0.1 by 0.1?

Answer step 4 first.

Two units of new area from the strips, and one hundredth of a unit from the corner. The corner is already almost irrelevant, and it was h² that made it so. Drag the slider above down towards zero and watch its share collapse.

4. The ratio

We are not really interested in how much area appeared. We want to know how much appeared per unit of growth, because that is what tells us how sensitive the area is to the side. So divide the extra area by h.

[ (x + h)² − x² ] / h = (2xh + h²) / h = 2x + hexact, with nothing thrown away yet

6Put numbers in

With x = 10 and h = 0.1, the extra area was 2.01. Divide that by h to get the extra area per unit of growth.

Answer step 5 first.

7Shrink it

The formula says the ratio is 2x + h, so at x = 10 it is 20 + h. With h = 0.1 you got 20.1. With h = 0.01 it would be 20.01. What number is it closing in on as h shrinks?

Answer step 6 first.

There is a question you should be asking here, and it is the one that makes people suspect calculus of cheating.

8Was that a swindle?

If h is not zero, then 2x + h is not 2x, so the answer looks wrong. But if h is zero, then dividing by h was dividing by zero, which is not allowed. So what actually happened?

Take your time over this one.

Answer step 7 first.

5. What you have

You have just differentiated something. The rate at which the area of a square grows, per unit of growth in its side, is 2x, and it was obtained with nothing but the algebra of (x+h)² and the observation that the corner is in a smaller league than the strips.

y = x²  ⇒   dy/dx = 2xread: a little bit of y, per little bit of x, is 2x

Check it against the picture. At x = 10 the answer is 20, and the two strips did indeed have area 2 × 10 × h. The strips are the answer. The corner was never anything but a distraction, and the whole art of the next chapter is knowing which parts of an expression are strips and which are corners.

The method, in three lines

Work out what the quantity becomes when you nudge the input by h, and subtract the original to find what the nudge added.
Divide by h, which is exact and legal because h is not yet zero.
Ask what the result approaches as h shrinks, and read off the answer.

The same three lines will handle x³, and then any power at all, which is the next chapter. What they will not yet handle is anything with a division in it, or a sine, or a power of a power. Those come later and none of them change the method.
