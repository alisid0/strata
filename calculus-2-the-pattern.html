Chapter two

The pattern

Chapter 1 differentiated one thing, x², using nothing but school algebra and the observation that some pieces are too small to count. This chapter does the same to a cube, notices that the answers are forming a pattern, and turns that pattern into a rule that handles any power at all.

1. Growing together

Two quantities are tied together when one of them settles the other. The side of a square settles its area. The radius of a balloon settles its volume. The hours you work settle your pay.

Tied together does not mean they grow at the same pace, and that is the interesting part. Add a centimetre to a small square and it gains very little area. Add the same centimetre to a large one and it gains a great deal. The relationship between the two is itself something that varies, and pinning it down is what the whole subject is for.

1Before any algebra

A square of side 3 grows to side 4, gaining 7 units of area. A square of side 30 grows to side 31, gaining the same one unit of side. Without working it out exactly, roughly how much area does the second square gain?

Pick one. The point is the rough size, not the exact figure.

2. A cube that grows

Now the same treatment as the square, one dimension further up. A cube of side x has volume x³. Push every face out by a little bit h and it gains volume in three sorts of piece.

What a growing cube gains, taken apart

The slabs and bars are drawn thicker than they would really be, so that you can see them at all.

A flat slab on each of the three faces you can see, a thin bar along each of the three edges where two slabs meet, and one small cube in the corner where all three meet. Written out, that is a line of algebra you may already know.

(x + h)³ = x³ + 3x²h + 3xh² + h³the cube, three slabs, three bars, one corner

Chapter 1 had one piece worth keeping and one worth discarding. Here there is one worth keeping and two worth discarding, and they are not even equally negligible.

3. The ratio again

Same three lines as chapter 1. Take what the growth added, divide by h, and see what survives when h is driven towards nothing.

[ (x + h)³ − x³ ] / h = (3x²h + 3xh² + h³) / h = 3x² + 3xh + h²exact, with nothing yet discarded

2Put numbers in

With x = 10 and h = 0.1, the cube gained 30.301 in volume. Divide that by h to get the gain per unit of growth.

3Shrink h away

The ratio is 3x² + 3xh + h², and at x = 10 that is 300 + 3h + h². What number does it close in on as h goes to nothing?

4. The pattern

Two results is not a pattern. Three might be. Here is x itself as well, which needs no picture: grow x by h and it gains exactly h, so the gain per unit of growth is 1, whatever x happens to be.

4Guess the next line

Read down the two columns and continue the pattern. If y = x⁴, what should dy/dx be? Type it using a caret for the power, so 4x³ would be typed as 4x^3.

The two moves are always the same.

Answer step 3 first.

The rule is doing two things, and they are worth saying separately because it is easy to remember one and forget the other. The old power comes down and multiplies. Then the power itself drops by one.

y = xn  ⇒   dy/dx = n·xn−1the power rule, and almost everything else is built on it

You do not have to take it on trust. Multiply out (x+h)n and every term carries at least one h except the leading xn, which cancels against the original. Exactly one term carries a single h and no more, and there are n ways of picking it, which is where the n comes from. Everything else carries h² or worse and is in the smaller league from chapter 1.

5. Beyond whole powers

The cube and the square gave the rule an honest start, but pictures run out at three dimensions. There is no fourth face to grow, and there is certainly no picture for a power of one half. So does the rule survive where the picture cannot follow?

It does, and you can check it without any theory at all. A square root is a power: √x = x1/2. A reciprocal is a power: 1/x = x−1. Put those into the rule and then measure directly to see whether the answer holds.

5Trust the rule where you cannot draw it

Apply the rule to y = √x, which is x1/2. Bring the half down and drop the power by one, giving ½x−1/2, which is the same as 1/(2√x). What is that at x = 4?

Answer step 4 first.

Measured directly, the gain per unit of growth of √x at x = 4 comes out as 0.2500 to four places, so the rule holds in territory where no cube can be drawn. It does the same for 1/x, where the rule gives −x−2, and the minus sign is telling the truth: as x grows, 1/x shrinks, so its rate of change is negative.

What you can now differentiate

Any single power of x, whole or fractional, positive or negative. That is a narrow-sounding class that turns out to include a great deal, and the next chapter widens it to anything built out of such powers by adding, subtracting or multiplying by a constant, which covers every polynomial there is.

What it will not yet do is anything with two variable quantities multiplied together, or a power raised to a power, or a sine. None of those change the method. They only need the same three lines applied more carefully, and they are what chapter 3 is for.
