## BB-NEW-100 — Isotopes: same element, different weight

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** atomic-structure | **Concept:** isotopes; neutron-count; mass-number | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 37]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Not every atom of the same element weighs the same. Carbon, the element of life, comes in at least three versions — identical in every chemical way, but with different masses. The only difference is the number of neutrons in the nucleus. These variants are isotopes.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons — 12 particles in the nucleus. Carbon-13 has 6 protons and 7 neutrons — 13 particles. Carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons — 14 particles. All three are carbon. They all have 6 protons, so they all have 6 electrons arranged the same way, so they all form the same bonds and make the same compounds. Chemically, they are indistinguishable. The only difference is mass — and for carbon-14, instability: it slowly decays, which is how radiocarbon dating works.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Isotopes</strong> are atoms of the same element — same number of protons, same atomic number Z — but with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different <strong>mass numbers</strong> A (where A = protons + neutrons). Isotopes are identified by their mass number: carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14. Most elements exist as a mixture of isotopes. The chemical properties of isotopes are nearly identical because chemical behaviour is determined by electrons, not neutrons.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The atomic masses on the periodic table are not whole numbers because they are weighted averages of all naturally occurring isotopes. Chlorine's atomic mass is 35.5 — not because any chlorine atom has a half-neutron, but because chlorine in nature is about 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Isotopes are also why the standard kilogram was once defined by a single platinum-iridium cylinder in Paris — a physical artefact whose mass could change, unlike the isotopic-constant-based definition adopted in 2019.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of three carbon atoms side by side: C-12 (6p, 6n), C-13 (6p, 7n), C-14 (6p, 8n). Nuclei are enlarged to show proton and neutron counts. All three have the same electron arrangement shown as 2,4. Below: "Same element. Different neutron count. Same chemistry." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-101 — Atomic number and mass number: the two-number ID

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** atomic-structure | **Concept:** atomic-number; mass-number; Z-and-A; proton-count | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 37, BB-NEW-100]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every atom carries two key numbers. The atomic number Z tells you what element it is — the number of protons, fixed and non-negotiable. The mass number A tells you which isotope it is — the total of protons and neutrons. Z is the identity. A is the heft.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>An atom with Z = 11 is sodium, always and forever. Change the proton count and you change the element — sodium to magnesium, oxygen to fluorine. The mass number A can vary without changing the element: sodium-23 has 11 protons and 12 neutrons (A = 23). The numbers are written as ²³₁₁Na — mass number on top, atomic number below, symbol to the right. The atomic number is often omitted because the symbol already implies it — Na is always Z = 11.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>atomic number</strong> Z is the number of protons in the nucleus. It uniquely identifies the element and determines its position on the periodic table. In a neutral atom, Z also equals the number of electrons. The <strong>mass number</strong> A is the total number of nucleons — protons plus neutrons. The number of neutrons is A − Z. Notation: ᴬ₂X, where X is the element symbol. The periodic table lists elements in order of increasing Z; the atomic mass listed is the weighted average of isotopic masses.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Z answers "what is it?" A answers "how heavy is this version?" Together they let you calculate the number of each subatomic particle in any atom: protons = Z, electrons = Z (neutral atom), neutrons = A − Z. For ²³₁₁Na: 11 protons, 11 electrons, 12 neutrons. For ³⁵₁₇Cl: 17 protons, 17 electrons, 18 neutrons. This two-number system — Z for identity, A for mass — is the universal ID card for every atom in the universe.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the notation ᴬ₂X with A, Z, and X labelled. Below, three examples: ¹²₆C, ¹³₆C, ²³₁₁Na each with proton/neutron/electron counts worked out. The periodic table is referenced with an arrow: "Z = position on the table." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-102 — Electron shells: where the electrons live

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** atomic-structure | **Concept:** electron-shells; energy-levels; Bohr-model | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 37, BB-NEW-101]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Electrons don't orbit the nucleus like planets around a sun — that's a useful picture, not the true one. But it captures something real: electrons are organised in layers, like the rungs of a ladder. Each rung — each shell — can hold only so many electrons before the next one starts to fill. And it's the outermost shell that decides almost everything about how an atom behaves.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Sodium has 11 electrons. They fill from the inside out: 2 in the first shell (the closest to the nucleus), 8 in the second shell, and 1 left over in the third shell — written as 2,8,1. That lone electron in the outermost shell is restless. It wants to leave. Chlorine has 17 electrons: 2,8,7. Its outer shell is almost full — it desperately wants one more. This is why sodium and chlorine react so violently together: one wants to give an electron away, the other wants to take it.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electron shells</strong> (or energy levels) are regions around the nucleus where electrons are found, labelled n = 1, 2, 3, ... from innermost outward. Each shell has a maximum capacity: shell 1 holds 2 electrons, shell 2 holds 8, shell 3 holds 18 (but is stable at 8 for the first 20 elements), shell 4 holds 32. The <strong>valence shell</strong> is the outermost occupied shell — its electrons, the <strong>valence electrons</strong>, determine the atom's chemical properties and bonding behaviour.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The shell model explains the shape of the periodic table. Elements in the same column have the same number of valence electrons — that's why they behave similarly. Lithium (2,1), sodium (2,8,1), and potassium (2,8,8,1) all have one valence electron — all are soft, reactive metals that explode in water. The periodic table isn't a random grid; it's a map of electron configurations, and the rows and columns follow the filling of shells. The octet rule — atoms tend to want 8 valence electrons — is the single most useful rule in predicting chemical behaviour.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a sodium atom with its nucleus (11p, 12n) and three concentric circles: shell 1 with 2 electrons, shell 2 with 8, shell 3 with 1 (highlighted as the valence electron). Below: shell capacities 2, 8, 18, 32. An arrow points from the valence electron: "This one decides everything." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-103 — Electronic configuration: the address of each electron

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** atomic-structure | **Concept:** electronic-configuration; subshells; s-p-d-f; Aufbau | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-102]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Shells are the neighbourhoods. But within each shell, electrons don't all live at the same address. Each shell is divided into subshells — s, p, d, f — each with a different shape and capacity. The electronic configuration of an atom is the full postal code of every electron: which shell, which subshell, how many.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Carbon has 6 electrons. They fill in order of increasing energy: 1s gets 2, 2s gets 2, 2p gets the remaining 2. Written as 1s² 2s² 2p². Oxygen has 8: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. Sodium has 11: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. The superscript is the electron count in that subshell. The order of filling is not always the obvious one — 4s fills before 3d because 4s is actually lower in energy for those elements. The Aufbau principle ("building up") gives the full filling order.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electronic configuration</strong> is the distribution of electrons among the shells and subshells of an atom. Subshells: s (holds 2, spherical), p (holds 6, dumbbell-shaped, three orientations), d (holds 10, five orientations), f (holds 14, seven orientations). The filling order follows the <strong>Aufbau principle</strong>, <strong>Pauli exclusion principle</strong> (max two electrons per orbital, opposite spins), and <strong>Hund's rule</strong> (electrons occupy separate orbitals within a subshell before pairing up). The noble gas shorthand compresses the inner configuration: Na = [Ne] 3s¹.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The electronic configuration is the real reason the periodic table has the shape it does. The s-block (two columns on the left) fills the s subshell. The p-block (six columns on the right) fills the p subshell. The d-block (ten columns in the middle) fills the d subshell — these are the transition metals. The f-block (the two rows usually shown detached) fills the f subshell — the lanthanides and actinides. The table is not a design choice; it's a direct map of quantum mechanics. The configuration is why copper is coloured, why iron is magnetic, and why gold doesn't tarnish.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the Aufbau filling order diagram: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p, with arrows showing the diagonal sequence. Below, the electronic configuration notation of carbon (1s²2s²2p²) and sodium (1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹) with their subshells labelled. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-104 — Ions: atoms that have lost or gained electrons

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** atomic-structure | **Concept:** ions; cations; anions; electron-transfer | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 37, BB-NEW-102]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>An atom is neutral — protons and electrons balance. But remove an electron and the balance tips — the atom becomes a positively charged ion. Add an electron and it becomes negatively charged. An ion is just an atom with an imbalance. And that imbalance changes everything about how it behaves.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Sodium has 11 protons and 11 electrons — neutral. Lose one electron and it becomes Na⁺, with 11 protons but only 10 electrons — a net charge of +1. Chlorine has 17 protons and 17 electrons. Gain one electron and it becomes Cl⁻, with 17 protons and 18 electrons — a net charge of −1. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have opposite charges, so they attract — they form the ionic compound NaCl, table salt. The neutral sodium atom explodes in water; the sodium ion dissolves harmlessly. Losing one electron transforms the atom completely.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>An <strong>ion</strong> is an atom or molecule that has gained or lost one or more electrons, giving it a net electric charge. A <strong>cation</strong> (positive ion) forms when an atom loses electrons — metals tend to form cations (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻). An <strong>anion</strong> (negative ion) forms when an atom gains electrons — non-metals tend to form anions (Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻). The charge is written as a superscript: Na⁺, Ca²⁺, O²⁻, Cl⁻. Ions of opposite charge attract each other with an electrostatic force — this is the basis of ionic bonding.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Atoms form ions to achieve a stable electron configuration — typically the configuration of the nearest noble gas (8 valence electrons, a full outer shell). Sodium (2,8,1) loses one to become Na⁺ (2,8) — the same configuration as neon. Chlorine (2,8,7) gains one to become Cl⁻ (2,8,8) — the configuration of argon. The drive toward a full outer shell is the engine of ion formation, and by extension, of all ionic bonding. Ions are not just charged atoms — they are atoms that have solved their instability by giving up or taking on electrons.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a sodium atom losing an electron (arrow labelled "e⁻") to become Na⁺, and a chlorine atom gaining that electron to become Cl⁻. The resulting ions are shown with full outer shells highlighted. Below: "Na → Na⁺ + e⁻" and "Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻". Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-105 — The noble gases: already full, already happy

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** atomic-structure | **Concept:** noble-gases; full-shell; inert; octet-rule | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-102, BB-NEW-104]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>At the far right of the periodic table sits a column of elements that don't react with anything. They don't bond, don't burn, don't combine. They are complete. Their outer shells are already full — they have nothing to gain, nothing to give, and no reason to engage with any other atom. These are the noble gases.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Helium has 2 electrons — its only shell is full (capacity 2). Neon has 10 electrons: 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second — both shells full. Argon has 18: 2,8,8 — full. Each noble gas has a complete outer shell with no room and no vacancies. A sodium atom desperately wants to be like neon (hence Na⁺). A chlorine atom desperately wants to be like argon (hence Cl⁻). The noble gases are the destination — every other atom is trying to get there.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>noble gases</strong> (Group 18 / VIIIA) — helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon — are characterised by having a completely filled outer electron shell. This full-shell configuration is exceptionally stable. As a result, noble gases are chemically <strong>inert</strong> under most conditions — they do not readily form compounds. The <strong>octet rule</strong> — the observation that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve 8 valence electrons — is named after this noble gas stability. "Octet" = eight electrons in the outer shell.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The noble gases are not just a curiosity — they are the framework for understanding all chemical bonding. Every bond type — ionic, covalent, metallic — is fundamentally about atoms rearranging electrons to achieve noble gas configurations. The octet rule, with exceptions (hydrogen wants 2, boron is comfortable with 6, elements beyond period 3 can expand their octet), is the single most predictive rule in introductory chemistry. The noble gases are the baseline. Everything else is a departure — and a return.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the periodic table column for Group 18 highlighted: He (2), Ne (2,8), Ar (2,8,8), Kr, Xe, Rn. Beside each, the electron configuration shows full outer shells. An arrow from Na → Ne and Cl → Ar shows "atoms become ions to reach noble gas configuration." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-106 — Why atoms bond: the drive to fill the shell

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** why-atoms-bond; stability; octet-rule-application | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-105, BB-NEW-104]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Two hydrogen atoms meet in space. Neither has a full shell — each has one lonely electron, wanting one more to reach helium's stability. So they share. They move close enough that their electron clouds overlap, and each nucleus now feels the pull of both electrons. The two atoms become one molecule — H₂ — and the arrangement is more stable than either atom alone. This is bonding: atoms sacrifice independence for stability.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A sodium atom (2,8,1) and a chlorine atom (2,8,7) are both unstable in their own way. Sodium's lone valence electron is weakly held; chlorine's almost-full shell exerts a strong pull for one more. Sodium gives its electron to chlorine. Both achieve noble gas configurations: Na⁺ (2,8) like neon, Cl⁻ (2,8,8) like argon. The resulting oppositely charged ions attract — an ionic bond. The energy of the Na⁺Cl⁻ pair is lower than the energy of Na + Cl separately. The difference is released as heat. Bonding releases energy because the bonded state is more stable.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Atoms bond to achieve a lower, more stable energy state — almost always by attaining a full outer electron shell (the noble gas configuration). The three main strategies: <strong>give and take</strong> (ionic bonding — one atom transfers electrons to another), <strong>share</strong> (covalent bonding — atoms pool electrons), and <strong>communal pool</strong> (metallic bonding — electrons delocalise across many atoms). All bonding is electrostatic at root: the attraction between positive nuclei and negative electrons, arranged in a configuration that minimises total energy.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Bonding is not a choice atoms "want" to make — it's a consequence of the laws of electrostatics and quantum mechanics. When the total energy of a bonded system is lower than the total energy of the separated atoms, the bond forms spontaneously. The energy released when a bond forms is the <strong>bond energy</strong>; the same energy must be supplied to break the bond. This energy accounting — bonds forming releases energy, bonds breaking absorbs energy — is the foundation of thermochemistry and the reason fuel burns and food sustains us.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing showing three panels: (1) two H atoms far apart, each with 1 electron, (2) the atoms close together sharing electrons — the H₂ molecule with a full shell, (3) an energy diagram showing the bonded state at lower energy than separated atoms with the gap labelled "bond energy released." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-107 — Ionic bonding: give and take

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** ionic-bonding; electron-transfer; ionic-lattice; electrostatic-attraction | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-104, BB-NEW-106]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Sodium gives an electron. Chlorine takes it. They are now oppositely charged, and opposite charges attract — hard. This is ionic bonding in one sentence: one atom transfers an electron to another, the resulting ions stick together electrostatically, and a compound is born.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Hold a piece of sodium metal. Soft, silvery, you can cut it with a knife. Drop it in chlorine gas — a pale green poison — and it erupts into a brilliant yellow flame. What's left is a white crystalline powder: sodium chloride, table salt. The sodium atoms each gave an electron to a chlorine atom. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions packed together in a regular, repeating 3D grid — an <strong>ionic lattice</strong> — each sodium surrounded by six chlorides, each chloride by six sodiums. The substance that emerges — brittle, high-melting, dissolving in water to conduct electricity — shares almost no properties with either parent element.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Ionic bonding</strong> is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when one or more electrons are transferred from a metal atom (low electronegativity) to a non-metal atom (high electronegativity). The resulting <strong>ionic compound</strong> is a crystalline lattice — not individual molecules, but an extended, repeating arrangement of ions. Properties: high melting and boiling points (strong electrostatic forces throughout), brittle (ions of like charge repel if layers shift), conduct electricity when molten or dissolved (ions are free to move), do not conduct as solids (ions are locked in place).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Ionic bonding is not just NaCl. Magnesium oxide (MgO) — Mg loses two electrons to become Mg²⁺, O gains two to become O²⁻. The double charges mean a stronger attraction — MgO melts at 2,852°C, used to line furnaces. Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) — the ionic lattice that makes up limestone, chalk, and seashells. The formula of an ionic compound is not a molecular formula but an <strong>empirical formula</strong> — the simplest whole-number ratio of ions that balances the charges: Na⁺Cl⁻ (1:1), Mg²⁺O²⁻ (1:1), Ca²⁺(Cl⁻)₂ (1:2). There is no "molecule" of NaCl — just a lattice of alternating ions.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the NaCl lattice — a 3D grid of alternating Na⁺ (small, labelled +) and Cl⁻ (larger, labelled −) ions. An arrow shows an electron transferring from Na to Cl. Below: "Ionic bonding = electron transfer + electrostatic attraction. No molecules — a lattice." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-108 — Covalent bonding: sharing is the strategy

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** covalent-bonding; electron-sharing; molecules; Lewis-structures | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-106]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>What if neither atom is willing to fully give up an electron? They share instead. Each atom contributes one electron to a shared pair, and both nuclei are attracted to both electrons — the pair acts as the glue. This is covalent bonding, and it's how the vast majority of substances in your body are held together.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Two hydrogen atoms, each with one electron. Neither can rip an electron from the other — they're equally matched. So they overlap. The two electrons now occupy the space between the two nuclei. Each nucleus is pulled toward the electron pair, and the pair is pulled toward both nuclei. The result is H₂ — a single covalent bond. Two fluorine atoms, each with 7 valence electrons, share one pair to form F₂ — both achieve a full octet. Oxygen atoms share two pairs (a double bond) to form O₂. Nitrogen atoms share three pairs (a triple bond) to form N₂ — one of the strongest bonds in nature.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Covalent bonding</strong> is the sharing of one or more electron pairs between two atoms, typically non-metals. A <strong>single bond</strong> shares one pair (H−H, F−F). A <strong>double bond</strong> shares two pairs (O=O). A <strong>triple bond</strong> shares three pairs (N≡N). Covalent bonds form discrete <strong>molecules</strong> — finite groups of atoms with a fixed formula — unlike ionic lattices. Bond strength increases from single to triple: a triple bond is shorter and stronger. <strong>Lewis structures</strong> (dot-and-cross diagrams) show how valence electrons are shared.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Covalent bonding is the chemistry of life. Water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), DNA — all are covalent molecules. The carbon atom, with 4 valence electrons, forms 4 covalent bonds — allowing it to build chains, rings, and frameworks of almost infinite variety. This is why carbon is the backbone of organic chemistry and of life itself. The shapes of covalent molecules — linear (CO₂), bent (H₂O), tetrahedral (CH₄) — are determined by the number of electron pairs around the central atom repelling each other into the arrangement that minimises repulsion. Shape determines function, in chemistry as in biology.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing showing three covalent bonds: H−H (single, with dot-and-cross diagram showing 2 shared electrons), O=O (double, 4 shared electrons), N≡N (triple, 6 shared electrons). Below: Lewis structures for H₂O and CO₂ with lone pairs shown. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-109 — Metallic bonding: a sea of electrons

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** metallic-bonding; delocalised-electrons; sea-of-electrons | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-106]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Metals don't transfer electrons and they don't share them in pairs. Instead, every metal atom in the lattice releases its valence electrons into a communal pool — a sea of electrons that flows freely between the positive metal ions. The ions are fixed in place; the electrons are not. This is metallic bonding, and it explains why metals conduct, bend, and shine.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Take a copper wire. The copper atoms are arranged in a regular lattice, but their outer electrons are not tied to any specific atom — they wander freely through the entire structure. Apply a voltage and the electrons drift — electric current. Hammer the wire and the layers of ions slide past each other — the sea of electrons flows around them and the metal bends instead of shattering. Shine light on the surface and the mobile electrons absorb and re-emit photons — the metal gleams. All three properties — conductivity, malleability, lustre — come from the same delocalised electrons.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Metallic bonding</strong> is the electrostatic attraction between a regular lattice of positive metal ions and a sea of <strong>delocalised electrons</strong> — valence electrons that are not bound to any specific atom but move freely throughout the structure. This model explains: (1) <strong>Electrical conductivity</strong> — mobile electrons carry charge. (2) <strong>Thermal conductivity</strong> — mobile electrons transfer kinetic energy. (3) <strong>Malleability and ductility</strong> — layers of ions can slide without breaking the bonding. (4) <strong>Lustre</strong> — mobile electrons interact with light. (5) Variable melting points — the strength of the metallic bond depends on the number of delocalised electrons and the size of the ions.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Metallic bonding covers the whole periodic table's left and centre. Sodium (one delocalised electron per atom) is soft enough to cut with a knife, melts at 98°C. Tungsten (many delocalised electrons, small ions) is hard enough to drill through steel, melts at 3,422°C — the filament in incandescent light bulbs. Alloys exploit metallic bonding: adding a small amount of carbon to iron disrupts the iron lattice just enough to prevent layers sliding, producing steel — harder, stronger, but still bendable. The sea of electrons is why a bent copper pipe doesn't break, why a gold ring can be resized, and why aluminium foil exists.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a metal lattice: a grid of positive metal ions (labelled M⁺, M⁺, M⁺) surrounded by a shaded cloud labelled "sea of delocalised electrons." An arrow shows a voltage source making the electrons drift. A second image shows layers of ions sliding under a hammer — the electron cloud flows around them. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.
