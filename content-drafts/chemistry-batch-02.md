## BB-NEW-220 — The mole in practice: from formula to grams and back

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** stoichiometry | **Concept:** mole-calculations; n=m/M; molar-mass; Avogadro-applications | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 34, BB-NEW-101]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Card 34 introduced the mole as the chemist's dozen — 6.022 × 10²³ things. Now we use it. The mole is the bridge between the atomic world (which you can't weigh) and the lab bench (which you can). One equation — n = m/M — converts grams to moles and back. The periodic table gives you M (molar mass in g/mol). The balance gives you m (mass in grams). The mole number n is what you actually use in equations.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>How many moles in 80 g of NaOH? Na = 23, O = 16, H = 1. M(NaOH) = 23+16+1 = 40 g/mol. n = m/M = 80/40 = 2 moles. How many formula units? 2 × 6.022×10²³ = 1.204×10²⁴. Now the reverse: you need 0.5 moles of H₂SO₄. What mass? M(H₂SO₄) = 2(1)+32+4(16) = 98 g/mol. m = nM = 0.5×98 = 49 g. Weigh out 49 g on the balance and you have exactly 0.5 moles. The balance reads grams; the equation reads moles. n = m/M is the translator.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The fundamental mole equation: <strong>n = m / M</strong>, where n = amount in moles, m = mass in grams, M = molar mass in g/mol (numerically equal to the relative atomic/formula mass from the periodic table). For gases at RTP (room temperature and pressure, ~20°C, 1 atm): 1 mole of any gas occupies <strong>24 dm³</strong> (or 24,000 cm³). For solutions: <strong>n = cV</strong>, where c is concentration in mol/dm³ and V is volume in dm³. These three equations — n = m/M, n = V/24 (gases), n = cV (solutions) — cover the vast majority of mole calculations.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Mole calculations are the daily arithmetic of chemistry. A pharmacist calculates the moles of active ingredient per tablet. An environmental chemist calculates the moles of CO₂ released per litre of fuel. A materials scientist calculates the moles of monomer needed to make a polymer of a target molecular weight. Every balanced equation is read in moles — "2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O" means "2 moles of H₂ react with 1 mole of O₂ to produce 2 moles of H₂O." The coefficients in the equation ARE mole ratios. Convert everything to moles first, then use the ratio, then convert back to the unit you need. This workflow never changes.</p>

## BB-NEW-221 — Empirical and molecular formulas: what the numbers really mean

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** stoichiometry | **Concept:** empirical-formula; molecular-formula; mass-percent-to-formula; combustion-analysis | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-220]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The molecular formula (C₆H₁₂O₆ for glucose) tells you the exact number of each atom in one molecule. The empirical formula (CH₂O) tells you the simplest whole-number ratio. They are related by a whole-number multiplier: molecular = empirical × n. The empirical formula comes from experiment — you burn the compound, measure the CO₂ and H₂O produced, and work backwards to the ratios of C, H, and O.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A compound is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Assume 100 g: 40.0 g C, 6.7 g H, 53.3 g O. Convert to moles: n(C) = 40.0/12 = 3.33 mol, n(H) = 6.7/1 = 6.7 mol, n(O) = 53.3/16 = 3.33 mol. Divide by smallest (3.33): C = 1, H = 2, O = 1. Empirical formula: CH₂O. If the molar mass is found to be 180 g/mol, the molecular formula multiplier n = 180 / 30 = 6. Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂O₆ — glucose. The empirical formula (CH₂O) is the blueprint; the molecular formula (×6) is the actual building.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>empirical formula</strong> is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The <strong>molecular formula</strong> is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. Procedure: (1) Convert mass percentages (or masses) to moles using n = m/M. (2) Divide all mole values by the smallest to get the simplest ratio. (3) If the ratios are not whole numbers (e.g. 1.5), multiply all by a factor to clear fractions. (4) The result is the empirical formula. (5) To get the molecular formula, divide the compound's molar mass by the empirical formula mass — the result is the multiplier n. Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Empirical formula determination is the detective work of chemistry. An unknown white powder — what is it? Burn it, measure the combustion products, calculate the empirical formula, measure the molar mass, deduce the molecular formula, then search the database for a match. This is how new compounds are identified and how purity is verified. For ionic compounds, the empirical formula IS the formula (NaCl, CaCl₂) because ionic lattices don't have discrete molecules — the formula gives the ion ratio, not a molecular unit. The term "molecular formula" only strictly applies to covalent molecules. For ionic compounds, we use "formula unit."</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the workflow: % composition → masses (per 100g) → moles → divide by smallest → empirical formula CH₂O → molar mass 180 → n=6 → molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆. A combustion apparatus sketched beside: sample burns, CO₂ and H₂O collected, masses measured. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-222 — Relative atomic mass: why chlorine is 35.5

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** stoichiometry | **Concept:** relative-atomic-mass; weighted-average; isotopic-abundance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-100, BB-NEW-220]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Look at the periodic table. Chlorine's atomic mass is 35.5 — not a whole number. But you can't have half a proton or half a neutron. The reason: chlorine in nature is a mixture of two isotopes — 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. The mass on the table is the weighted average. No single chlorine atom weighs 35.5; that's the average you'd get if you scooped up a handful.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Natural chlorine: 75.77% Cl-35 (mass = 34.97 u) and 24.23% Cl-37 (mass = 36.97 u). Weighted average: (0.7577 × 34.97) + (0.2423 × 36.97) = 26.50 + 8.96 = 35.46 ≈ 35.5. Bromine: 50.7% Br-79 and 49.3% Br-81. Average: (0.507 × 79) + (0.493 × 81) ≈ 79.9. The periodic table mass listed for each element is this weighted average of its naturally occurring isotopes. The unit is the unified atomic mass unit (u), defined as 1/12 the mass of one carbon-12 atom — so by definition, carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12 u.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Relative atomic mass</strong> A_r of an element is the weighted average mass of its naturally occurring isotopes relative to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It is dimensionless (a ratio) but numerically equal to the molar mass in g/mol. For an element with isotopes of masses m₁, m₂, ... and fractional abundances a₁, a₂, ...: A_r = a₁m₁ + a₂m₂ + ... where a₁+a₂+... = 1. <strong>Relative molecular mass</strong> M_r is the sum of the A_r values of all atoms in the molecular formula. <strong>Relative formula mass</strong> is the same concept applied to ionic compounds.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The non-integer masses on the periodic table are not rounding errors — they are evidence of isotopes. Mass spectrometry measures isotopic ratios with extraordinary precision, and from these the weighted average is computed. The same principle applies to forensic science: the isotopic ratio of carbon (¹³C/¹²C) in a sample can reveal whether a material came from a plant using C3 or C4 photosynthesis — tracing the origin of drugs, food, or fibres. The decimal places on the periodic table are not imperfections; they're fingerprints of nuclear physics preserved in the chemistry of the Earth's crust.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of two pie charts for chlorine: 75% Cl-35 (shown as 17p+18n) and 25% Cl-37 (17p+20n). The weighted average calculation below: (0.75×35) + (0.25×37) = 35.5. The periodic table entry for Cl is shown with 35.5 circled. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-223 — Ionic lattices: the giant structure and its consequences

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** ionic-lattice; giant-structure; properties-from-ionic-bonding | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-107]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>An ionic bond is not a single Na⁺–Cl⁻ pair floating alone. It's a giant, repeating 3D lattice of alternating positive and negative ions, extending in all directions. A grain of salt contains billions of ions in a single continuous structure. There are no "molecules" of NaCl — just a crystal. And this giant structure dictates every property: high melting point, brittleness, and the ability to conduct when molten.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Sodium chloride melts at 801°C. Why so high? Every Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions, and every Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺ ions. To melt the crystal, you must overcome the electrostatic attraction between every ion and all its neighbours simultaneously — a colossal energy requirement. Magnesium oxide (MgO) melts at 2,852°C because Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have double the charge, so the attraction is four times stronger. Now strike a salt crystal with a hammer: it shatters. Why? The blow shifts a layer of ions so that like charges (Na⁺ next to Na⁺) align — they repel violently and the crystal cleaves. Ionic solids are brittle because their strength is also their weakness: the rigid lattice tolerates no misalignment.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>An <strong>ionic lattice</strong> is a giant, regular, repeating 3D arrangement of oppositely charged ions held together by strong electrostatic forces throughout the structure. Properties arising from the lattice: (1) <strong>High melting/boiling points</strong> — strong electrostatic forces require large energy to overcome. (2) <strong>Hard but brittle</strong> — hard because the forces are strong; brittle because a small shift brings like charges together. (3) <strong>Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved</strong> — free-moving ions carry charge; as solids, ions are fixed and cannot conduct. (4) <strong>Soluble in water</strong> (many, not all) — water molecules surround and separate the ions.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The lattice model explains why we write NaCl, not Na₁Cl₁, Na₂Cl₂, or NaₙClₙ — the formula gives the simplest ion ratio, not a molecular count. It explains why salt water conducts electricity (ions are free) but solid salt does not (ions locked in place). It explains why MgO lines furnaces (enormous lattice energy = enormous melting point). It explains the octahedral shape of NaCl and the different crystal habits of different ionic compounds. The properties of the substance are not in any single ion pair — they emerge from the cooperative behaviour of the entire lattice.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the NaCl lattice: a 3D grid of alternating Na⁺ (small, +) and Cl⁻ (large, −) ions. A layer shift is shown — Na⁺ now adjacent to Na⁺ with a repulsion arrow, explaining brittleness. Below: "High mp — strong electrostatic forces throughout. Brittle — like charges repel if layers shift. Conducts only when molten/dissolved — ions must be free to move." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-224 — Covalent networks: diamond, graphite, and giant molecules

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** giant-covalent; diamond; graphite; silicon-dioxide; allotropes | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-108]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Some covalent substances form molecules (H₂O, CO₂ — small, discrete). Others form giant covalent networks — every atom bonded to neighbouring atoms in a continuous 3D framework. Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. One is the hardest natural substance on Earth; the other is soft enough to write with. The atoms are identical; the bonding pattern is everything.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>In <strong>diamond</strong>, each carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds to four other carbons, arranged in a rigid tetrahedral 3D network. Every electron is locked in a bond — no free electrons. Result: hardest natural substance, doesn't conduct electricity, extremely high melting point (~3500°C), transparent when pure. In <strong>graphite</strong>, each carbon forms three bonds in a flat hexagonal sheet. The fourth electron from each carbon becomes delocalised — free to move between the sheets. The sheets are held together by weak van der Waals forces and slide easily. Result: soft (sheets slip), conducts electricity (delocalised electrons), high melting point (strong in-plane bonds), opaque and shiny. Same element. Two bonding patterns. Opposite properties.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Giant covalent structures</strong> (network solids) are continuous 3D frameworks where atoms are joined by covalent bonds throughout the entire structure. There are no discrete molecules — the whole crystal is one giant molecule. Examples: diamond (C, tetrahedral, each C bonded to 4 others), graphite (C, hexagonal layers, each C bonded to 3), silicon dioxide SiO₂ (each Si bonded to 4 O, each O bonded to 2 Si). Properties: very high melting points (strong covalent bonds throughout), generally hard, do not conduct electricity (except graphite — delocalised electrons), insoluble in water. <strong>Allotropes</strong> are different structural forms of the same element — diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The diamond/graphite dichotomy is the most dramatic example of structure determining properties in all of chemistry. Diamond's hardness makes it an industrial abrasive and cutting tool. Graphite's slipperiness makes it a dry lubricant and pencil "lead." Graphite's electrical conductivity makes it useful for electrodes and brushes in motors. The recently discovered allotropes — graphene (single graphite sheet, strongest material known, superb conductor) and fullerenes (C₆₀ football-shaped molecules) — extend the lesson: carbon's bonding flexibility produces an entire materials library from one element.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of diamond (tetrahedral network, each C bonded to 4 others) beside graphite (hexagonal sheets with weak inter-layer forces and delocalised electrons shown as a cloud between sheets). Properties table: diamond — hard, insulator, transparent; graphite — soft, conductor, opaque. "Same element. Different bonding. Opposite properties." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-225 — Molecular shapes: VSEPR — the electrons decide the geometry

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** VSEPR; molecular-shapes; electron-pair-repulsion; bond-angles | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-108, BB-NEW-102]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Molecules are not flat drawings on paper. They are 3D objects with definite shapes — linear, bent, tetrahedral, pyramidal. The shape is not arbitrary. It is determined by one rule: electron pairs repel each other, and they arrange themselves around the central atom to be as far apart as possible. This is VSEPR — Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion — and it predicts the geometry of almost any molecule.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Carbon dioxide (CO₂): carbon has two double bonds to two oxygens — two regions of electron density around C. They repel to opposite sides: the molecule is <strong>linear</strong>, 180°. Water (H₂O): oxygen has two bonds to hydrogen AND two lone pairs — four regions of electron density. A tetrahedral arrangement is 109.5°, but the lone pairs squeeze the bonds closer together — the H-O-H angle is 104.5°, and the molecule is <strong>bent</strong>. Methane (CH₄): four bonds, no lone pairs — perfect tetrahedron, 109.5°. Ammonia (NH₃): three bonds, one lone pair — the lone pair pushes the three N-H bonds down, giving a <strong>trigonal pyramidal</strong> shape with H-N-H angle ≈ 107°. In every case: count the electron regions (bonds + lone pairs), and the shape follows.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>VSEPR theory</strong> states that electron pairs (both bonding pairs and lone pairs) around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves to minimise repulsion, determining the molecular geometry. Key geometries: 2 electron pairs → <strong>linear</strong> (180°). 3 pairs → <strong>trigonal planar</strong> (120°). 4 pairs → <strong>tetrahedral</strong> (109.5°). With lone pairs: 4 pairs with 1 lone pair → <strong>trigonal pyramidal</strong> (~107°). 4 pairs with 2 lone pairs → <strong>bent</strong> (~104.5°). 5 pairs → <strong>trigonal bipyramidal</strong>. 6 pairs → <strong>octahedral</strong>. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs (they occupy more space), compressing bond angles slightly.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Shape determines function — in chemistry as in biology. The bent shape of water makes it polar, which makes it an extraordinary solvent. The tetrahedral shape of methane makes it non-polar, which is why oil and water don't mix. The planar shape of a drug molecule determines whether it fits into a receptor like a key into a lock. The double helix of DNA is a specific shape held by specific bond angles. VSEPR gives you the shape from the formula — a sketch on paper that becomes a 3D object, and that 3D object's shape determines what it can and cannot do.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the five key molecular shapes: linear (CO₂, 180°), trigonal planar (BF₃, 120°), tetrahedral (CH₄, 109.5°), trigonal pyramidal (NH₃, ~107°), bent (H₂O, ~104.5°). Each shows bonds, lone pairs as dashed clouds, and bond angles. Below: "Electron pairs repel — they go as far apart as possible." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-226 — Polarity: when sharing isn't equal

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** electronegativity; polar-bonds; polar-molecules; dipole-moment | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-108, BB-NEW-225]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In a covalent bond, electrons are shared — but not always equally. Oxygen pulls electrons harder than hydrogen. In an O-H bond, the electrons spend more time near the oxygen, giving it a partial negative charge (δ−) and leaving hydrogen with a partial positive charge (δ+). This is a polar bond — a covalent bond with unequal sharing. If the molecule's shape allows these bond polarities to add up rather than cancel out, the whole molecule is polar.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Electronegativity increases across the periodic table (left → right) and decreases down a group. Fluorine (4.0) is the most electronegative element; caesium (0.7) the least. In HF, the difference is 4.0−2.1 = 1.9 — a very polar bond. In CH, the difference is 2.5−2.1 = 0.4 — nearly non-polar. Water (H₂O): O-H bonds are polar, and the bent shape prevents the bond dipoles from cancelling — the molecule has a net dipole moment. Carbon dioxide (CO₂): C=O bonds are polar, but the linear shape makes the two bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel exactly — CO₂ is non-polar despite having polar bonds. Shape decides whether bond polarities add up or cancel out.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electronegativity</strong> is the ability of an atom to attract the shared electrons in a covalent bond (Pauling scale, 0.7–4.0). A <strong>polar bond</strong> forms when the electronegativity difference ΔEN > 0.4 (approximately). The atom with higher EN gets δ−; the other δ+. When ΔEN > ~1.7, the bond is considered <strong>ionic</strong> rather than polar covalent. A <strong>polar molecule</strong> has a net dipole moment — an asymmetrical distribution of charge. A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if the symmetry cancels the bond dipoles (e.g. CO₂, CCl₄ — tetrahedral symmetry cancels four polar C-Cl bonds).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Polarity governs solubility: "like dissolves like." Polar water dissolves polar and ionic substances (salt, sugar, alcohol). Non-polar solvents (hexane, oil) dissolve non-polar substances (grease, wax). It governs boiling points: polar molecules attract each other more strongly, requiring more energy to separate. It governs how drugs cross cell membranes (non-polar molecules pass through; polar ones need transport proteins). It governs chromatography: polar substances stick to polar stationary phases and move slowly. The polarity of a molecule is a single number (dipole moment, measured in Debyes) that encodes how asymmetrically its electrons are distributed — and that asymmetry dictates its interactions with everything around it.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of H₂O and CO₂ side by side. H₂O: bent shape, polar O-H bonds, net dipole arrow pointing down. CO₂: linear shape, polar C=O bonds, two equal and opposite dipole arrows cancelling — net dipole = 0. Electronegativity trend arrows on a mini periodic table. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-227 — Intermolecular forces: the weak attractions that shape the world

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** intermolecular-forces; van-der-Waals; dipole-dipole; London-dispersion | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-108, BB-NEW-226]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Covalent and ionic bonds hold atoms together within a molecule. Intermolecular forces hold molecules together. They are far weaker — tens of kJ/mol vs hundreds — but they are the reason water is liquid at room temperature, why geckos can walk on ceilings, and why DNA strands stay paired. There are three kinds, and every molecule experiences at least one.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Compare methane (CH₄, M_r = 16) and water (H₂O, M_r = 18) — similar masses. Methane boils at −161°C; water at +100°C. The 261°C difference is entirely due to intermolecular forces. Methane is non-polar — the only forces between its molecules are weak <strong>London dispersion forces</strong> (instantaneous dipoles from randomly shifting electrons). Water has <strong>hydrogen bonding</strong> — the strongest IMF — plus dipole-dipole forces and dispersion forces. The IMFs in water are orders of magnitude stronger than in methane. Compare the halogens: F₂ (gas, −188°C), Cl₂ (gas, −34°C), Br₂ (liquid, +59°C), I₂ (solid, +184°C). All non-polar, so only dispersion forces — but dispersion forces increase with the number of electrons. Iodine has 106 electrons; fluorine has 18. More electrons = larger instantaneous dipoles = stronger dispersion forces = higher boiling point.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The three types of <strong>intermolecular force</strong> (IMF), in order of increasing strength: (1) <strong>London dispersion forces</strong> (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole) — present between ALL molecules; increase with molecular size (more electrons). (2) <strong>Dipole–dipole forces</strong> — between polar molecules; permanent δ+ and δ− attract. (3) <strong>Hydrogen bonding</strong> — a special, strong dipole–dipole force when H is bonded to N, O, or F (the three most electronegative elements). IMF strength: ~1–40 kJ/mol. Compare: covalent bond ~150–800 kJ/mol. IMFs are weak per interaction but additive — in giant molecules (polymers, DNA), their sum is enormous.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>IMFs explain the physical properties of everything. Why does butter melt at ~35°C but diamond at ~3500°C? Butter molecules are held by weak van der Waals forces; diamond atoms are held by strong covalent bonds throughout. Why does a longer hydrocarbon chain have a higher boiling point? More electrons, stronger dispersion forces. Why does water bead on a waxed car? Water's strong hydrogen bonds keep water molecules together (cohesion); wax is non-polar and offers only weak dispersion forces (poor adhesion) — the water minimises contact and beads up. The living world runs on IMFs: protein folding, DNA base pairing, cell membrane formation — all governed by the interplay of these weak, additive, and exquisitely selective forces.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing showing three IMF types: (1) Two atoms with fluctuating electron clouds creating instantaneous dipoles — arrows showing induced attraction. (2) Two polar molecules with δ+ and δ− aligned — dipole-dipole. (3) Water molecules with dashed H-bonds between H(δ+) of one and O(δ−) of another. Below: relative strength scale — dispersion < dipole-dipole < H-bond << covalent bond. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-228 — Hydrogen bonding: the special force that defines life

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** hydrogen-bond; N-O-F; strongest-IMF; biological-significance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-227]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When hydrogen is covalently bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine — the three most electronegative elements — the bond is so polar that the hydrogen carries a strong δ+ charge. This δ+ hydrogen is then strongly attracted to a lone pair on an N, O, or F atom on a neighbouring molecule. This is a hydrogen bond — about 10 times stronger than ordinary dipole–dipole forces, but still about 10 times weaker than a covalent bond. It's the Goldilocks force: strong enough to matter, weak enough to break and re-form at biological temperatures.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Hydrogen bonds between water molecules: each water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds — two via its hydrogens (donating), two via its lone pairs (accepting). This tetrahedral network is why water has such a high specific heat capacity (absorbing heat breaks H-bonds rather than speeding up molecules), why ice floats (the H-bond network holds molecules further apart than in liquid water — ice is less dense), and why water has a high surface tension (H-bonds at the surface pull inward). In DNA, hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (A=T with two H-bonds, G≡C with three) hold the double helix together — strong enough for stability, weak enough to unzip for replication. In proteins, H-bonds between C=O and N-H groups along the backbone create the α-helix and β-sheet secondary structures.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>hydrogen bond</strong> is a strong intermolecular attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F — the donor) and a lone pair of electrons on another electronegative atom (the acceptor). H-bond energy: ~10–40 kJ/mol (compare: O-H covalent bond ~460 kJ/mol). Requirements: H must be bonded to N, O, or F; the acceptor must have a lone pair. H-bonds are directional (strongest when donor-H···acceptor are collinear) and specific. They can be intermolecular (between molecules — water, DNA base pairs) or intramolecular (within the same molecule — protein folding).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Without hydrogen bonding, water would boil at about −80°C — Earth's oceans would be gas. DNA would not form a stable double helix. Proteins would not fold into their functional shapes. Cellulose (plant fibre) and nylon derive their strength from hydrogen bonds between polymer chains. The reason your cotton T-shirt stays together is hydrogen bonding between cellulose chains. The reason a spider's silk is stronger than steel by weight involves hydrogen-bonded β-sheet nanocrystals. Hydrogen bonding is the most important non-covalent interaction in chemistry and biology — it's the scaffold on which the molecules of life are assembled.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of water molecules connected by dashed H-bonds — each O has two covalent bonds to H and two H-bonds to neighbouring molecules' H atoms. Below: DNA base pairs A=T (two H-bonds) and G≡C (three H-bonds). Water property table: high bp, high SHC, ice floats, high surface tension — all explained by H-bonding. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-229 — Why water is weird: hydrogen bonds explain everything

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** water-properties; anomalous-expansion; high-heat-capacity; universal-solvent | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-228]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Water is the most familiar substance on Earth, and also the strangest. It expands when it freezes (almost everything else contracts). It has an enormous heat capacity. Its solid form floats on its liquid form. It dissolves more substances than any other liquid. Every single one of these anomalies traces back to the same cause: the hydrogen-bonded network that gives water its structure and its stubbornness.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>The density anomaly: most liquids get denser as they cool — the molecules slow down and pack tighter. Water does too — until 4°C. Below 4°C, the hydrogen bonds begin forcing molecules into an open, hexagonal arrangement (the ice lattice). At 0°C, water freezes into this open structure — ice is about 9% less dense than liquid water, so it floats. If ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, killing all life. The high specific heat capacity (4.18 J/g·°C): to raise water's temperature, you must first break hydrogen bonds — the energy goes into restructuring the network, not just speeding up molecules. This makes water an excellent coolant (sweat, car radiators) and a climate moderator (oceans buffer temperature swings).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Water's anomalous properties arise from its extensive <strong>hydrogen-bonded network</strong>: (1) <strong>Maximum density at 4°C</strong> — below this, H-bond-directed open structure begins to dominate. (2) <strong>Solid less dense than liquid</strong> — ice floats. (3) <strong>High specific heat capacity</strong> — 4.18 J/g·°C, among the highest of any liquid. (4) <strong>High latent heat of vaporisation</strong> — 2260 J/g, making evaporative cooling highly effective. (5) <strong>High surface tension</strong> — H-bonds create a strong "skin." (6) <strong>Universal solvent</strong> — polar nature and H-bonding ability dissolve ionic and polar substances. All trace to the same molecular feature: two H-bond donors + two H-bond acceptors per molecule.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Water's weirdness is why life uses it. The floating ice insulates aquatic ecosystems through winter. The high heat capacity stabilises our body temperature and the Earth's climate. The high latent heat makes sweating an effective cooling mechanism — evaporating 1 g of water removes 2260 J of heat. The solvent power allows blood (mostly water) to carry nutrients, ions, and waste. The surface tension allows water to rise through plant xylem by capillary action. In the search for extraterrestrial life, "follow the water" is not a slogan — it's a recognition that water's unique hydrogen-bonding chemistry makes it almost irreplaceable as a medium for biochemistry.</p>

## BB-NEW-230 — Properties from structure: predicting how a substance will behave

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** bonding | **Concept:** structure-property-relationship; giant-vs-simple; bonding-type-decision-tree | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-223, BB-NEW-224, BB-NEW-227]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You're handed an unknown white solid. You've never seen it before. But you can predict its properties — melting point, conductivity, solubility, hardness — by answering one question: what kind of bonding and structure does it have? Ionic lattice, giant covalent, metallic, or simple molecular? The structure type plus a few rules gives you a complete property profile. No experiment required.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Four substances. Substance A: high melting point, conducts when molten but not solid, brittle, dissolves in water. → Ionic lattice (like NaCl — giant ionic, ions fixed when solid, free when molten/dissolved). Substance B: very high melting point, doesn't conduct, extremely hard, insoluble. → Giant covalent (like diamond — 3D network of strong covalent bonds, no free charges). Substance C: conducts as solid, malleable, shiny, high melting point. → Metallic (like copper — delocalised electrons, layers of cations can slide). Substance D: low melting point, doesn't conduct, soft, may dissolve in non-polar solvents. → Simple molecular (like wax — strong covalent bonds inside molecules, weak IMFs between molecules). The structure type is the master switch — flip it and every property changes together.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Structure–property relationship decision framework:</strong> (1) Identify the bonding type: metallic → metallic bonding. Non-metal + non-metal → covalent. Metal + non-metal → ionic. (2) Identify the structure: giant ionic, giant covalent, giant metallic, or simple molecular. (3) Predict properties: <strong>Melting point</strong> — giant >> simple (you're breaking strong bonds vs weak IMFs). <strong>Electrical conductivity</strong> — free charges (delocalised electrons or mobile ions) required. <strong>Hardness</strong> — giant structures are hard; simple molecular are soft. <strong>Solubility</strong> — ionic → polar solvents; covalent → non-polar solvents; metals and giant covalent → insoluble. (4) Exceptions exist (graphite conducts as a covalent structure), but the framework catches 95% of substances.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>This is the practical payoff of bonding theory. A materials engineer choosing a furnace lining needs a substance with high melting point and low conductivity — giant ionic (MgO). An electrical engineer needs a conductor that can be drawn into wire — metallic (Cu). A packaging designer wants something lightweight, flexible, and waterproof — simple molecular polymer. A jeweller wants hard, shiny, and corrosion-resistant — metallic (Au, Pt). Bonding theory is not abstract classification — it's the predictive toolkit that tells you what a material can and cannot do before you ever touch it.</p>

Four substance cards: A (NaCl — ionic lattice), B (diamond — giant covalent), C (Cu — metallic), D (wax — simple molecular). Each card lists: mp, conductivity, hardness, solubility. "Structure determines properties." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-231 — What is a chemical reaction?

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** chemical-reaction; reactants-products; bond-breaking-forming; conservation | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 33, BB-NEW-106]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A chemical reaction is not magic — it's rearrangement. Atoms don't appear or disappear. Bonds break and new bonds form, and the atoms that were in the reactants are all still there in the products, just connected differently. A reaction is a reshuffling of the same deck of atoms into a new hand.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Methane burns in oxygen: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Count atoms. Before: 1 C, 4 H, 4 O. After: 1 C (in CO₂), 4 H (in 2 H₂O), 4 O (2 in CO₂, 2 in 2 H₂O). Every atom is accounted for — nothing is lost, nothing is created. What happened: the C-H bonds in methane and the O=O bonds in oxygen broke; C=O bonds (in CO₂) and O-H bonds (in H₂O) formed. Energy was released because the bonds formed are stronger than the bonds broken (exothermic). The atoms didn't change identity — carbon stayed carbon, hydrogen stayed hydrogen. Only their partnerships changed. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms; nuclear reactions (next topic, not this one) change atoms into different elements.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>chemical reaction</strong> is a process in which one or more substances (reactants) are transformed into one or more different substances (products) through the breaking and forming of chemical bonds. Atoms are <strong>conserved</strong> — the number and type of atoms is the same before and after. Mass is conserved. A reaction is represented by a <strong>chemical equation</strong>: reactants → products. Evidence of a reaction: colour change, gas evolved, precipitate formed, temperature change, light emitted. Reactions can be classified by type (synthesis, decomposition, combustion, displacement, acid–base, redox) — covered in subsequent BBs.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The distinction between physical change and chemical reaction matters. Ice melting is physical — H₂O molecules are still H₂O, just reorganised. Electrolysis of water (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂) is chemical — the H₂O molecules are torn apart and reassembled into entirely different substances. Cooking an egg: chemical — the proteins denature and re-form new bonds; the change is irreversible. Dissolving salt in water: physical — the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are separated but unchanged; evaporate the water and you get the same salt back. The line: if new substances with new properties form, it's a chemical reaction. If the same substance just changes state or mixes, it's physical.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the methane combustion reaction: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Atom counts shown before and after. Bonds broken (4 C-H, 2 O=O) and bonds formed (2 C=O, 4 O-H) with energy changes labelled. "Atoms are conserved. Bonds rearrange. Energy is released or absorbed." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-232 — Balancing equations: atoms in must equal atoms out

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** balancing-equations; stoichiometric-coefficients; conservation-of-mass | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-231]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A chemical equation is a sentence in the language of chemistry. Like any sentence, it must be grammatically correct. The grammar is conservation of mass: the number of each type of atom on the left must equal the number on the right. If the atoms don't balance, the equation is wrong — no matter how plausible the products look. Balancing is the first skill of reaction chemistry. Without it, nothing else works.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Balance the combustion of propane: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Step 1 — carbon: 3 C on left → need 3 CO₂. C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + H₂O. Step 2 — hydrogen: 8 H → need 4 H₂O. C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Step 3 — oxygen: right side has 3×2 + 4×1 = 10 O atoms → need 5 O₂ on left. C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Check: C: 3=3 ✓, H: 8=8 ✓, O: 10=10 ✓. Balanced. The coefficients (1, 5, 3, 4) are the stoichiometric coefficients — they tell you the mole ratios: 1 mole of propane reacts with 5 moles of oxygen to produce 3 moles of CO₂ and 4 moles of H₂O.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>balanced chemical equation</strong> has equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides, satisfying the <strong>law of conservation of mass</strong>. Procedure: (1) Write the skeleton equation with correct formulas. (2) Balance elements one at a time using coefficients (whole numbers placed BEFORE formulas). (3) Start with elements that appear in only one reactant and one product. (4) Save elements that appear in multiple places (often O and H) for last. (5) Check all elements. (6) Reduce coefficients to the simplest whole-number ratio. Never change subscripts — that changes the compound's identity. Only adjust coefficients.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Balancing is not busywork — it's stoichiometry. A balanced equation gives the mole ratios, and from mole ratios come all quantitative predictions: how much product from how much reactant, which reactant runs out first (limiting reagent), what the yield should be, and what's left over. An unbalanced equation is worse than useless — it leads to wrong masses, wrong volumes, and in industrial chemistry, wrong orders of magnitude in cost and waste. Every stoichiometric calculation starts with a balanced equation. If the equation isn't balanced, stop. Nothing after it can be trusted.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of the step-by-step balancing of C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Step 1: balance C → 3CO₂. Step 2: balance H → 4H₂O. Step 3: balance O → 5O₂. Final equation with atom counts verified. Below: "Coefficients = mole ratios. Subscripts are NEVER changed." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-233 — Acids and bases: proton donors and acceptors

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** acids; bases; Brønsted-Lowry; proton-transfer | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-231]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor. A base is a proton acceptor. This is the Brønsted-Lowry definition, and it covers almost every acid–base reaction you'll encounter. HCl gas meets NH₃ gas and they react instantly to form NH₄Cl — a white smoke. HCl donated a proton to NH₃. That's the entire transaction. Acids give; bases receive. The proton is the currency.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Hydrochloric acid (HCl) in water: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). HCl donated a proton to water — HCl is the acid, H₂O is the base. The products: H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion) and Cl⁻. Ammonia in water: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). Ammonia accepted a proton from water — NH₃ is the base, H₂O is the acid this time. Water is amphoteric — it can act as both acid and base depending on what it meets. Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) donate protons completely — the reaction goes to completion. Weak acids (CH₃COOH, carbonic acid) donate only partially — an equilibrium mixture results.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Brønsted-Lowry theory:</strong> An <strong>acid</strong> is a proton (H⁺) donor. A <strong>base</strong> is a proton acceptor. Acid–base reactions are <strong>proton transfer</strong> reactions. When an acid loses a proton, it forms its <strong>conjugate base</strong> (HCl → Cl⁻). When a base gains a proton, it forms its <strong>conjugate acid</strong> (NH₃ → NH₄⁺). Strong acids have weak conjugate bases; weak acids have stronger conjugate bases. <strong>Common strong acids</strong>: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (first proton), HClO₄. <strong>Common strong bases</strong>: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂. <strong>Common weak acids</strong>: CH₃COOH (acetic), H₂CO₃ (carbonic), HF. <strong>Common weak bases</strong>: NH₃, amines.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The Brønsted-Lowry definition is broader than the older Arrhenius definition (which restricted acids to substances producing H⁺ in water and bases to substances producing OH⁻). Brønsted-Lowry works in any solvent, or even without solvent — HCl gas + NH₃ gas react directly in the air. It explains why salts can be acidic or basic: NH₄Cl dissolves to give NH₄⁺ (a weak acid — donates protons to water, making the solution acidic) and Cl⁻ (an extremely weak conjugate base — does nothing). It underpins buffer chemistry: a weak acid and its conjugate base resist pH changes by absorbing added H⁺ or OH⁻. Every biological fluid is buffered — blood pH is maintained at 7.40±0.05 by the H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ buffer system. The Brønsted-Lowry framework turns acid–base chemistry from a list of substances into a unified proton economy.</p>

## BB-NEW-234 — The pH scale and neutralisation

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** pH; hydrogen-ion-concentration; neutralisation; indicators | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-233]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The acidity of a solution is measured by its pH — a number from 0 (strongly acidic) to 14 (strongly alkaline), with 7 as neutral. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Each unit of pH represents a factor of 10 in hydrogen ion concentration. pH 3 is ten times more acidic than pH 4, a hundred times more than pH 5. The scale is logarithmic — because the range of [H⁺] in everyday solutions spans 14 orders of magnitude.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Stomach acid: pH ~1.5 ([H⁺] ≈ 0.03 mol/dm³). Lemon juice: pH ~2.5. Vinegar: pH ~3. Black coffee: pH ~5. Pure water: pH = 7 ([H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷). Blood: pH 7.4. Baking soda solution: pH ~8.5. Household ammonia: pH ~11.5. Drain cleaner: pH ~14 ([OH⁻] ≈ 1 mol/dm³). Neutralisation: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l). The H⁺ from the acid and OH⁻ from the base combine to form water. If exactly equal moles of acid and base are mixed, the resulting solution has pH 7 — neutral. The salt (NaCl) stays dissolved. The net ionic equation for ANY strong acid–strong base neutralisation is always the same: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]</strong>, where [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/dm³. For pure water at 25°C, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷, so pH = 7 — <strong>neutral</strong>. pH < 7: acidic. pH > 7: alkaline (basic). <strong>pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]</strong> and pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C). <strong>Neutralisation</strong> is the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water. The <strong>equivalence point</strong> is when exactly enough base has been added to neutralise all the acid (or vice versa). <strong>Indicators</strong> (litmus, phenolphthalein, universal indicator) change colour at specific pH ranges.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The logarithmic nature of pH is easy to misunderstand. A solution at pH 6 is not "slightly acidic compared to pH 7" — it has ten times the H⁺ concentration. A small pH shift in blood (from 7.4 to 7.2) is a medical emergency — it means [H⁺] has increased by ~60%, disrupting every pH-dependent enzyme in the body. In swimming pools, agriculture (soil pH), and food preservation, pH monitoring is constant. Neutralisation is the basis of antacids (CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ — the fizz), of treating acidic industrial waste, and of titrations — the lab technique that determines the concentration of an unknown acid or base by neutralising it with a known base or acid. The pH scale reduces an enormous concentration range to a simple 0–14 number — the logarithm at its most practical.</p>

The neutralisation reaction H⁺+OH⁻→H₂O in a box. Below: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; each unit = factor of 10. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.
