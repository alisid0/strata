## BB-NEW-235 — Types of reaction: the five patterns

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** reaction-types; synthesis; decomposition; combustion; displacement; precipitation | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-231]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Most chemical reactions fall into a handful of patterns. Recognise the pattern, and you can predict the products without memorising each reaction individually. Synthesis: A + B → AB. Decomposition: AB → A + B. Combustion: fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Displacement: A + BC → AC + B. Precipitation: two solutions mix → solid drops out. Five patterns cover the majority of reactions you'll encounter.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p><strong>Synthesis:</strong> 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s) — a brilliant white flame as magnesium ribbon burns. Two elements combine into one compound. <strong>Decomposition:</strong> CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) — limestone heated, carbon dioxide driven off, quicklime left behind. <strong>Combustion:</strong> C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g) — the gas in your stove, burning with a blue flame. Always O₂ in, CO₂ + H₂O out for hydrocarbons. <strong>Displacement:</strong> Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s) — grey zinc metal in blue copper sulphate solution; copper plates out as reddish-brown solid. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one. <strong>Precipitation:</strong> AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq) — two clear solutions mix and a white cloud of solid silver chloride appears. The solid that forms is the precipitate.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The five fundamental reaction types: (1) <strong>Synthesis</strong> (combination) — two or more substances form one product: A + B → AB. (2) <strong>Decomposition</strong> — one substance breaks into two or more: AB → A + B (often requires heat, light, or electricity). (3) <strong>Combustion</strong> — reaction with oxygen, producing oxides; hydrocarbon combustion always yields CO₂ + H₂O. (4) <strong>Single displacement</strong> — one element replaces another in a compound: A + BC → AC + B (driven by the reactivity series). (5) <strong>Double displacement</strong> (precipitation or neutralisation) — ions swap partners: AB + CD → AD + CB. Two additional overarching types — acid–base (proton transfer) and redox (electron transfer) — can overlap with these patterns.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Pattern recognition in reaction types saves enormous mental effort. Given reactants, identify the pattern → predict products → balance the equation. Rusting: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ — synthesis (and redox). Baking soda + vinegar: NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂ — double displacement (and acid–base). Bleaching a stain: NaClO → NaCl + [O] — decomposition. The reactivity series governs displacement: a metal higher in the series (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, Cu, Ag, Au) displaces any metal below it from its compounds. This is why zinc displaces copper but copper won't displace zinc — the series encodes the relative tendency to lose electrons, which is also the redox activity order.</p>

## BB-NEW-236 — Oxidation: losing electrons

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** oxidation; electron-loss; oxidising-agent; oxidation-in-organic | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-231, BB-NEW-104]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Historically, oxidation meant "reacting with oxygen." That's how the name stuck. But the modern definition is broader and more powerful: oxidation is the loss of electrons. Iron rusts (Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻) — the iron loses electrons to oxygen. Magnesium burns (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻) — the metal loses electrons. Any process where an atom, ion, or molecule gives up electrons is oxidation, whether oxygen is involved or not.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Place an iron nail in copper sulphate solution. The nail turns reddish-brown as copper metal plates onto it. What happened? Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ — the iron lost electrons (oxidised). Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) — the copper ions gained electrons (reduced). The iron atoms gave electrons to the copper ions — one substance was oxidised, the other reduced. Oxidation and reduction always happen together. In a blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. The iron in Fe₂O₃ goes from Fe³⁺ to Fe(0) — it gains electrons (reduced). The carbon in CO goes from C²⁺ to C⁴⁺ (in CO₂) — it loses electrons (oxidised). Carbon monoxide is the reducing agent; iron oxide is the oxidising agent.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Oxidation</strong> is the loss of electrons. The substance that loses electrons is <strong>oxidised</strong>. The substance that accepts those electrons is the <strong>oxidising agent</strong> (it causes oxidation and is itself reduced). In terms of oxidation number (see BB-NEW-239), oxidation is an increase in oxidation number. In organic chemistry, oxidation often involves gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen. The OIL RIG mnemonic: <strong>O</strong>xidation <strong>I</strong>s <strong>L</strong>oss of electrons; <strong>R</strong>eduction <strong>I</strong>s <strong>G</strong>ain of electrons.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Oxidation is everywhere — literally. Rusting (iron → iron oxide) costs the global economy billions per year in structural damage. The brown colour of a cut apple is enzymatic oxidation. Bleach works by oxidising coloured compounds to colourless ones. A car battery delivers power because lead is oxidised at one electrode (Pb → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻) while lead dioxide is reduced at the other. Respiration — the process powering your cells — is the controlled oxidation of glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. The oxygen you breathe is the terminal electron acceptor in a chain of redox reactions that powers every cell in your body. Oxidation is not a niche lab phenomenon — it is the chemistry of energy, decay, and life itself.</p>

Below: the blast furnace reaction Fe₂O₃+3CO→2Fe+3CO₂ with oxidation states labelled. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-237 — Reduction: gaining electrons

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** reduction; electron-gain; reducing-agent; reduction-in-organic | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-236]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Reduction is the gain of electrons. The name is historical — metal ores are often oxides, and "reducing" the ore to metal involves removing oxygen. Aluminium, for example, was once more precious than gold because reducing Al₂O₃ to Al metal was nearly impossible. Today, the Hall-Héroult process uses enormous electric currents to force the reduction — and aluminium is cheap enough for drink cans. Reduction is the chemical act of turning a compound back into its elemental form.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Aluminium extraction: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al(l) — each aluminium ion gains three electrons to become aluminium metal. The electrons are supplied by a massive direct current passed through molten aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite. In a hydrogen fuel cell: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O — oxygen is reduced to water, gaining electrons (and protons) at the cathode. In photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ — carbon in CO₂ (oxidation state +4) is reduced to carbon in glucose (average oxidation state 0). The carbon gains electrons. Photosynthesis is a massive reduction process powered by sunlight.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Reduction</strong> is the gain of electrons. The substance that gains electrons is <strong>reduced</strong>. The substance that supplies those electrons is the <strong>reducing agent</strong> (it causes reduction and is itself oxidised). In terms of oxidation number, reduction is a decrease in oxidation number. In organic chemistry, reduction often involves gaining hydrogen or losing oxygen. Metals are extracted from their ores by reduction — the metal ion gains electrons to become the neutral metal. Common reducing agents: carbon (in blast furnaces), hydrogen, reactive metals (Na, Mg, Al), and electrons themselves (in electrolysis).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Reduction is the chemical basis of metallurgy — every metal above gold in the reactivity series exists in nature as a compound and must be reduced to its elemental form. The Iron Age began when humans learned to reduce iron oxide with charcoal (carbon). The Hall-Héroult process (1886) made aluminium an industrial material instead of a scientific curiosity. In biology, reduction is how energy is stored: NAD⁺ → NADH is a reduction, and NADH carries those high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain. Reduction and oxidation are not two topics — they are two halves of every redox reaction, and you cannot have one without the other.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of an electrolytic cell for aluminium: Al³⁺+3e⁻→Al at the cathode (reduction, labelled RIG), 2O²⁻→O₂+4e⁻ at the anode (oxidation, labelled OIL). Molten aluminium collects at the bottom. Below: "Reduction = gain of electrons. The metal ion becomes the metal." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-238 — Redox: oxidation and reduction are one story

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** redox; electron-transfer; oxidising-agent; reducing-agent | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-236, BB-NEW-237]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You can't have oxidation without reduction. Electrons don't vanish — when one substance loses them, another must gain them. A redox reaction is the full story: who gave, who received, and how many electrons changed hands. The substance that is oxidised is the reducing agent. The substance that is reduced is the oxidising agent. The terminology sounds backwards, but once you see the transaction, it's clear: the reducing agent reduces the other by giving electrons; the oxidising agent oxidises the other by taking electrons.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). Zinc loses 2 electrons — oxidised. Copper ions gain 2 electrons — reduced. Zinc is the <strong>reducing agent</strong> (it gave electrons, causing reduction of Cu²⁺). Cu²⁺ is the <strong>oxidising agent</strong> (it accepted electrons, causing oxidation of Zn). The electron transfer is direct — zinc atoms touch copper ions and hand over electrons. Now the thermite reaction: 2Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → Al₂O₃(s) + 2Fe(l). Aluminium loses electrons (Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻) — oxidised. Iron gains electrons (Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Fe) — reduced. Al is the reducing agent; Fe₂O₃ is the oxidising agent. The reaction is so exothermic it produces molten iron — used to weld railway tracks.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>redox</strong> (reduction–oxidation) reaction involves the transfer of electrons from one species to another. The species that loses electrons is <strong>oxidised</strong> and acts as the <strong>reducing agent</strong>. The species that gains electrons is <strong>reduced</strong> and acts as the <strong>oxidising agent</strong>. The total number of electrons lost equals the total number gained. A redox reaction can be split into two <strong>half-reactions</strong>: oxidation half (electrons on the right) and reduction half (electrons on the left). A <strong>disproportionation</strong> reaction is a redox where the same element is both oxidised and reduced (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ — oxygen goes from −1 to −2 and 0).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Redox is the unifying framework for an enormous range of chemistry. Rusting: Fe → Fe₂O₃ (iron oxidised by O₂). Bleaching: coloured compounds oxidised to colourless ones. Batteries: redox reactions separated into two half-cells, electron flow through an external circuit. Electroplating: metal ions reduced onto a surface. Respiration: glucose oxidised. Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide reduced. Metabolism: thousands of coupled redox reactions, each enzyme catalysing one electron transfer step in a chain. The ubiquity of redox is why the OIL RIG mnemonic is worth memorising — it opens the door to understanding energy in chemistry, biology, and industry.</p>

## BB-NEW-239 — Oxidation numbers: keeping score in the electron game

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** oxidation-number; oxidation-state; rules; identifying-redox | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-238]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In a redox reaction, electrons move. But in a covalent molecule like CO₂, no electron is fully transferred — they're shared. How do you tell who's oxidised and who's reduced when there's no obvious electron transfer? You assign oxidation numbers — a bookkeeping score that pretends all bonds are ionic. If the number goes up, it's oxidation. If it goes down, it's reduction. The rules are simple, and they turn every reaction into a scoreable game.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>In MnO₄⁻ (permanganate ion): oxygen is −2 (rule: oxygen is −2 except in peroxides). Four oxygens = −8 total. The ion's charge is −1. So Mn + (−8) = −1 → Mn = +7. In SO₄²⁻: four oxygens = −8. S + (−8) = −2 → S = +6. Now the reaction: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O. Mn goes from +7 to +2 — decrease of 5 → reduction. Fe goes from +2 to +3 — increase of 1 → oxidation. Five Fe²⁺ are oxidised (total electron loss = 5) to match one MnO₄⁻ being reduced (electron gain = 5). The oxidation numbers balance — as they must. This is how you identify redox reactions when no isolated charges are visible.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Oxidation number</strong> (oxidation state) is the hypothetical charge an atom would have if all bonds were fully ionic. Rules (in order of priority): (1) Free element = 0 (Na, O₂, Fe). (2) Simple ion = charge (Na⁺ = +1, Cl⁻ = −1). (3) Fluorine in compounds = −1. (4) Hydrogen in compounds = +1 (except metal hydrides = −1). (5) Oxygen in compounds = −2 (except peroxides = −1, OF₂ = +2). (6) Group 1 metals = +1, Group 2 = +2. (7) Sum of oxidation numbers = overall charge of species. In a redox reaction, the element whose oxidation number increases is <strong>oxidised</strong>; the element whose oxidation number decreases is <strong>reduced</strong>.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Oxidation numbers reveal hidden redox reactions. Is this a redox? CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Oxidation numbers: Ca = +2 throughout, C = +4 throughout, O = −2 throughout — no changes. This is NOT a redox; it's a decomposition. Is this a redox? 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. H goes from 0 to +1 (oxidised); O goes from 0 to −2 (reduced). Yes — redox. Oxidation numbers are the universal redox test. They also allow you to balance complex redox equations (like permanganate oxidising iron) by balancing the electron gain and loss before adding H⁺/OH⁻ and H₂O. Titration calculations in analytical chemistry depend entirely on oxidation number changes to determine the equivalence point.</p>

Below: MnO₄⁻ reduced to Mn²⁺ — Mn goes from +7 to +2, shown on a number line with a downward arrow (reduction = decrease). Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ — Fe goes from +2 to +3, upward arrow (oxidation = increase). Electrons transferred: 5e⁻ balanced. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-240 — Half-equations and electrochemistry: redox with a circuit

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** reactions | **Concept:** half-equations; electrochemical-cell; electrolysis; applications | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-238, BB-NEW-239, BB-NEW-202]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A redox reaction can be physically split in two. Put the oxidation in one beaker, the reduction in another, connect them with a wire and a salt bridge, and electrons flow through the wire — an electric current. This is an electrochemical cell. Reverse it — force current through a cell — and you drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction: electrolysis. Half-equations are the language for both. Each half tells one side of the electron story.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A zinc–copper cell (the Daniell cell): Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ — oxidation at the anode (negative terminal). Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) — reduction at the cathode (positive terminal). Electrons flow through the external wire from Zn to Cu. The salt bridge (filter paper soaked in KNO₃) allows ions to migrate, completing the circuit. Cell voltage ≈ 1.1 V. For electrolysis of molten NaCl: at the anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻ (oxidation, green chlorine gas bubbles off). At the cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na(l) (reduction, molten sodium metal collects). This is how sodium metal and chlorine gas are manufactured — the same reaction that gives table salt, but in reverse, driven by electricity. Electrolysis of brine (NaCl solution) gives different products: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ at anode; 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ at cathode (water is reduced instead of Na⁺ because H₂O is more easily reduced).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>half-equation</strong> shows either the oxidation or reduction part of a redox reaction, with electrons explicitly shown. Oxidation half: electrons on the right (lost). Reduction half: electrons on the left (gained). Multiplying half-equations so electron counts match and adding them gives the full redox equation. An <strong>electrochemical cell</strong> (galvanic/voltaic cell) converts chemical energy to electrical energy by separating the half-reactions. <strong>Electrolysis</strong> uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. <strong>Anode</strong> = where oxidation occurs. <strong>Cathode</strong> = where reduction occurs (mnemonic: AN OX, RED CAT).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Electrochemistry is the bridge between chemistry and electricity. Every battery — from a 1.5 V AA to a lithium-ion pack powering a phone — is an electrochemical cell. The voltage depends on which two half-reactions you pair. Electrolysis produces aluminium (Hall-Héroult), chlorine and sodium hydroxide (chlor-alkali process), and pure copper (electrorefining). Electroplating deposits a thin layer of metal (silver on cutlery, chromium on car parts). In a hydrogen fuel cell, H₂ is oxidised at the anode and O₂ is reduced at the cathode — the only product is water. Electrochemistry is not a niche — it's the industrial backbone of the metal, chemical, and energy-storage industries.</p>

Wire connects electrodes, salt bridge connects solutions. Electron flow labelled. Half-equations written beside each beaker. Below: electrolysis of NaCl with products labelled. ## BB-NEW-241 — Exothermic and endothermic: energy out or energy in

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** energetics | **Concept:** exothermic; endothermic; energy-profile; bond-making-breaking | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-231, BB-NEW-106]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Some reactions release energy — they get hot. These are exothermic. Others absorb energy — they get cold. These are endothermic. The difference is whether the bonds being formed are stronger (exothermic — the extra energy is released as heat) or weaker (endothermic — energy must be absorbed from the surroundings to break stronger bonds and form weaker ones). Every reaction has an energy signature. The sign tells you whether the reaction is a heat source or a heat sink.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Burning methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. The bonds formed (2 C=O at ~800 kJ/mol each, 4 O-H at ~460 each) are collectively stronger than the bonds broken (4 C-H at ~410 each, 2 O=O at ~498 each). The excess energy — about 890 kJ per mole of methane — is released as heat. The flame is hot. This is exothermic. Now dissolve ammonium nitrate in water: NH₄NO₃(s) → NH₄⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq). Breaking the ionic lattice absorbs energy; hydrating the ions releases energy — but the net is absorption. The beaker gets cold. This is endothermic — the reaction pulls heat from the water. Cold packs use this: ammonium nitrate and water in separate compartments; break the seal, they mix, and the pack turns icy cold.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>An <strong>exothermic</strong> reaction releases energy to the surroundings (ΔH < 0). The products have less enthalpy than the reactants. The surroundings get warmer. Examples: combustion, neutralisation, respiration, thermite reaction. An <strong>endothermic</strong> reaction absorbs energy from the surroundings (ΔH > 0). The products have more enthalpy than the reactants. The surroundings get colder. Examples: photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate, thermal decomposition of limestone. In an <strong>energy profile diagram</strong>, exothermic reactions show products lower than reactants (energy released); endothermic show products higher (energy absorbed). The difference is the <strong>enthalpy change</strong> ΔH.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The exo/endo distinction determines how reactions are used. Exothermic reactions are energy sources — fuels, explosives, heat packs. Endothermic reactions are energy sinks — cold packs, cooling systems, and the most important endothermic reaction on Earth: photosynthesis, which absorbs 2800 kJ of sunlight per mole of glucose produced. A reaction that is spontaneous at one temperature can become non-spontaneous at another — the sign of ΔH alone doesn't tell you spontaneity (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, the Gibbs free energy, decides that). But the exo/endo classification is the first cut — it tells you which direction the heat flows, and whether you need to supply energy or can extract it.</p>

Right: endothermic — reactants lower, products higher, ΔH positive, arrow up. Below: methane combustion (exothermic, −890 kJ/mol) and NH₄NO₃ dissolution (endothermic, +25 kJ/mol). ## BB-NEW-242 — Enthalpy: measuring the heat of reaction

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** energetics | **Concept:** enthalpy; ΔH; standard-conditions; calorimetry | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-241]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Enthalpy (H) is the total heat content of a chemical system. You can't measure H directly — but you CAN measure the change in H, ΔH, when a reaction occurs. ΔH is the heat absorbed or released at constant pressure — the number you get from a calorimetry experiment. A negative ΔH means the reaction released heat (exothermic). A positive ΔH means it absorbed heat (endothermic). The magnitude tells you how much.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Measure the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol. Burn 0.46 g of ethanol (0.01 mol) in a spirit burner under a copper calorimeter containing 200 g of water. The water temperature rises from 20.0°C to 34.3°C. Energy absorbed by water: q = mcΔT = 200 × 4.18 × 14.3 = 11,960 J ≈ 12.0 kJ. This came from 0.01 mol ethanol. So ΔH_combustion ≈ −1200 kJ/mol (actual value: −1367 kJ/mol — the difference is heat loss to the surroundings). The negative sign means exothermic. In a neutralisation: 50 cm³ of 1 M HCl + 50 cm³ of 1 M NaOH. Temperature rises by 6.8°C. Total solution mass ≈ 100 g. q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.8 = 2842 J. Moles of water formed = 0.05. ΔH_neut ≈ −2842/0.05 = −56,800 J/mol ≈ −57 kJ/mol. Strong acid–strong base neutralisation always gives about −57 kJ per mole of water formed — because the net reaction is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Enthalpy change</strong> ΔH is the heat absorbed or released by a reaction at constant pressure, measured in kJ/mol. Standard enthalpy changes (ΔH°) are measured under <strong>standard conditions</strong>: 298 K (25°C), 100 kPa (1 atm), all solutions at 1 mol/dm³. Types: ΔH_combustion (burning 1 mole in excess O₂), ΔH_neutralisation (acid + base → 1 mol H₂O), ΔH_formation (forming 1 mole of compound from its elements in standard states), ΔH_reaction (any reaction as written). <strong>Calorimetry</strong> measures ΔH using q = mcΔT, where q = heat, m = mass, c = specific heat capacity, ΔT = temperature change. Divide q by moles reacted to get ΔH in kJ/mol.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Enthalpy data is the bedrock of chemical engineering. The ΔH_combustion of a fuel determines its value as an energy source. Methane: −890 kJ/mol. Hydrogen: −286 kJ/mol. Octane (petrol): −5470 kJ/mol. Per gram, hydrogen delivers about 2.5× the energy of petrol — which is why it's attractive as a fuel, and why rockets use liquid hydrogen. ΔH_formation data (tabulated for thousands of compounds) lets you calculate ΔH for ANY reaction using Hess's Law — you don't need to measure every reaction directly. The enthalpy of a reaction is a single number that encodes the net energy consequence of breaking all the reactant bonds and forming all the product bonds.</p>

Below: standard enthalpy definitions, each with an example reaction. "Negative ΔH = exothermic. Positive ΔH = endothermic." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-243 — Hess's Law: the energy route doesn't matter

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** energetics | **Concept:** Hesss-law; enthalpy-cycle; indirect-determination | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-242]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You want to know the enthalpy change for a reaction you can't measure directly — maybe it's too slow, or side reactions interfere. Hess's Law says: it doesn't matter. The overall enthalpy change depends only on the initial and final states, not the route. If you can find a path from reactants to products using reactions you DO know, just add up their ΔH values. The answer is the same as if you'd measured the direct reaction.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Find ΔH for: C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g) — the incomplete combustion of carbon. You can't easily stop carbon burning at CO; it tends to go all the way to CO₂. But you can measure two reactions: (1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₁ = −394 kJ/mol. (2) CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₂ = −283 kJ/mol. Hess's Law: the path C → CO₂ (direct, route 1) is equivalent to C → CO → CO₂ (route A then route 2 reversed). So: ΔH₁ = ΔH_A + ΔH₂ → −394 = ΔH_A + (−283) → ΔH_A = −111 kJ/mol. The enthalpy of formation of CO is −111 kJ/mol — found without ever isolating CO in a calorimeter. The cycle: C + ½O₂ → CO (wanted, ΔH_A). C + O₂ → CO₂ (measured, −394). CO + ½O₂ → CO₂ (measured, −283). Arrow manipulation: ΔH_A = −394 − (−283) = −111.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Hess's Law</strong> states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken — it depends only on the initial and final states. This is a direct consequence of enthalpy being a <strong>state function</strong>. Practical application: construct an <strong>enthalpy cycle</strong> (often a triangle or Born–Haber cycle), write known ΔH values on the arrows, and solve for the unknown. Alternative routes must sum to the same total. This works because energy is conserved — the net energy change around any closed cycle must be zero. Hess's Law allows calculation of ΔH for reactions that are impossible or impractical to measure directly.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Hess's Law is the accounting system of thermochemistry. The standard enthalpy of formation ΔH_f° of every compound is tabulated. For any reaction: ΔH° = Σ(ΔH_f° of products) − Σ(ΔH_f° of reactants) — multiply each by its stoichiometric coefficient. This means you can calculate ΔH for millions of hypothetical reactions from a database of a few thousand formation enthalpies. Born–Haber cycles use Hess's Law to calculate lattice energies — the enthalpy change when gaseous ions form a solid lattice — which cannot be measured directly. Every ΔH value you see quoted for a reaction that wasn't directly measured was found using Hess's Law. The cycle is the tool; the conservation of energy is the guarantee.</p>

## BB-NEW-244 — Bond enthalpies: breaking costs, making pays

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** energetics | **Concept:** bond-enthalpy; bond-breaking-endothermic; bond-making-exothermic; mean-bond-energy | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-241, BB-NEW-108]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every chemical bond has a price — the bond enthalpy, measured in kJ/mol. Breaking a bond always costs energy (endothermic). Making a bond always releases energy (exothermic). The net ΔH of a reaction is the sum: energy to break all reactant bonds minus energy released making all product bonds. If the products have stronger bonds overall, the reaction is exothermic. If they have weaker bonds, it's endothermic. Bond enthalpies reduce every reaction to an energy balance sheet.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Estimate ΔH for: H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g). Bonds broken: 1 H-H (436 kJ) + 1 Cl-Cl (243 kJ) = 679 kJ absorbed. Bonds formed: 2 H-Cl (2 × 431 = 862 kJ) released. Net: ΔH ≈ +679 − 862 = −183 kJ/mol (per mole of reaction as written — producing 2 HCl). The actual value is −184 kJ/mol — bond enthalpy estimates are remarkably close. For methane combustion: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Bonds broken: 4 C-H (4×413=1652) + 2 O=O (2×498=996) = 2648 kJ. Bonds formed: 2 C=O (2×799=1598) + 4 O-H (4×463=1852) = 3450 kJ. Net: +2648 − 3450 = −802 kJ (actual: −890 kJ). The estimate is slightly off because bond enthalpies are averages — the C-H bond in methane isn't exactly the same as C-H in other molecules. But the estimate is within 10%.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Bond enthalpy</strong> (bond dissociation energy) is the energy required to break one mole of a specific bond in the gaseous state. <strong>Mean bond enthalpy</strong> is the average value for a given bond type across many compounds. Key values (kJ/mol): H-H 436, C-H 413, O-H 463, C-C 347, C=C 614, C≡C 839, O=O 498, C=O 799, Cl-Cl 243, H-Cl 431. ΔH_reaction ≈ Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed). Breaking bonds = endothermic (+). Making bonds = exothermic (−). This is an estimation method — it assumes all bonds of a given type are identical, which they aren't.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Bond enthalpies explain why some substances are stable and others aren't. N₂ has an N≡N triple bond (944 kJ/mol) — one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. This is why nitrogen gas is almost inert, and why explosives containing nitrogen (TNT, nitroglycerine) release so much energy when the stable N₂ triple bond forms. The weakness of the O-O single bond (146 kJ/mol) in peroxides makes them reactive bleaching and disinfecting agents. The strength of the C-F bond (485 kJ/mol) makes PTFE (Teflon) incredibly resistant to chemical attack. Bond enthalpies turn the qualitative idea of "strong bonds" and "weak bonds" into numbers — and those numbers predict reactivity, stability, and energy output.</p>

The balance: ΔH = 679−862 = −183 kJ/mol. Below: a mini table of common bond enthalpies. "Breaking = costs energy. Making = releases energy. Net = making minus breaking." Chalk on dark green board. ## BB-NEW-245 — Activation energy: the barrier every reaction must cross

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** energetics | **Concept:** activation-energy; Ea; transition-state; energy-barrier | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-241]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Petrol doesn't spontaneously ignite at room temperature — even though its combustion is highly exothermic. Why? Because before bonds can form, existing bonds must begin to break. This takes energy — the activation energy, E_a. It's the hump in the energy profile that reactants must climb before they can slide down to products. A reaction can be thermodynamically favoured (negative ΔH) but kinetically frozen (high E_a). The match provides the spark; the spark provides the E_a.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature sits unchanged for years. Apply a spark — the spark provides the activation energy to break a few H-H and O=O bonds. Those atoms recombine, releasing far more energy than the spark supplied. That energy provides E_a for neighbouring molecules, which react and release more energy — a chain reaction. The mixture explodes. The overall ΔH is −286 kJ/mol (exothermic), but the E_a is around 200 kJ/mol. Without that initial 200 kJ push, nothing happens. Catalysts work by providing an alternative pathway with lower E_a. A catalytic converter uses platinum to lower the E_a for 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ and 2NO → N₂ + O₂, allowing these reactions to proceed at exhaust temperatures instead of requiring a flame.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Activation energy</strong> E_a is the minimum energy colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur. On an energy profile diagram, it's the difference between the reactants' energy and the <strong>transition state</strong> (activated complex) — the highest-energy arrangement along the reaction path. E_a is always positive (even for exothermic reactions). It is measured in kJ/mol. The <strong>Arrhenius equation</strong> relates E_a to the rate constant: k = A e^(−E_a/RT). High E_a → slow reaction at a given temperature. Low E_a → fast reaction. A <strong>catalyst</strong> lowers E_a without being consumed, providing an alternative mechanism.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Activation energy is why the world isn't on fire. Thermodynamics says a mixture of oxygen and almost anything organic should react exothermically — but kinetics, governed by E_a, keeps those reactions frozen until triggered. It's why food doesn't spontaneously combust in your cupboard. It's why diamonds don't turn into graphite (the thermodynamically stable form) at room temperature — the E_a for rearranging all those strong C-C bonds is enormous. It's why enzymes are essential for life — biochemical reactions that would take millennia at body temperature happen in milliseconds because enzymes lower the E_a by factors of a million or more. E_a is nature's safety catch: it separates the thermodynamically possible from the kinetically actual.</p>

The transition state at the peak. Two curves: uncatalysed (high E_a) and catalysed (lower E_a, same ΔH). Spark → H₂+O₂ → explosion labelled with "spark provides E_a." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-246 — Reaction rate: how fast?

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** kinetics | **Concept:** reaction-rate; rate-equation; monitoring; gradient | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-231, BB-NEW-245]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Some reactions are over in microseconds (an explosion). Others take millennia (diamond → graphite). The rate of a reaction measures how quickly reactants disappear or products appear. Rate = change in concentration ÷ time. It's the speedometer of chemistry. And unlike a car's speed, a reaction's rate is usually fastest at the start (when concentrations are highest) and slows as reactants are consumed.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Marble chips (CaCO₃) react with hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Monitor the rate by measuring the volume of CO₂ gas evolved every 30 seconds. At t=0: 0 cm³. At t=30s: 24 cm³. At t=60s: 40 cm³. At t=90s: 50 cm³. At t=120s: 55 cm³. The rate is fastest at the start (0–30s: 24/30 = 0.8 cm³/s) and slows down (90–120s: 5/30 = 0.17 cm³/s) as the acid is consumed and the marble chips shrink. The average rate over the whole reaction is total volume/total time. The instantaneous rate at any moment is the gradient of the tangent to the volume–time curve at that point. At t=0, the initial rate is the steepest tangent.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>rate of reaction</strong> is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time: rate = −Δ[reactant]/Δt = +Δ[product]/Δt (units: mol/dm³/s). Rate can be followed by monitoring: gas volume, mass loss, colour change (using a colorimeter), pH change, or conductivity. The <strong>rate equation</strong> (rate law) expresses rate in terms of concentrations: rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where k is the <strong>rate constant</strong>, and m and n are the <strong>orders</strong> of reaction with respect to A and B (determined experimentally, not from the stoichiometric coefficients). The <strong>overall order</strong> = m + n.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Reaction rate is not a single number — it's a function of concentration. The initial rate is often quoted for comparison. Rate data is analysed graphically: a straight line when concentration is plotted against time means zero order (rate is constant, independent of concentration). A constant half-life means first order (exponential decay of reactant). Plotting 1/[A] against time giving a straight line means second order. These graphical methods reveal the rate law, which in turn reveals the mechanism — the sequence of elementary steps by which the reaction actually occurs. The stoichiometric equation tells you what reacts and what's produced; the rate law tells you HOW it happens, which molecules collide in which step, and which step is the bottleneck.</p>

Below: rate = −Δ[R]/Δt = +Δ[P]/Δt. Three graphs showing zero order ([A] vs t — straight line), first order (ln[A] vs t — straight line), second order (1/[A] vs t — straight line). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-247 — Factors affecting rate: temperature, concentration, surface area

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** kinetics | **Concept:** collision-theory; rate-factors; temperature; concentration; surface-area | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-246, BB-NEW-245]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>For a reaction to happen, particles must collide with enough energy (≥E_a) and the right orientation. Anything that increases the frequency of successful collisions increases the rate. Three factors do this: higher temperature (faster particles, more with E≥E_a), higher concentration (more particles in the same volume = more collisions), and greater surface area (more exposed particles available to collide). These are the three levers of chemical kinetics.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p><strong>Temperature:</strong> The reaction between sodium thiosulphate and HCl produces a sulphur precipitate that turns the solution cloudy. At 20°C, the cross drawn on paper beneath the flask disappears in 60 seconds. At 40°C: 15 seconds. At 60°C: 4 seconds. A 10°C rise roughly doubles the rate for many reactions at room temperature. <strong>Concentration:</strong> Marble chips with 2 M HCl produce CO₂ twice as fast as with 1 M HCl — more acid particles per unit volume means more frequent collisions with the marble surface. <strong>Surface area:</strong> A large marble chip takes 5 minutes to fully react. The same mass of powdered marble reacts in 30 seconds — the powder has enormously more surface area exposed to the acid. A spark can ignite flour dust in air, causing a devastating explosion — the fine powder has huge surface area for oxidation.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Collision theory</strong> states that for a reaction to occur, particles must collide with energy ≥ E_a and with the correct orientation. Rate ∝ (collision frequency) × (fraction with E ≥ E_a). The three controllable factors: (1) <strong>Temperature</strong> — increases the average kinetic energy of particles, so more collisions exceed E_a; also increases collision frequency slightly. (2) <strong>Concentration</strong> (or pressure for gases) — more particles per unit volume, more collisions per second. (3) <strong>Surface area</strong> — for solids, only surface particles can react; powdering increases the surface-to-volume ratio enormously. A <strong>catalyst</strong> (see BB-NEW-249) provides an alternative pathway with lower E_a.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Kinetic control is everywhere in industry and daily life. Refrigerators slow food spoilage (lower T means slower reaction rates for bacterial metabolism). Pressure cookers raise the boiling point of water, increasing the temperature and dramatically speeding cooking. Catalytic converters use enormous surface area (honeycomb structure coated with platinum/rhodium nanoparticles) to maximise contact between exhaust gases and catalyst. In a flour mill or coal mine, dust explosions are a constant hazard — controlled by ventilation (reducing concentration) and avoiding sparks (ignition source). The same principles govern drug dosing: concentration in the bloodstream must stay in the therapeutic window — too low (no effect), too high (toxic). Kinetics determines the dosing interval.</p>

(2) Concentration — two beakers, 1M (few particles, slow) vs 2M (many particles, fast). (3) Surface area — large chip (slow) vs powder (fast). ## BB-NEW-248 — The Boltzmann distribution: who has enough energy?

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** kinetics | **Concept:** Boltzmann-distribution; Maxwell-Boltzmann; energy-distribution; temperature-effect | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-245, BB-NEW-247]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In a gas or liquid, not all particles have the same energy. Some are slow, some are fast, most are somewhere in the middle. The distribution of energies is given by the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve — a skewed bell shape that tails off to the right. Only the particles in the high-energy tail (those with E ≥ E_a) can react. The area under the tail is tiny at low temperature. Raise the temperature, and the whole curve flattens and shifts right — the tail area grows enormously. This is why a small temperature rise can double the rate.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>At 298 K (25°C), the fraction of molecules with E ≥ 50 kJ/mol in a typical gas is very small — perhaps 1 in 10⁹. Raise the temperature to 308 K (35°C) — only a 10°C increase. The average kinetic energy increases by just 3.3% (proportional to T). But the fraction with E ≥ 50 kJ/mol roughly <strong>doubles</strong>. The rate doubles because the number of successful collisions doubles — even though the total number of collisions barely changes. The high-energy tail is exquisitely sensitive to temperature. At 600 K, a much larger fraction crosses the barrier. At 1000 K, most molecules do. The Boltzmann distribution explains why a match lights a fire: the match raises a tiny region to ~1000 K, enough molecules in that region cross E_a, the reaction releases heat, neighbouring molecules are heated, and the reaction propagates.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution</strong> describes the distribution of kinetic energies among molecules in a gas at a given temperature. Key features: (1) No molecules have zero energy. (2) The curve passes through a maximum (most probable energy). (3) Mean energy > most probable energy. (4) The curve is asymmetric with a long tail to the right. (5) The area under the curve = total number of molecules. (6) The area under the tail beyond E_a = number of molecules with enough energy to react. (7) Increasing temperature flattens the curve, shifts the peak right, and dramatically increases the tail area beyond E_a. The distribution applies rigorously to ideal gases but qualitatively to liquids and solutions as well.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The Boltzmann distribution is the statistical foundation of chemical kinetics. It explains why reaction rates increase exponentially with temperature (the Arrhenius equation, k = Ae^(−E_a/RT), falls out of the fraction of molecules with E ≥ E_a). It explains why catalysts are so effective — lowering E_a from 100 to 50 kJ/mol can increase the reactive fraction by a factor of millions at room temperature. It explains why enzymes have optimal temperature ranges — too cold and the tail is too small; too hot and the enzyme denatures (unfolds). It explains why a fever of just 2°C above normal speeds up immune responses — and why a fever of 5°C above normal can denature proteins and become life-threatening. The Boltzmann distribution turns the qualitative observation "heat speeds reactions" into a quantitative, predictive tool.</p>

E_a marked as a vertical line on the right. The tail area beyond E_a is shaded for both curves — much larger for T₂. Below: "At higher T, the tail beyond E_a is dramatically larger — rate increases." ## BB-NEW-249 — Catalysts: lowering the barrier without being consumed

**Subject:** chemistry | **Topic:** kinetics | **Concept:** catalysts; activation-energy; enzyme; heterogeneous-catalysis | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-245, BB-NEW-247]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up. It does this by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. More molecules can cross the lower barrier at the same temperature — the rate increases, sometimes by factors of millions. The catalyst emerges unchanged at the end, ready to catalyse another reaction cycle. It's the chemical equivalent of a facilitator — it lowers the entry fee but doesn't pay it.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). Uncatalysed, this is slow — a bottle of H₂O₂ lasts for months. Add a pinch of manganese dioxide (MnO₂) powder and the solution erupts in a fountain of oxygen. The MnO₂ provides a surface where H₂O₂ molecules adsorb, bonds weaken, and the reaction occurs with a much lower E_a. Filter the MnO₂ out afterward and it's chemically unchanged — the same mass, the same composition. In the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃), an iron catalyst lowers the temperature needed from ~1000°C to ~450°C, saving enormous energy and making the process economically viable — producing the ammonia that feeds half the world's population through fertilisers. Without the iron catalyst, the world's food supply would be a fraction of what it is.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>catalyst</strong> is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, without being permanently consumed. <strong>Heterogeneous catalysis</strong>: catalyst is in a different phase from reactants (typically solid catalyst, gaseous/liquid reactants) — e.g., iron in Haber process, platinum in catalytic converters, MnO₂ in H₂O₂ decomposition. <strong>Homogeneous catalysis</strong>: catalyst is in the same phase — e.g., acid-catalysed ester hydrolysis. <strong>Enzymes</strong> are biological catalysts — proteins with active sites that bind specific substrates with exquisite selectivity. A catalyst does NOT change ΔH, equilibrium position, or the yield — it only changes the rate. It lowers E_a for BOTH forward and reverse reactions equally.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Catalysis is one of the most economically important fields in chemistry. The Haber–Bosch process (iron catalyst) produces ~180 million tonnes of ammonia annually. Catalytic converters (Pt, Rh, Pd) clean car exhaust. Ziegler–Natta catalysts (titanium compounds) produce polyethylene and polypropylene — the plastics of modern life. Enzymes catalyse every biochemical reaction in your body: carbonic anhydrase speeds up CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ by a factor of ~10⁷, essential for respiration. Catalysts do not violate thermodynamics — they cannot make an endergonic reaction (ΔG > 0) occur spontaneously. They can only accelerate reactions that are already thermodynamically favoured. But that acceleration, from geological to industrial timescales, is what makes modern chemistry possible.</p>

ΔH unchanged. Below: the H₂O₂ decomposition — MnO₂ powder added, oxygen gas evolving vigorously. "Catalyst lowers E_a. Not consumed. ΔH unchanged." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.
