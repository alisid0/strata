## BB-NEW-280 — The nature of force: what a push actually is

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** force-definition; contact-vs-field; four-fundamental-forces; Newton-context | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 03, Card 05, BB-NEW-26]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Pick up a book. Your hand exerts a force. The Earth exerts a force on the same book from 6,371 km away, through empty space, with no strings attached. A force is a push or a pull — but that's the effect, not the cause. At the deepest level, every force in the universe is one of four: gravity, electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force, or the weak nuclear force. The contact forces you feel — a table pushing up, friction dragging back, tension in a rope — are all electromagnetic at root. Electrons in your hand repel electrons in the book. That repulsion IS the contact force.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Push a book across a table. Four forces act on the book: (1) Gravity — the Earth's mass pulls the book's mass. This is a field force, acting at a distance. (2) Normal force — the table's atoms repel the book's atoms. This is a contact force, ultimately electromagnetic. (3) Your hand's push — another contact force, electromagnetic at root. (4) Friction — the table resists sliding, also a contact electromagnetic force. The book accelerates because the net of these four vectors is not zero. If you stop pushing, friction alone decelerates the book to rest. Remove the table and only gravity acts — the book falls. Every force has an identifiable source (Earth, table, hand) and an identifiable type (gravity, electromagnetic). If you can't name the source, you haven't found the force — you've imagined one.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>force</strong> is an interaction that, when unopposed, changes the motion of an object. It is a vector quantity — magnitude, direction, point of application. SI unit: <strong>newton</strong> (N). 1 N = the force needed to accelerate 1 kg at 1 m/s². The <strong>four fundamental forces</strong> of nature: (1) <strong>Gravity</strong> — between masses, weakest but infinite range. (2) <strong>Electromagnetism</strong> — between charges, holds atoms and molecules together. (3) <strong>Strong nuclear force</strong> — binds protons and neutrons in the nucleus, strongest but ~10⁻¹⁵ m range. (4) <strong>Weak nuclear force</strong> — responsible for certain types of radioactive decay. Every macroscopic force you experience — tension, normal, friction, spring — is electromagnetic at the atomic level.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Knowing the four fundamental forces reframes every mechanics problem. Gravity always acts, is always attractive, and is always proportional to mass. Electromagnetism is behind every contact force — and it's why you don't fall through your chair. The electrons in the chair and the electrons in you repel each other with staggering force at tiny distances. The strong force explains why atomic nuclei don't fly apart despite the electrostatic repulsion of protons — it's ~100× stronger than electromagnetism at nuclear distances. The weak force explains why the Sun shines — it mediates the first step of the proton-proton chain that powers stellar fusion. Forces are not a list to memorise. They are the four ways the universe talks to itself, and everything you push, pull, or feel is one of these four conversations.</p>

## BB-NEW-281 — Forces on an inclined plane: the complete attack

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** inclined-plane; resolving-weight; friction-on-slope; complete-FBD | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-27, BB-NEW-28, BB-NEW-36, BB-NEW-38]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The inclined plane is the perfect testing ground for force analysis. Gravity pulls straight down — always. But the slope only cares about the component along its surface. So you resolve weight into two pieces: mg sin θ pulling the block down the slope, and mg cos θ pressing it into the slope. The normal force equals mg cos θ (not mg — the slope matters). Friction opposes motion (or impending motion) along the slope. Draw the free body diagram, resolve carefully, write ΣF = ma along and perpendicular to the slope — and the inclined plane surrenders every time.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 10 kg block rests on a slope at 30°. μₛ = 0.5, μₖ = 0.3. Will it slide? Weight: mg = 98 N. Resolve: mg sin 30° = 98 × 0.5 = 49 N (down slope). mg cos 30° = 98 × 0.866 = 84.9 N (into slope). Normal N = 84.9 N (perpendicular equilibrium). Maximum static friction: fₛ_max = μₛN = 0.5 × 84.9 = 42.5 N. Down-slope pull (49 N) > max static friction (42.5 N) → it slides. Acceleration: net force down slope = 49 − μₖN = 49 − 0.3×84.9 = 49 − 25.5 = 23.5 N. a = F/m = 23.5/10 = 2.35 m/s². Now add a string pulling UP the slope with tension T = 60 N. Net force down slope = 49 − 60 = −11 N (i.e. 11 N UP the slope). Friction now opposes this upward tendency — it acts DOWN the slope. The block may or may not move depending on whether 11 N exceeds fₛ_max. This is the inclined plane puzzle: mg sin θ, mg cos θ, normal, friction, and any applied forces — five vectors that must be correctly resolved and summed.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>For a block of mass m on a slope of angle θ: (1) Resolve weight into <strong>mg sin θ</strong> (parallel, down slope) and <strong>mg cos θ</strong> (perpendicular, into slope). (2) Normal force <strong>N = mg cos θ</strong> (if no other perpendicular forces). (3) Friction acts along the slope, opposing motion or impending motion: maximum static friction fₛ_max = μₛN; kinetic friction fₖ = μₖN. (4) Write ΣF_∥ = ma and ΣF_⊥ = 0 (no acceleration perpendicular to the slope). The angle of repose: tan θ = μₛ — the slope angle where the block just begins to slide.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The inclined plane is the universal force-analysis workout. Add an external force at an arbitrary angle — resolve IT into slope-parallel and slope-perpendicular components too. Add a second mass hanging over a pulley at the top of the slope — now tension joins the party. The procedure never changes: (1) Draw the FBD. (2) Choose axes — ALWAYS align one axis with the slope, one perpendicular. (3) Resolve EVERY force that isn't already aligned. (4) Write ΣF = ma for each axis. (5) Solve. The inclined plane trains the skill that every complex mechanics problem demands: systematic resolution, careful sign conventions, and the discipline to account for every force before writing equations. Master the inclined plane, and you can handle any static or dynamic force configuration.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a block on a 30° slope. Weight mg resolved into mg sin θ (along slope, down) and mg cos θ (perpendicular, into slope). Normal N = mg cos θ. Friction f along slope opposing motion. Below: the full equations — ΣF_∥ = mg sin θ − f = ma, ΣF_⊥ = N − mg cos θ = 0. A worked example with numbers. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-282 — Connected bodies: tension, pulleys, and linked motion

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** connected-bodies; pulley-systems; tension; constrained-motion | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-29, BB-NEW-35, BB-NEW-281]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Two masses connected by a string. Pull one, and the other moves. They share a tension, they share an acceleration magnitude, but they may have different forces acting on them. The trick: treat each mass as a separate free body. Write F = ma for each one. The tension appears in both equations, tying them together. Solve simultaneously. The string is the courier — it transmits force without storing it (if massless and inextensible). A pulley changes the direction of tension without changing its magnitude (if frictionless). Connected-body problems are F = ma applied twice, linked by a constraint.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Atwood's machine: two masses, m₁ = 3 kg and m₂ = 5 kg, connected by a light string over a frictionless pulley. Both accelerate — the heavier falls, the lighter rises — with the same magnitude a (the string doesn't stretch). FBD for m₁ (rising): T − m₁g = m₁a. FBD for m₂ (falling): m₂g − T = m₂a. Add the equations to eliminate T: m₂g − m₁g = (m₁+m₂)a → a = g(m₂−m₁)/(m₂+m₁) = 9.8(2)/8 = 2.45 m/s². Tension: T = m₁(g+a) = 3(9.8+2.45) = 36.75 N (or T = m₂(g−a) = 5(9.8−2.45) = 36.75 N — consistent). Check extremes: if m₁=m₂, a=0, T=mg — balanced. If m₂≫m₁, a≈g, T≈2m₁g — the lighter mass limits the tension. If the pulley has mass and friction, we need torque — that's the bridge to rotational dynamics (coming in Part 3).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>For <strong>connected bodies</strong> linked by an ideal (massless, inextensible) string over a frictionless, massless pulley: (1) Tension T is the same on both sides of the pulley. (2) The magnitudes of acceleration of both masses are equal (|a₁| = |a₂| = a) because the string length is constant. (3) Write F = ma for each body separately, with T as a linking variable. (4) Solve the simultaneous equations. For a mass on a horizontal surface connected to a hanging mass: a = m_hanging g / (m_total). For an inclined plane with a hanging mass: the hanging weight fights mg sin θ ± friction. The general principle: isolate, apply F=ma, link via constraints.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Connected-body problems are the prototype for every multi-object system in physics. An elevator: the cabin is one body, the counterweight another, linked by a cable over a pulley. A train: locomotive pulls carriages, each coupling transmits tension to the next — a chain of connected bodies. In biomechanics: muscles pull bones via tendons — tension elements connecting body segments. The method is always the same: separate FBDs, F=ma for each, constraints linking them. The constraints encode the geometry (string length, pulley radius, linkage length) and the physics (tension uniform for ideal string, acceleration equal). When you add pulleys with mass — as in real machinery — the rotational dynamics of the pulley itself (τ = Iα) must be included. The connected-body method is the scaffold on which all multi-object dynamics is built.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of an Atwood machine: two masses m₁ and m₂ hanging from a pulley. FBDs for each mass shown beside — m₁: T up, m₁g down, net m₁a up; m₂: T up, m₂g down, net m₂a down. The simultaneous equations solved. Below: a horizontal-surface-connected-to-hanging-mass variant. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-283 — Systems of particles: the whole is F = ma on the COM

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** system-of-particles; COM-motion; internal-vs-external-forces; reduced-mass | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-48, BB-NEW-33]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>So far, F = ma has been applied to single particles. But what about a system — a swarm of particles, a rigid body, a solar system? The miracle: the centre of mass of ANY system moves as if all the mass were concentrated there and all the EXTERNAL forces acted there. Internal forces — the forces particles exert on each other — cancel out in pairs (Newton's third law) and do not affect the COM's motion. You can treat a spinning, tumbling, exploding object as a single point particle for the purpose of its overall trajectory.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A firework rocket explodes in mid-air. The fragments spray in all directions. But the centre of mass of all the fragments continues on exactly the same parabolic trajectory the rocket was following before the explosion. The explosion was internal forces — fragments pushed on each other. Those forces cancel for the COM. Gravity, the only external force, continues to pull the COM down in its perfect parabola. A high-jumper arches over the bar — their body contorts, but their centre of mass passes UNDER the bar even as their body passes OVER it (the Fosbury Flop). The COM doesn't care about the body's acrobatics; it follows the projectile parabola determined by the takeoff velocity and gravity. In a collision: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁+m₂)V_COM — the COM velocity is constant throughout (if no external forces).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>For a system of particles: (1) The <strong>centre of mass</strong> r⃗_COM = (Σ mᵢr⃗ᵢ) / M. (2) Newton's second law for the system: <strong>ΣF⃗_ext = M a⃗_COM</strong> — the sum of external forces equals total mass times COM acceleration. Internal forces sum to zero and do not appear. (3) Total momentum P⃗ = M v⃗_COM. If ΣF_ext = 0, v⃗_COM is constant — the COM moves with constant velocity. (4) The motion of the system can be decomposed into: COM translation + motion relative to COM (rotation, vibration). The COM equation handles the translation. For rotation about the COM, we need torque and angular momentum — the subject of Parts 2–4 of this course.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The COM principle is the great simplifier. A wrench thrown spinning through the air — its COM traces a perfect parabola, even as the ends whirl around it. A binary star system — two stars orbiting each other — has a COM that moves in a straight line through the galaxy (or an orbit around the galactic centre). In rocket propulsion: the COM of the rocket + ejected exhaust continues with constant velocity (no external forces in deep space) even as the rocket accelerates forward. The exhaust goes backward, the rocket goes forward, the COM stays put. The COM theorem turns a chaotic many-body problem into a single-particle problem — find the COM, sum the external forces, and F = ma takes care of the rest. The internal forces can do whatever they like; the COM doesn't care.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of an exploding firework: fragments spraying in all directions, but their COM traced as a dashed parabola continuing the pre-explosion path. Below: ΣF_ext = M a_COM in a box. A high-jumper arching over a bar, COM passing under it. "COM ignores internal forces." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-284 — What is torque? The force that twists

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** torque; moment-of-force; lever-arm; turning-effect | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 05, BB-NEW-283]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Push on a door near the hinge — it barely moves. Push with the same force at the handle — it swings easily. The force is the same. The distance from the hinge is different. This is torque: the rotational equivalent of force. Torque depends on three things: how hard you push, how far from the pivot you push, and the angle between your push and the lever. Push perpendicular to the door at the handle — maximum torque. Push parallel to the door (into the hinge) — zero torque. Torque = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot. It's what makes things spin.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A wrench loosening a bolt: apply F = 50 N at the end of a 0.3 m wrench, perpendicular to the handle. Torque: τ = F × r_⊥ = 50 × 0.3 = 15 N·m. Now apply the same force at a 30° angle to the handle. Only the perpendicular component counts: F sin 30° = 50 × 0.5 = 25 N. Torque = 25 × 0.3 = 7.5 N·m — half as effective. The best technique: push perpendicular at the very end of the wrench. Worst: push along the handle (θ = 0°, sin 0° = 0, τ = 0). The same principle governs seesaws: a child of mass 20 kg sitting 2 m from the pivot exerts torque τ = (20×9.8) × 2 = 392 N·m. A 40 kg child can balance at 1 m: τ = (40×9.8) × 1 = 392 N·m — equal torque, equilibrium.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Torque</strong> τ (also called moment of force) is the rotational analogue of force. For a force F applied at a distance r from a pivot point O: <strong>τ = r × F</strong> (cross product). Magnitude: <strong>τ = r F sin θ</strong>, where θ is the angle between the position vector r⃗ (from pivot to point of application) and the force vector F⃗. <strong>Perpendicular distance</strong> (lever arm): d = r sin θ. So τ = F × d — force times lever arm. Units: <strong>N·m</strong> (same dimensions as energy, but conceptually different — torque is a vector, energy is a scalar). Direction: given by the right-hand rule (fingers curl from r⃗ to F⃗, thumb points along τ⃗). By convention: anticlockwise torque is positive; clockwise is negative.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Torque is the universal language of turning. Every wrench, screwdriver, steering wheel, door handle, and tap is a torque device. The longer the lever arm, the less force needed — this is mechanical advantage in its purest form. A longer wrench multiplies your force. A gear system trades torque for speed (or vice versa): a small gear driving a large gear multiplies torque and reduces speed. In the human body: muscles attach close to joints (short lever arm), so they must exert enormous forces to produce modest torques. The bicep attaches ~5 cm from the elbow pivot; holding a 10 kg mass at 30 cm from the elbow requires bicep tension of ~600 N — six times the weight. Torque explains why your back hurts when you lift with bad form: the lever arm of the weight about your spine is large, demanding enormous muscle forces.</p>

Three pushes shown: near hinge (small effect), at handle perpendicular (maximum, τ = F×d), at handle at 30° (reduced, τ = F×d×sin30°). Below: a wrench on a bolt with τ = rF sin θ labelled. A seesaw with two children — equal torques balancing. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-285 — Calculating torque: the cross product in practice

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** torque-calculation; lever-arm; sign-convention; multiple-forces | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-284, BB-NEW-168, BB-NEW-27]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Torque is a cross product: τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗. Its magnitude is rF sin θ. Its direction is perpendicular to both r⃗ and F⃗ — along the axis of rotation. For most problems, you don't need the full 3D vector treatment. You need the magnitude (r F sin θ) and the sign (anticlockwise = +, clockwise = −). But when multiple forces act on an extended body, you must compute torque about a CHOSEN pivot point — and the choice of pivot matters. Choose wisely and the problem simplifies.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A uniform beam of length 4 m and weight 200 N rests on two supports at its ends. A 600 N weight sits 1 m from the left end. Find the reaction forces at the supports. Choose the left support as the pivot. Calculate torque about this point: (1) R₁ (left support): r = 0, so τ = 0 — this is why you choose a support as pivot, to eliminate an unknown. (2) Beam weight (200 N at 2 m from left — the centre): τ = −200 × 2 = −400 N·m (clockwise). (3) Load (600 N at 1 m): τ = −600 × 1 = −600 N·m (clockwise). (4) R₂ (right support, at 4 m): τ = +R₂ × 4 (anticlockwise). For rotational equilibrium, Στ = 0: 4R₂ − 400 − 600 = 0 → R₂ = 250 N. For translational equilibrium, ΣF_y = 0: R₁ + R₂ − 200 − 600 = 0 → R₁ = 800 − 250 = 550 N. Check: choosing the RIGHT support as pivot should give the same R₁. Try it — it does. The choice of pivot is a strategy, not a physical constraint.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>To calculate torque about a point O: (1) For each force, find the <strong>position vector</strong> r⃗ from O to the point of application. (2) Compute τ = rF sin θ, or equivalently τ = F × d, where d = r sin θ is the <strong>lever arm</strong> — the perpendicular distance from O to the line of action of the force. (3) Assign sign: anticlockwise = positive, clockwise = negative (convention must be consistent). (4) Sum all torques. For a <strong>couple</strong> (two equal, opposite, parallel forces with different lines of action): τ_couple = F × d, where d = perpendicular distance between the lines of action. A couple produces pure rotation — no net force, so the COM doesn't accelerate.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The pivot-choice strategy is the torque equivalent of choosing axes in force problems. Choose the pivot where an unknown force acts — that unknown disappears from the torque equation. Choose the pivot where multiple unknown forces act — they all disappear. In a ladder-against-wall problem: choose the pivot at the bottom of the ladder. The normal force and friction at the bottom produce zero torque, leaving only the wall's normal force and the ladder's weight. In a crane or extended structure: choose the pivot to eliminate the maximum number of unknowns. The torque equation is one equation — use it strategically to extract one unknown at a time. Combined with ΣF_x = 0 and ΣF_y = 0, you have three equations for 2D static equilibrium problems — enough to solve for three unknowns. If there are more unknowns, the problem is statically indeterminate (you need material properties, not just force/torque balance).</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a beam on two supports with a load. The left-support-as-pivot calculation: τ_R₁ = 0 (at pivot), τ_beam = −200×2, τ_load = −600×1, τ_R₂ = +4R₂. Στ=0 solved for R₂. ΣF_y=0 for R₁. Below: the couple τ = Fd with two equal opposite forces. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-286 — Couples and pure rotation

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** couple; pure-torque; centre-of-mass-rotation | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-284, BB-NEW-285, BB-NEW-283]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Take a bicycle wheel by the axle and spin it. Your two hands apply equal and opposite forces — one pushing up, one pushing down. The net force is zero — the wheel's centre of mass doesn't accelerate. But the net torque is NOT zero — the wheel spins. This is a couple: two equal, opposite, parallel forces offset by a distance. It produces pure rotation with no translation. A couple is the rotational equivalent of a single force: as far as rotation is concerned, a couple is the fundamental building block.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Turning a steering wheel: your hands apply equal and opposite forces at the rim. If each hand pushes with 20 N and the wheel diameter is 40 cm (radius 20 cm), the couple torque = F × (distance between forces) = 20 × 0.4 = 8 N·m. If you push with one hand and pull with the other, the forces are in the same rotational sense and add — still a couple of 8 N·m. Opening a tap: your thumb and finger form a couple on the handle. Twisting a screwdriver: your hand applies a couple to the handle. The key: the net force is zero, so the object's centre of mass stays put (or continues with constant velocity). But the net torque is non-zero, so the angular velocity changes. A couple cannot be replaced by a single force — it IS the simplest force configuration that produces rotation without translation. The torque of a couple is independent of the choice of pivot — it's the same about any point. τ_couple = Fd, always.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>couple</strong> is a pair of equal, opposite, parallel forces with different lines of action. Properties: (1) Net force = 0 — no translation of the COM. (2) Net torque τ = <strong>F × d</strong>, where d is the perpendicular distance between the force lines. (3) The torque of a couple is <strong>independent of the pivot</strong> — it's a free vector. (4) A couple cannot be reduced to a single force — it is the simplest system that produces pure rotation. Any system of forces can be reduced to a single resultant force (acting at the COM for translation) PLUS a resultant couple (for rotation). This is the fundamental theorem of statics.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The couple is the reason your washing machine walks across the floor if the load is unbalanced. The spinning drum's centre of mass is offset from the rotation axis — the centrifugal force on the COM plus the reaction at the bearing form a couple (or, equivalently, a rotating unbalanced force). This couple produces a net torque on the machine, which rocks and walks. Electric motors work by producing a couple: the magnetic forces on opposite sides of the armature are equal and opposite, producing a torque on the rotor. In structural engineering, a couple is the internal resistance of a beam to bending — tension on one side, compression on the other, separated by the beam's depth, forming a couple that resists the applied bending moment. The couple is not an odd special case — it's half of mechanics. Forces handle translation; couples handle rotation.</p>

Below: a washing machine with an unbalanced load — the rotating offset mass creates a wobbling couple. "τ_couple = Fd, independent of pivot." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-287 — Rotational equilibrium: ΣF = 0 AND Στ = 0

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** rotational-equilibrium; static-equilibrium-extended; ladder-problem; two-conditions | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-35, BB-NEW-285]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A point particle is in equilibrium when the net force is zero. An extended body needs TWO conditions: ΣF = 0 (no translation) AND Στ = 0 (no rotation). A ruler balanced on your finger satisfies both — the normal force up equals weight down (ΣF_y = 0), and the weight acts through the pivot so torque is zero (Στ = 0). If ΣF = 0 but Στ ≠ 0, the body spins in place. If Στ = 0 about one point but ΣF ≠ 0, the body accelerates without spinning. Both must be satisfied for static equilibrium.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A uniform ladder of length 5 m and weight 200 N leans against a frictionless wall at 60° to the horizontal. The ground has friction. Find the forces. Forces: weight W = 200 N down at the centre (2.5 m along the ladder from either end). Normal from wall N_w (horizontal, to the left — the wall is frictionless so no vertical force). Normal from ground N_g (vertical, up). Friction from ground f (horizontal, to the right — prevents the ladder's foot from sliding left). Four unknowns: N_w, N_g, f, and (implicitly) the angle. Three equations: ΣF_x = 0 → N_w = f. ΣF_y = 0 → N_g = W = 200 N. Στ = 0 about the foot of the ladder. Take torques about the foot: W produces torque −200 × (2.5 cos 60°) = −200 × 1.25 = −250 N·m (clockwise). N_w produces torque +N_w × (5 sin 60°) = +N_w × 4.33 (anticlockwise). Στ = 0: 4.33 N_w = 250 → N_w = 57.7 N. So f = 57.7 N. The ladder stays put as long as f ≤ μₛ N_g — i.e., 57.7 ≤ μₛ × 200 → μₛ ≥ 0.29. If the floor is more slippery than μₛ = 0.29, the ladder slides.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A rigid body is in <strong>static equilibrium</strong> when: (1) <strong>ΣF⃗ = 0</strong> — vector sum of all external forces is zero (no translational acceleration). (2) <strong>Στ⃗ = 0</strong> about ANY point — vector sum of all external torques is zero (no angular acceleration). The torque condition must hold about EVERY point; if it holds about one point and ΣF = 0, it automatically holds about all points. In 2D, this gives three scalar equations: ΣF_x = 0, ΣF_y = 0, Στ_z = 0 — enough to solve for three unknowns. For more unknowns, the structure is statically indeterminate — internal forces and deformations must be considered.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The two equilibrium conditions are the foundation of all structural engineering. A bridge doesn't move or twist — every beam, cable, and joint satisfies ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0 under its design loads. A crane lifting a load: the counterweight is positioned so that the net torque about the base is zero — without it, the crane topples. Your body standing on one foot: the ankle, knee, and hip joints adjust moments to keep your COM over the contact patch. A picture frame hanging from two nails — the tensions adjust so that the weight is shared and the frame doesn't rotate. The ladder problem is the classic workout: choose the pivot at the foot (eliminates N_g and f), compute torques, find the wall force, then use ΣF = 0 for the rest. The pattern — choose pivot to eliminate unknowns, write Στ = 0, then ΣF = 0 — solves every static equilibrium problem.</p>

## BB-NEW-288 — Moment of inertia: the rotational mass

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** moment-of-inertia; rotational-inertia; I=Σmr²; radius-of-gyration | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-284, BB-NEW-39, BB-NEW-48]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Push an empty shopping trolley — it accelerates easily. Push a fully loaded one — it resists. Mass is linear inertia. Now spin a bicycle wheel — it resists being spun up AND resists being stopped. That's rotational inertia — moment of inertia, I. And it's not just about how much mass, but about WHERE the mass is. Mass far from the axis contributes far more to I than mass near the axis. I = Σmr² — every particle's mass multiplied by the square of its distance from the axis. A disc with mass at the rim has higher I than the same mass concentrated at the centre. Moment of inertia is the rotational mass.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Two cylinders of equal mass (1 kg) and equal radius (0.1 m): one is a solid cylinder, one is a thin cylindrical shell (all mass at the rim). I_solid = ½MR² = 0.5 × 1 × 0.01 = 0.005 kg·m². I_shell = MR² = 1 × 0.01 = 0.01 kg·m². The shell has TWICE the moment of inertia. Roll both down an incline — the solid cylinder wins because less of the gravitational potential energy goes into rotation and more goes into translation. Ice skater spinning: arms outstretched → mass far from axis → large I, slow spin. Pull arms in → mass close to axis → small I, fast spin. Same angular momentum L = Iω, so ω increases as I decreases. This is the skater effect — conservation of angular momentum in action (coming in BB-NEW-290).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Moment of inertia</strong> I of a rigid body about an axis is <strong>I = Σ mᵢ rᵢ²</strong> (discrete) or <strong>I = ∫ r² dm</strong> (continuous). Units: kg·m². It depends on: mass, mass distribution, and the chosen axis. Common values about the centre of mass: thin rod (length L, about centre): I = ML²/12; about end: I = ML²/3. Solid cylinder/disc (radius R, about central axis): I = ½MR². Thin spherical shell: I = ⅔MR². Solid sphere: I = ⅖MR². <strong>Parallel axis theorem</strong>: I = I_COM + Md², where d is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes. <strong>Radius of gyration</strong> k: I = Mk² — the distance from the axis where all the mass would need to be concentrated to give the same I.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Moment of inertia is the design parameter for everything that rotates. A flywheel stores rotational energy (½Iω²) — large I, high ω = massive energy storage, used to smooth power delivery in engines. A racing car wheel has low I (lightweight materials, mass concentrated near the hub) for fast acceleration. A cyclist's wheels: reducing rim mass improves acceleration more than reducing frame mass because of the r² factor — mass at the rim contributes fully to I, mass at the hub contributes almost nothing. A tightrope walker's long pole: by holding a pole with mass far from the rotation axis (their body), they enormously increase I, which makes them harder to rotate — giving them time to correct their balance. In all cases, I is the resistance to angular acceleration. The larger I is, the more torque is needed to achieve the same angular acceleration. τ = Iα — the rotational F = ma. That's next.</p>

## BB-NEW-289 — Newton's second law for rotation: τ = Iα

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** rotational-Newtons-second-law; τ=Iα; angular-acceleration; pulley-with-mass | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-288, BB-NEW-284, BB-NEW-282]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>F = ma governs translation. τ = Iα governs rotation. Torque is the rotational force. Moment of inertia is the rotational mass. Angular acceleration (α) is the rotational acceleration. The equation is the exact analogue: net torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration. Just as a net force produces linear acceleration, a net torque produces angular acceleration. And just as a=0 means constant velocity, α=0 means constant angular velocity. The parallel is perfect.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A solid disc (M = 5 kg, R = 0.2 m, I = ½MR² = 0.5 × 5 × 0.04 = 0.1 kg·m²) is free to rotate about its central axis. A constant torque of 2 N·m is applied. Angular acceleration: α = τ/I = 2/0.1 = 20 rad/s². After 3 seconds: ω = αt = 60 rad/s ≈ 573 rpm. Number of revolutions: θ = ½αt² = ½ × 20 × 9 = 90 rad = 90/(2π) ≈ 14.3 revolutions. Now wind a string around the disc's rim and hang a 2 kg mass (weight = 19.6 N). The tension T in the string provides torque τ = TR = T × 0.2. The mass accelerates down: mg − T = ma. The disc rotates: τ = Iα = TR. And a = αR (the string unwinds without slipping). Three equations: mg − T = ma, TR = Iα, a = αR. Substitute a = αR into the first: mg − T = mαR. From the second: T = Iα/R. Substitute: mg − Iα/R = mαR → α = mgR/(mR² + I) = (2×9.8×0.2)/(2×0.04 + 0.1) = 3.92/0.18 = 21.8 rad/s². Linear acceleration a = αR = 4.36 m/s² — less than g because some of gravity's work goes into spinning the disc. This is why a real pulley (with mass) reduces acceleration compared to an ideal massless pulley.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Newton's second law for rotation: <strong>Στ = Iα</strong>, where Στ = net torque about the rotation axis, I = moment of inertia about that axis, and α = angular acceleration. The equation holds for a rigid body rotating about a FIXED axis. Analogous to ΣF = ma: τ ↔ F, I ↔ m, α ↔ a. For a body both translating and rotating, use ΣF = ma for the COM motion AND Στ = Iα for rotation about the COM. The torque and I must be about the SAME axis. The condition for rolling without slipping: a = αR, where a is the COM acceleration and α is the angular acceleration about the COM.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>τ = Iα is the engine of rotational dynamics. Every motor — from a phone's vibration motor to a factory's induction motor — is specified by its torque and its rotor's moment of inertia. The start-up time to reach operating speed is t = ω/α = Iω/τ. A hard disk drive spins up in seconds because τ is high and I is small. A loaded cement mixer takes minutes because I is enormous. In biomechanics: your arm is a lever with a moment of inertia about the shoulder. The deltoid muscle provides torque τ = Iα to raise the arm. Holding a weight in your hand increases I dramatically (the weight is far from the shoulder joint, and r² amplifies its contribution) — the muscle must produce far more torque for the same α. The τ = Iα framework, combined with F = ma for translation, is the complete set of equations for any rigid body motion — translation plus rotation. The two worlds are controlled by two equations, operating in parallel.</p>

## BB-NEW-290 — Angular momentum: the spinning quantity that is conserved

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** angular-momentum; L=Iω; conservation-of-angular-momentum | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-288, BB-NEW-289, BB-NEW-32, BB-NEW-49]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Linear momentum p = mv is conserved when there's no external force. Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved when there's no external torque. This is the rotational twin of momentum conservation — and it's arguably even more dramatic. The ice skater pulling in their arms: I decreases, ω increases, L = Iω stays constant. The spinning neutron star (pulsar): a star collapses from ~10⁶ km to ~10 km radius — I decreases by a factor of ~10¹⁰, so ω increases by the same factor. A star that rotated once a month now rotates hundreds of times per second. Angular momentum conservation is ruthless — it demands that the product Iω remains unchanged, and nature obeys.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A student sits on a freely rotating stool holding two 3 kg masses at arm's length (0.8 m from the axis). Their body's moment of inertia (without masses) is 4 kg·m². Masses at 0.8 m: I_masses = 2 × 3 × (0.8)² = 2 × 3 × 0.64 = 3.84 kg·m². Total I_initial = 4 + 3.84 = 7.84 kg·m². The stool is spun to ω = 2 rad/s. L = Iω = 7.84 × 2 = 15.68 kg·m²/s. The student pulls the masses in to 0.2 m. I_masses_new = 2 × 3 × (0.2)² = 2 × 3 × 0.04 = 0.24 kg·m². I_new = 4 + 0.24 = 4.24 kg·m². With L conserved: ω_new = L/I_new = 15.68/4.24 ≈ 3.70 rad/s — nearly double. The student feels themselves spinning faster — the rotational equivalent of an ice skater. The torque required to pull the masses inward does work, which increases the rotational kinetic energy (½Iω²_new > ½Iω²_old). Where did the extra energy come from? The student's muscles doing work against the centrifugal force. Energy is NOT conserved in this process (the student did work); angular momentum IS conserved because no external torque acted on the stool+student+masses system.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Angular momentum</strong> L of a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis: <strong>L = Iω</strong>. It is a vector directed along the rotation axis (right-hand rule). Units: kg·m²/s. For a particle: L⃗ = r⃗ × p⃗ = r⃗ × (mv⃗). <strong>Conservation of angular momentum</strong>: if the net external torque on a system is zero (Στ_ext = 0), the total angular momentum of the system is conserved — L_initial = L_final. This follows from τ = dL/dt: if τ = 0, then dL/dt = 0, so L = constant. Angular momentum is conserved independently in each direction — L_x, L_y, L_z are each conserved if τ_x, τ_y, τ_z are zero respectively.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Angular momentum conservation is why the solar system is flat. The original gas cloud had some tiny net angular momentum from random turbulence. As it collapsed, conservation amplified that rotation — the cloud flattened into a disc, and the planets orbit in roughly the same plane. It's why helicopters need tail rotors: the main rotor's angular momentum change produces a torque on the helicopter body — the tail rotor counters it. It's why gyroscopes resist tipping: changing the direction of L requires a torque (τ = dL/dt), and a large L resists change. It's why a cat dropped upside-down can land on its feet — by twisting its body in a specific sequence, it changes its moment of inertia distribution to rotate without violating angular momentum conservation (the cat's total L remains zero — it rotates its front and rear halves in opposite directions, then adjusts). Angular momentum is conserved, period. No exceptions in classical mechanics. If it looks like L changed, you missed a torque or you missed where the compensating angular momentum went.</p>

## BB-NEW-291 — Rolling motion: translation + rotation, locked together

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** rolling-without-slipping; a=αR; kinetic-energy-rolling; friction-in-rolling | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-289, BB-NEW-288, BB-NEW-45]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A rolling object is doing two things at once: its centre of mass is translating, and it's rotating about its centre of mass. When it rolls without slipping, these two motions are locked: the distance the COM travels equals the arc length unrolled — x = Rθ. Differentiate: v = Rω. Differentiate again: a = Rα. The point of contact with the ground is instantaneously at REST relative to the ground. This is the magic of rolling: the bottom point stops, the top point moves at 2v_COM. And it's friction that makes it happen — static friction, not kinetic — because the contact point doesn't slide.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A solid sphere (M = 2 kg, R = 0.1 m, I = ⅖MR² = 0.4 × 2 × 0.01 = 0.008 kg·m²) rolls down a 1 m high incline without slipping. Energy conservation: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω². With v = ωR → ω = v/R: mgh = ½mv² + ½I(v²/R²) = ½v²(m + I/R²). For the solid sphere: I/R² = ⅖M. So mgh = ½v²(m + ⅖m) = ½v²(7/5)m. v² = (10/7)gh = (10/7) × 9.8 × 1 = 14. v = √14 ≈ 3.74 m/s. Compare to a frictionless sliding block: mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √19.6 ≈ 4.43 m/s. The rolling sphere is slower because some energy goes into rotation. Compare different shapes rolling: solid sphere (I = ⅖MR²) → v = √(10gh/7). Solid cylinder (I = ½MR²) → v = √(4gh/3). Hollow cylinder (I = MR²) → v = √(gh). The solid sphere wins — least energy "wasted" on rotation. For a given shape, the speed at the bottom depends ONLY on height, not on mass or radius — Galileo would have loved this.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>For <strong>rolling without slipping</strong>: (1) <strong>v_COM = ωR</strong> — the translational speed equals the rotational speed times radius. (2) <strong>a_COM = αR</strong>. (3) The instantaneous point of contact with the surface is at REST — static friction acts there (not kinetic). (4) Total kinetic energy: <strong>KE = ½Mv²_COM + ½I_COM ω²</strong> — translation plus rotation. (5) On an incline: a = g sin θ / (1 + I/(MR²)). Objects with smaller I/(MR²) accelerate faster. A frictionless object would slide with a = g sin θ — faster than any rolling object. Static friction provides the torque for rolling but does NO work (the contact point doesn't move).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Rolling explains the "which reaches the bottom first?" demonstration. Release a solid cylinder, a hollow cylinder, and a solid sphere simultaneously from the top of an incline. The sphere wins, the solid cylinder comes second, the hollow cylinder last — and they always finish in that order, regardless of mass or radius. It's purely I/(MR²) — the smaller the ratio, the less energy diverted into rotation. In vehicle dynamics: a car wheel that locks and skids (kinetic friction) has less stopping power than a wheel rolling at the edge of slipping (static friction). Anti-lock brakes (ABS) keep the wheels rolling — static friction, maximum braking. In bowling: the ball initially slides, then starts rolling as friction converts sliding to rolling. The transition point is when v = ωR. Rolling is the bridge from translational dynamics to rotational dynamics — F = ma and τ = Iα, linked by a = αR. The two worlds, operating in synchrony.</p>

## BB-NEW-292 — Conservation of angular momentum: the universe's spin budget

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** angular-momentum-conservation; applications; astrophysics; gyroscopic-stability | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-290, BB-NEW-283]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Linear momentum is conserved when ΣF_ext = 0. Angular momentum is conserved when Στ_ext = 0. This is not an analogy — it's the same principle, rotational symmetry, expressed two ways. If the universe has no preferred direction (it doesn't), angular momentum is conserved. This single law explains why galaxies spin, why planets orbit in planes, why neutron stars are the fastest-spinning objects known, and why you can't tip a spinning gyroscope without it fighting back in a surprising direction.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A neutron star is born when a massive star's core collapses. The original star (radius ~10⁶ km, rotation period ~30 days) collapses to a neutron star (radius ~10 km). I ∝ R², so I decreases by (10⁶/10)² = 10¹⁰. Since L = Iω is conserved, ω increases by 10¹⁰. Period goes from 30 days to ~0.26 seconds — about 230 rotations per second. The fastest known pulsar spins at 716 Hz — 716 rotations per second. Its surface speed is about ¼ the speed of light. All from angular momentum conservation. In our solar system: the original gas cloud had some tiny angular momentum. As it contracted, the spin increased. The cloud flattened into a disc, and the material in the disc coalesced into planets — all orbiting in the same direction, in nearly the same plane. The Sun contains 99.8% of the solar system's mass but only ~0.5% of its angular momentum — the planets, especially Jupiter, carry most of the spin. This distribution is evidence for how the solar system formed.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Conservation of angular momentum</strong>: For a system with zero net external torque, L⃗_total = constant — both magnitude and direction. For a single rigid body: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ (if I changes and no external torque acts). For a system of particles: Σ r⃗ᵢ × mᵢv⃗ᵢ = constant. Key consequences: (1) As I decreases, ω increases. (2) The direction of L is fixed — a spinning object resists changes to its spin axis (gyroscopic stability). (3) Angular momentum is a vector — it has three independent components, each conserved if the corresponding torque component is zero. (4) Angular momentum conservation is a consequence of the isotropy of space (no preferred direction) via Noether's theorem.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Angular momentum conservation is the universe's spin accountant. In a diving competition: the diver leaves the board with some angular momentum (from the takeoff). In the air, they tuck into a ball — I decreases, ω increases, they spin fast. Before entry, they extend — I increases, ω decreases, they enter the water with minimal splash. Total L throughout the dive is constant (ignoring small air resistance torques). A falling cat: starting from rest (L=0), it can rotate its front and rear halves in opposite directions, keeping total L=0, to land on its feet. A helicopter with a single main rotor: the rotor's angular momentum change produces a torque on the fuselage (τ = dL/dt). Without a tail rotor to counter this torque, the fuselage would spin opposite to the main rotor. Gyroscopic navigation: a gyroscope maintains its spin axis direction in inertial space. Aircraft and spacecraft use gyroscopes to know their orientation even when GPS and visual references are unavailable — because L is conserved, the gyro's axis is a fixed reference.</p>

## BB-NEW-293 — Levers and mechanical advantage: trading force for distance

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** levers; mechanical-advantage; classes-of-levers; torque-balance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-284, BB-NEW-285]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>"Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth," Archimedes said. He was talking about levers. A lever is a rigid bar pivoted at a fulcrum. A small force applied far from the fulcrum balances a large force close to the fulcrum. The ratio of the distances is the mechanical advantage: MA = d_effort / d_load = F_load / F_effort. Push with 100 N at 2 m from the fulcrum and you can lift 400 N at 0.5 m — an MA of 4. You move your end 4× farther; the load moves 4× less. Force and distance trade off. The product F × d is the same at both ends — torque balance. Every lever in the world works on this one principle.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Three classes of levers, classified by the relative positions of fulcrum (F), load (L), and effort (E). <strong>Class 1 (FLE):</strong> fulcrum in the middle — seesaw, crowbar, scissors. Can have MA > 1, MA = 1, or MA < 1 depending on arm lengths. <strong>Class 2 (FEL):</strong> load in the middle — wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener. Always MA > 1 (effort arm > load arm). A wheelbarrow: the wheel is the fulcrum, the load is in the bin (close to the wheel), your hands are on the handles (far from the wheel). You lift a heavy load with modest upward force — the trade-off is that your hands travel farther than the load rises. <strong>Class 3 (FLE with E in middle):</strong> effort in the middle — tweezers, fishing rod, human forearm. Always MA < 1 — the effort arm is shorter than the load arm. Your bicep pulls ~5 cm from the elbow to lift a weight 30 cm from the elbow. MA ≈ 5/30 = 1/6 — you must pull with 6× the weight's force. Why would nature design such an inefficient lever? Because a small muscle contraction produces a large hand movement — speed and range of motion, not force, is the priority. Tweezers: a small squeeze at the middle produces a precise grip at the tip. Class 3 levers trade force for speed and precision.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>lever</strong> is a rigid bar that rotates about a fixed point (fulcrum). <strong>Mechanical advantage</strong>: <strong>MA = F_load / F_effort = d_effort / d_load</strong>. MA > 1: force multiplier (small effort lifts large load). MA < 1: speed/distance multiplier (small effort movement produces large load movement). MA = 1: direction changer only. The lever principle follows directly from torque equilibrium about the fulcrum: F_effort × d_effort = F_load × d_load. The three classes: Class 1 (F-L-E), Class 2 (F-E-L actually load in middle), Class 3 (effort in middle).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Levers are the simplest machines and the most ubiquitous. Every joint in your body is a lever system. Your neck is a Class 1 lever — the skull's centre of mass is forward of the spine (fulcrum), and the neck muscles at the back pull down to keep your head from nodding forward. Your jaw is a Class 2 or Class 3 lever depending on which teeth you're using and which muscle group dominates. A nutcracker (Class 2) multiplies your grip force enormously. A pair of pliers combines two Class 1 levers with a shared fulcrum. Bolt cutters use compound levers — one lever drives another — to achieve astonishing MA, cutting through hardened steel with moderate hand force. The cost is always the same: distance. Your hands move ten centimetres; the cutting edges move one millimetre. Force × distance is conserved (ignoring friction). The lever doesn't create work — it redistributes it across a different distance and force.</p>

## BB-NEW-294 — Gyroscopes and precession: when angular momentum fights back

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** gyroscope; precession; torque-on-spinning-body; angular-momentum-vector | **Ground:** g2 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-290, BB-NEW-292, BB-NEW-284]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Spin a wheel, hold the axle horizontal, and try to tip it. It doesn't tip the way you expect. It turns — precesses — at right angles to your push. A gyroscope seems to defy gravity, staying horizontal when it should fall. It's not defying physics; it's obeying angular momentum with absolute fidelity. τ = dL/dt. The torque you apply changes the direction of L, and the gyroscope moves in the direction of the torque, not the force. This is precession — one of the most counterintuitive and beautiful phenomena in classical mechanics.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A bicycle wheel is mounted on an axle with handles. Spin the wheel to high speed (L large, horizontal). Hold one handle and let go of the other. Gravity produces a torque about the held handle — you expect the wheel to tip down. Instead, it precesses — the axle rotates horizontally around the pivot. Why? L is a vector pointing along the axle. The torque from gravity is perpendicular to L (horizontal, perpendicular to the axle). τ = dL/dt means the CHANGE in L is in the direction of τ. Since torque is perpendicular to L, the change in L is perpendicular to L — it ROTATES L rather than changing its magnitude. The axle sweeps out a horizontal circle. This is precession. The precession angular velocity: Ω = τ/L = mgr/(Iω). The faster the wheel spins (larger ω, larger L), the slower the precession. The heavier the wheel or longer the axle (larger τ), the faster the precession.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>When a torque τ⃗ is applied to a spinning body with angular momentum L⃗, and τ⃗ is perpendicular to L⃗, the angular momentum vector precesses — its tip traces a circle. The <strong>precession angular velocity</strong> is <strong>Ω = τ / L</strong> (for τ perpendicular to L). The direction of precession: L⃗ chases τ⃗ (Ω⃗ is perpendicular to both L⃗ and τ⃗). This follows directly from τ = dL/dt: in time dt, dL = τ dt — a small vector in the direction of τ, added perpendicularly to L, rotating L without changing |L|. <strong>Nutation</strong> is the wobbling (nodding) motion that accompanies precession when the torque is not perfectly steady or the initial conditions include a small tilt.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Gyroscopic precession is everywhere in rotating systems. The Earth precesses: its axis traces a circle in the sky every ~26,000 years, because the Sun and Moon's gravity torques on the Earth's equatorial bulge. Right now Polaris is the North Star; in 12,000 years, Vega will be. A spinning top: as it slows (ω decreases, L decreases), precession speeds up (Ω = τ/L increases) until the top wobbles and falls — the familiar death spiral. A rifle bullet is spin-stabilised: the rifling imparts enormous spin (ω ~ 3000 rev/s). Aerodynamic torques that would tumble a non-spinning bullet instead cause precession — the bullet's axis stays roughly aligned with its trajectory. Motorcycles: at speed, the spinning wheels provide gyroscopic stability that helps keep the bike upright. You steer a motorcycle at speed not by turning the handlebars the way you want to go, but by countersteering — a torque input that precesses the bike into a lean. Gyroscopes are used in inertial navigation systems on submarines, spacecraft, and aircraft — a spinning mass maintains its orientation in inertial space, providing a reference direction independent of GPS or magnetic compass.</p>

## BB-NEW-295 — The complete mechanics toolkit: F = ma and τ = Iα, working together

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** mechanics-toolkit; translation-plus-rotation; problem-solving-strategy; review | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-289, BB-NEW-283, BB-NEW-291]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You now have the complete classical mechanics toolkit. For translation: ΣF = ma, with F = ma as the special case for constant mass. For rotation: Στ = Iα. For combined motion: both equations, linked by constraints like a = αR for rolling or by tension in connected-body systems. Plus conservation laws: energy, linear momentum, angular momentum. This toolkit — Newton's laws in both linear and rotational form, plus the three great conservation laws — solves essentially every problem in classical mechanics. The art is choosing the right tool for the problem, and knowing when to switch.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Problem: A solid cylinder (M, R) rolls without slipping down an incline (θ), pulled by a string wound around its axle. The string goes over a frictionless pulley and attaches to a hanging mass m. Find the acceleration. Strategy: Three objects — cylinder, hanging mass, pulley (massless here, so no rotational dynamics for the pulley). For the hanging mass (translating): mg − T = ma. For the cylinder (translating + rotating): Translation along slope: T − Mg sin θ − f = Ma_COM. Rotation about COM: τ = fR = Iα = ½MR²α. Constraint (rolling): a_COM = αR. Constraint (connected): a = a_COM (string doesn't stretch). Four equations, four unknowns (a, T, f, α). Solve: from rotation, f = ½MRα = ½MR(a_COM/R) = ½Ma. Substitute into translation: T − Mg sin θ − ½Ma = Ma → T = Mg sin θ + (3/2)Ma. Substitute into hanging mass equation: mg − [Mg sin θ + (3/2)Ma] = ma → mg − Mg sin θ = (m + 3M/2)a → a = g(m − M sin θ)/(m + 3M/2). If m > M sin θ, the system accelerates with the hanging mass descending. If the cylinder's mass were concentrated at the rim (I = MR²), the denominator would be m + 2M instead of m + 1.5M — the hoop accelerates slower. The rotational inertia directly affects the translational acceleration. Every choice of shape (solid cylinder, hoop, sphere) changes the result through I. This is the power of the toolkit — F = ma for each translating object, τ = Iα for each rotating object, constraints linking them, solve simultaneously.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The complete classical mechanics problem-solving strategy: (1) Identify EVERY object. (2) For each translating object, draw FBD, write ΣF = ma (vector equation, 2 or 3 scalar equations). (3) For each rotating object, compute I about the rotation axis, write Στ = Iα. (4) Identify constraints: strings (a₁ = a₂), pulleys (a = αR for pulley rotation, or T₁ = T₂ for ideal pulley), rolling (v = ωR → a = αR). (5) Count equations and unknowns — they must match. (6) Solve simultaneously. (7) Check limiting cases (mass → 0, angle → 0, friction → 0) to verify the result makes physical sense. The conservation laws (energy, momentum, angular momentum) are shortcuts when applicable — they reduce equation count. But when in doubt, F = ma and τ = Iα always work.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>This toolkit scales from a single block on a table to the most complex machinery. An engine: pistons translate, crankshaft rotates, connected by a linkage constraint. A robot arm: each joint has a motor providing torque τ = Iα, while the arm's links translate. A satellite: translation of COM under gravity, rotation about COM under thruster torques — two independent sets of equations. The hardest part is never the equations — it's the geometry of the constraints. What's connected to what? Which way does the string pull? Which way does friction act? That's why the FBD is always step one — if you get the arrows right, the equations follow mechanically. And if you're stuck, try the energy method — it avoids forces and torques entirely by tracking energy conversions. The toolkit is not a list to memorise. It's a problem-solving rhythm: isolate, draw forces, write equations, link constraints, solve, check. Every mechanics problem, from a falling apple to a Mars rover, uses the same rhythm. The physics doesn't change — only the complexity of the system does.</p>
