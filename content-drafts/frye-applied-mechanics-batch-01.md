# Frye's Applied Physics → Qubix BBs — Batch 01: Applied Mechanics (15 BBs)

These 15 BBs are drawn from Royal M. Frye's *Essentials of Applied Physics* (1947, Chapters 4–6), a textbook designed for engineering students who needed practical, hands-on physics. Frye's approach: start with a machine you can touch, find the principle that makes it work, then do the numbers. Every BB opens with something observable — a crowbar, a dam, a bridge cable — and builds the physics from there.

The tone is deliberately gentler than Verma. If Verma is the rigorous theorist, Frye is the engineer who shows you how it works with a sketch on a napkin. First-time users will find these accessible; returning users will find them grounding.

**Already covered (don't duplicate):** BB-NEW-26–30 (forces), BB-NEW-36–38 (friction), BB-NEW-43–47 (work/energy/power), BB-NEW-48 (centre of mass), BB-NEW-284–295 (torque/rotational), BB-NEW-632 (Bernoulli in calculus context), BB-NEW-645 (hydrostatic force integral).

**What's new here:** Mechanical advantage, efficiency, the six simple machines, Pascal's principle and the hydraulic press, Archimedes' principle and buoyancy, Bernoulli's principle (qualitative, no calculus), stress and strain as a ratio, Young's modulus as a material property, ultimate strength and the safety factor, Hooke's law in its full engineering form.

## BB-NEW-700 — Why a crowbar works: the lever

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** lever; mechanical-advantage; fulcrum; effort-and-load | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-284, BB-NEW-43]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You already know how a crowbar works. You wedge it under a heavy rock, push down on the long end, and the rock lifts. You don't need physics to use one — but physics tells you exactly how much easier the job gets. A lever trades distance for force: push a long way with a small force, and the short end moves a short way with a big force. The ratio is the mechanical advantage.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>You use a 1.5 m crowbar to lift a 300 kg rock. The fulcrum (the pivot point) is 0.15 m from the rock. The effort arm (your end to the fulcrum) is 1.35 m. The load arm (rock to fulcrum) is 0.15 m. The mechanical advantage = effort arm ÷ load arm = 1.35 ÷ 0.15 = 9. So you only need to push with 300 ÷ 9 = 33.3 kg of force — about the weight of a large bag of dog food. Without the lever, you'd need to lift the full 300 kg. The price: you have to push your end down 9 cm to lift the rock 1 cm. Force × distance is conserved — the work in equals the work out (ignoring friction).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>lever</strong> is a rigid bar that rotates around a fixed point — the <strong>fulcrum</strong>. The <strong>mechanical advantage</strong> (MA) is the ratio of the output force to the input force: MA = F_out / F_in. For an ideal lever (no friction): MA = effort arm / load arm. Three classes: Class 1 (fulcrum between effort and load — crowbar, seesaw), Class 2 (load between fulcrum and effort — wheelbarrow, bottle opener), Class 3 (effort between fulcrum and load — tweezers, cricket bat). Class 3 levers <em>reduce</em> force but increase speed and range — your forearm is a Class 3 lever, and that's why you can throw a ball fast.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Everyday levers include scissors (two Class 1 levers joined at the fulcrum), a bottle opener (Class 2 — the fulcrum is the far edge of the cap, the load is the cap itself), and your own jaw (the temporomandibular joint is a Class 3 lever with the muscle providing the effort between the joint and the teeth). The principle scales: a construction crane is a lever system; the boom is the effort arm, the counterweight balances the load. The ancient Egyptians used levers to position stones weighing tonnes. Archimedes, who formalised the lever principle around 250 BC, famously said: "Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth." He was exaggerating — the effort arm would need to be quadrillions of kilometres long — but the principle was sound.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a crowbar lifting a rock. The fulcrum (triangle) marked. The effort arm and load arm labelled with distances. Force arrows: small downward force at the long end, large upward force at the short end. Equation: MA = effort arm / load arm = 9. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-701 — Pulleys: trading rope for muscle

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** pulley; block-and-tackle; mechanical-advantage-pulley | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-700, BB-NEW-43]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A single pulley just changes the direction of your pull — no mechanical advantage. Add a second pulley, and suddenly you can lift twice the weight with the same effort. A third pulley triples it. Pulleys multiply force by sharing the load across multiple rope segments. Every extra strand of rope supporting the load is an extra helper sharing the weight.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>You need to lift a 200 kg engine block. With a simple pulley hung from the ceiling: you pull down on one rope, the engine goes up — MA = 1, no advantage, just a direction change. With two pulleys (one at the ceiling, one attached to the engine): the rope goes from ceiling to engine to ceiling to your hand. The engine is now supported by <em>two</em> rope segments, so each carries half the weight. You pull with 100 kg of force, but you have to pull 2 metres of rope to lift the engine 1 metre. With a four-pulley block-and-tackle: four rope segments support the load, MA = 4, you pull 4 metres to lift 1 metre. The trade is always the same: less force for more rope.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>pulley</strong> is a wheel on an axle designed to support movement of a rope or cable. A <strong>fixed pulley</strong> changes direction only (MA = 1). A <strong>movable pulley</strong> is attached to the load and provides mechanical advantage equal to the number of rope segments supporting the load. The <strong>block and tackle</strong> combines multiple fixed and movable pulleys. For an ideal system (no friction): MA = number of supporting rope strands. With friction, actual MA is less. The <strong>velocity ratio</strong> (VR) is the distance the effort moves divided by the distance the load moves — always equal to the number of rope strands. Efficiency = MA / VR × 100%. Pulleys are levers in disguise: each pulley redirects the rope tension, effectively giving you multiple "effort arms" working simultaneously.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Cranes use multi-pulley systems to lift tonnes with a manageable motor. A theatre's fly system (the ropes and pulleys above the stage) lets a single stagehand raise and lower heavy backdrops. Elevators use pulleys and counterweights — the counterweight balances the car, so the motor only has to overcome the difference. Sailing ships use pulleys (blocks) everywhere — the mainsheet, the halyard, the boom vang. The phrase "block and tackle" comes from sailing. In rock climbing, a Z-pulley system (three pulleys arranged in a Z-shape) gives MA = 3 and can haul an injured climber out of a crevasse using only human muscle. The oldest pulley systems date to Archimedes and the ancient Greeks.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a block and tackle with four rope segments supporting the load. Each rope segment labelled "T." Force arrows: small downward pull (F_in), large upward lift (F_out = 4 × F_in). Equation: MA = number of supporting strands = 4. A single fixed pulley beside it for comparison (MA = 1). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-702 — The inclined plane and the screw

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** inclined-plane; screw; thread-pitch; mechanical-advantage-ramp | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-700, BB-NEW-36]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Pushing a heavy barrel up a ramp is easier than lifting it straight up. The longer and gentler the ramp, the less force you need. A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder — every turn of the screw advances it by one thread's width. Both trade distance for force: push further, push easier.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>You roll a 100 kg barrel onto a truck bed 1.2 m high using a 4.8 m ramp. Without the ramp: you'd have to lift 100 kg straight up 1.2 m — work = 100 × 9.8 × 1.2 = 1176 J. With the ramp: the force needed to push it up (ignoring friction) = weight × height / ramp length = 980 × 1.2 / 4.8 = 245 N — about 25 kg of effort. MA = ramp length / height = 4.8 / 1.2 = 4. You push four times further, but with a quarter of the force.</p>
<p>A screw with pitch 2 mm (the distance between adjacent threads) turned by a screwdriver handle of radius 20 mm: in one full turn, the screwdriver rim travels 2π × 20 = 125.7 mm, while the screw advances only 2 mm. MA = distance effort moves / distance load moves = 125.7 / 2 ≈ 63. A screw gives enormous mechanical advantage — which is why a small screw can hold hundreds of kilograms.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>An <strong>inclined plane</strong> gives MA = length of slope / height. The shallower the angle, the greater the MA. A <strong>screw</strong> is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder: MA = 2πr / pitch, where r is the handle radius and pitch is the distance between adjacent threads. The <strong>jackscrew</strong> (car jack) combines a lever (the handle) with a screw — total MA = (2π × handle length / pitch) × (any lever advantage). The <strong>wedge</strong> is a double inclined plane — an axe splits wood by converting a downward blow into outward force. All three are "force multipliers" that convert a small input force applied over a long distance into a large output force over a short distance.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The screw is everywhere. A C-clamp uses a screw to exert hundreds of kilograms of clamping force from a few finger-turns. A car jack lifts a tonne with a handle you can turn with one hand. Wood screws and machine bolts hold your furniture, your bicycle, and the wings on an aeroplane together — each one is a tiny inclined plane converting torque into immense axial force. The thread pitch determines the tradeoff: fine threads (small pitch) give higher MA but are slower; coarse threads (large pitch) are faster but hold less. The Romans used wooden screws to drain mines; Archimedes' screw, invented around 250 BC, is still used today to pump water in irrigation systems.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a barrel on a ramp. The ramp length L, height h, and weight W labelled. The force needed to push = W × h / L. Beside it: a screw with handle radius r and thread pitch p. One full turn: handle moves 2πr, screw advances p. Equation: MA = 2πr/p. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-703 — Efficiency: why some machines waste your effort

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** efficiency; friction-loss; output-over-input; percent-efficiency | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-700, BB-NEW-701, BB-NEW-47]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>No machine is perfect. Some of the work you put in is always lost — to friction, to heat, to bending and stretching. Efficiency tells you how much of your input actually becomes useful output. A 100% efficient machine is impossible; a 90% efficient machine is excellent; a 50% efficient machine wastes half your effort. The rest becomes heat.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>You use a pulley system to lift a 500 N weight. The theoretical MA is 4, so the ideal effort is 500/4 = 125 N. But when you pull, you actually need 160 N — the extra 35 N overcomes friction in the pulley bearings and the stiffness of the rope. Efficiency = (ideal effort / actual effort) × 100% = (125/160) × 100% = 78%. Alternatively: output work = 500 N × 1 m = 500 J (if you lift 1 m). Input work = 160 N × 4 m = 640 J (because MA = 4, you pull 4 m of rope). Efficiency = output/input × 100% = 500/640 × 100% = 78%. Same number, two routes. The 140 J difference becomes heat — you can feel it if you touch the pulley after heavy use.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Efficiency</strong> η = (useful work output / total work input) × 100% = (actual MA / ideal MA) × 100%. For any machine: η < 100% because of friction. In a series of machines (compound machine): total efficiency = η₁ × η₂ × η₃ × ... — losses multiply. For a lever, efficiency is typically 90–98% (very high, only pivot friction). For a screw jack, 30–50% (high friction between threads). For a car engine, ~25–30% (most energy lost as heat in the exhaust and coolant). The second law of thermodynamics guarantees that some energy is always degraded into unusable heat — 100% efficiency is forbidden by physics, not just by engineering.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Efficiency determines fuel economy. A petrol engine at 25% efficiency means 75% of the energy in every litre of fuel becomes heat, not motion. An electric motor is 85–95% efficient — one reason EVs use less energy per kilometre. A bicycle chain drive is ~98% efficient — almost all your leg power reaches the wheel. An incandescent light bulb is ~5% efficient at producing visible light; an LED is ~40%. The entire industrial revolution was a story of improving efficiency — Watt's steam engine (1776) was about 3% efficient; modern combined-cycle gas turbines exceed 60%. Efficiency is the accountant of physics: it tells you where the energy really went.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a pulley system with input and output labelled. Input: F_in × d_in = 640 J. Output: F_out × d_out = 500 J. The gap (140 J) is heat, labelled "lost to friction." Efficiency formula η = output/input × 100% = 78%. A small graph showing input, output, and loss as bars. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-704 — The hydraulic press: multiplying force with fluid

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** hydraulic-press; Pascal-principle; pressure-transmission; force-multiplication | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-700, BB-NEW-43]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Push on a small piston with your hand, and a large piston on the other side lifts a car. No levers, no pulleys — just fluid in a sealed container. Pascal's principle: pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally in all directions. A small force on a small area creates a pressure; that same pressure on a large area creates a large force.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A hydraulic press has a small piston of diameter 2 cm (area = π × 1² ≈ 3.14 cm²) and a large piston of diameter 20 cm (area = π × 10² ≈ 314 cm²). You push the small piston with 100 N (about 10 kg). The pressure created: P = F/A = 100 N / 3.14 cm² = 31.8 N/cm². This same pressure acts on the large piston: F_out = P × A_large = 31.8 × 314 ≈ 10,000 N — enough to lift about 1000 kg. The mechanical advantage = A_large / A_small = 314 / 3.14 = 100. But you have to push the small piston 100 cm to lift the large piston 1 cm. Volume is conserved: A₁ × d₁ = A₂ × d₂.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Pascal's principle:</strong> Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the container. For a hydraulic system with two pistons: P₁ = P₂ → F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂ → F₂ = F₁ × (A₂/A₁). The <strong>mechanical advantage</strong> = A₂/A₁ (area ratio). The <strong>distance trade:</strong> d₂ = d₁ × (A₁/A₂). The <strong>volume</strong> of fluid displaced on one side equals the volume received on the other: A₁d₁ = A₂d₂. The fluid is typically oil (incompressible, lubricates, doesn't corrode). Hydraulic systems are force multipliers that use fluid instead of rigid linkages — they can transmit force around corners and through flexible hoses.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Hydraulics are everywhere force needs to be multiplied. A car brake system: your foot pushes a small master cylinder piston (~2 cm²); the pressure travels through brake fluid to calliper pistons at each wheel (~20 cm² total), multiplying the force roughly 10×. A hydraulic car jack lifts a tonne with a few pumps of a small lever. Excavators and bulldozers use hydraulic rams — the engine drives a pump, and the pressurised fluid moves pistons that can exert tonnes of force. Aircraft landing gear, suspension bridges, and even your office chair (the height adjustment) use hydraulics. The principle scales: the largest hydraulic press ever built, at the Shibaura plant in Japan, exerts 80,000 tonnes of force — enough to forge a ship's propeller shaft from a single steel ingot.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of two connected cylinders — one small (diameter 2 cm), one large (diameter 20 cm). A hand pushing down on the small piston with 100 N. The large piston lifting 10,000 N. The pressure P labelled in the fluid as equal everywhere. Equation: F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂. Distance trade: small piston moves 100 cm, large moves 1 cm. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-705 — Archimedes' principle: why things float

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** Archimedes-principle; buoyancy; displaced-fluid; upthrust | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-26]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Put a stone in water and it sinks. Put a log in water and it floats. The difference is density — but the mechanism is buoyancy. Any object submerged in a fluid experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid it pushes aside. If that upthrust is greater than the object's weight, it floats. If not, it sinks. Archimedes figured this out in his bathtub around 250 BC — and according to legend, ran naked through the streets shouting "Eureka!"</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A block of wood 0.30 m × 0.20 m × 0.10 m (volume = 0.006 m³) is placed in water (density ρ = 1000 kg/m³). When fully submerged, it displaces 0.006 m³ of water. The weight of displaced water = ρVg = 1000 × 0.006 × 9.8 = 58.8 N. This is the buoyant force. The wood's mass is 3.6 kg (density 600 kg/m³), so its weight is 3.6 × 9.8 = 35.3 N. Since buoyant force (58.8 N) > weight (35.3 N), the wood rises until it's only partially submerged — at equilibrium, the submerged volume is just enough to displace water weighing 35.3 N. Submerged fraction = density_object / density_fluid = 600/1000 = 0.60 — so 60% of the block is underwater, 40% above.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Archimedes' principle:</strong> A body wholly or partially immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. F_buoyant = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g. The object floats if ρ_object < ρ_fluid; sinks if ρ_object > ρ_fluid; is neutrally buoyant (stays at any depth) if ρ_object = ρ_fluid. The fraction submerged = ρ_object / ρ_fluid. The principle applies to gases as well — a helium balloon rises because helium is less dense than air. The buoyant force acts through the <strong>centre of buoyancy</strong> (the centroid of the displaced volume), which may not coincide with the centre of gravity — this offset creates the righting moment that keeps ships upright.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Archimedes' principle governs everything that floats. A cargo ship weighing 200,000 tonnes displaces 200,000 tonnes of seawater — the hull is shaped to spread the displacement over a huge volume. Submarines adjust buoyancy by flooding or blowing ballast tanks, changing their average density. Hot-air balloons rise because heated air is less dense than cold air. Icebergs float with about 90% of their volume submerged — because ice (917 kg/m³) is only slightly less dense than seawater (1025 kg/m³). The human body, with lungs full of air, is slightly less dense than water (about 985 kg/m³) — most people float. Exhale completely, and you'll sink. The specific gravity test (comparing density to water) was allegedly how Archimedes proved a crown was not pure gold — the goldsmith had mixed in silver, which is less dense, so the crown displaced more water than pure gold of the same weight.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a wooden block partially submerged in water. The weight W pulling down, the buoyant force F_b up. The submerged volume V_sub marked. Equation: F_b = ρ_fluid × V_sub × g. A ship with huge displaced volume beside a sinking stone. The fraction submerged = ρ_object/ρ_fluid = 0.60. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-706 — Bernoulli's principle: why planes fly and chimneys draw

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** Bernoulli-principle; pressure-velocity-relationship; fluid-dynamics | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-704, BB-NEW-43]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Blow across the top of a piece of paper, and it lifts. Squeeze a garden hose nozzle, and the water shoots faster but with less pressure against the walls. Bernoulli's principle: where a fluid moves faster, its pressure drops. This is not a separate law — it's conservation of energy applied to fluid flow: kinetic energy + pressure energy + potential energy = constant along a streamline.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Water flows through a horizontal pipe that narrows from diameter 10 cm (area A₁ = 78.5 cm²) to diameter 5 cm (A₂ = 19.6 cm²). Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂, so if v₁ = 2 m/s, then v₂ = 2 × (78.5/19.6) = 8 m/s — four times faster. Bernoulli: P₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ½ρv₂² (horizontal, same height). With ρ = 1000 kg/m³: P₁ + ½(1000)(4) = P₂ + ½(1000)(64). P₁ + 2000 = P₂ + 32,000. P₁ − P₂ = 30,000 Pa — the pressure in the wide section is 30 kPa higher. If P₁ is atmospheric (101 kPa), P₂ is 71 kPa — low enough to pull fluid from a side tube. This is how a Venturi meter measures flow rate: the pressure difference tells you the speed.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Bernoulli's principle:</strong> For steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant. The three terms are pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy — all per unit volume (J/m³). <strong>Consequences:</strong> as velocity increases, pressure decreases (and vice versa). The principle explains: aerofoil lift (faster air over the curved top → lower pressure → upward force), the curveball (spinning ball drags air faster on one side → pressure difference → sideways force), the atomiser (fast air across a tube draws liquid up), and chimney draft (wind across the top lowers pressure, drawing smoke up). <strong>Limitations:</strong> Bernoulli ignores viscosity and compressibility — real fluids have friction, and at high speeds, compressibility matters.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Bernoulli explains why your shower curtain billows inward when you turn on the water — the fast-moving water-air mixture has lower pressure than the still air outside. It explains why a roof can lift off in a hurricane — wind racing over the roof creates low pressure above, and the higher pressure inside pushes the roof upward. Pitot tubes on aircraft measure airspeed by comparing stagnation pressure (½ρv²) to static pressure — a direct application of Bernoulli. In medicine, a Venturi mask delivers precise oxygen concentrations by using a fast oxygen jet to entrain room air. The principle is one of the most elegant in physics: three forms of energy — pressure, motion, height — trading off against each other while their sum never changes.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a pipe narrowing from wide (A₁, v₁ slow, P₁ high) to narrow (A₂, v₂ fast, P₂ low). Pressure gauges on each section showing the drop. The Bernoulli equation: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant. A small aerofoil cross-section with faster air over the curved top, slower underneath, pressure arrows showing lift. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-707 — Stress and strain: the language of materials

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** stress; strain; tensile; compressive; shear | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-26, BB-NEW-30]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Pull on a rubber band and it stretches. Pull on a steel rod and it stretches too — just so little you can't see it. Every material deforms under force. Stress is the force per unit area; strain is the resulting deformation as a fraction of the original dimension. Stress is the cause; strain is the effect. The ratio tells you how stiff the material is.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A steel cable 5.0 m long, cross-sectional area 2.0 cm² (2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²), supports a 2000 kg load. The tensile stress = F/A = (2000 × 9.8) / (2.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 19,600 / 2.0×10⁻⁴ = 9.8 × 10⁷ Pa = 98 MPa. The steel stretches by 2.45 mm (measured). Tensile strain = ΔL/L₀ = 0.00245 / 5.0 = 4.9 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.049%. Compare with a rubber band of the same dimensions under the same load: the rubber stretches by maybe 2 metres — strain ≈ 0.4 = 40%, about 800 times more. The stress is the same; the strain is dramatically different because steel is far stiffer.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Stress</strong> σ = F/A (units: pascal, Pa = N/m²). Three types: <strong>tensile</strong> (pulling apart), <strong>compressive</strong> (pushing together), <strong>shear</strong> (sliding layers past each other). <strong>Strain</strong> ε is dimensionless: tensile strain = ΔL/L₀ (increase in length / original length), compressive strain = ΔV/V₀ (decrease in volume / original volume), shear strain = x/y (displacement / perpendicular distance). Stress and strain are related by the material's stiffness — see BB 708 for the modulus. For most materials at small strains, stress ∝ strain (Hooke's law). Units note: 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa ≈ 10 atmospheres of pressure. Atmospheric pressure is about 0.1 MPa; structural steel yields around 250 MPa; diamond can withstand over 100 GPa in compression.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Every structure is a stress-and-strain problem. The cables of a suspension bridge are under tensile stress — they're being pulled apart. The columns of a building are under compressive stress — they're being crushed. The rivets and bolts are under shear stress — they're being sliced. Engineers calculate the maximum stress each component will experience and choose a material that can handle it with a safety margin. The human body has its own stress limits: bone (compressive strength ~170 MPa), tendon (tensile strength ~50–100 MPa), skin (~5–20 MPa). When you run, your tibia experiences compressive stresses of about 5–10 MPa — well within its limit. A hard landing from a jump can briefly reach 50 MPa, which is why stress fractures happen.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a rod being pulled (tensile), pushed (compressive), and sheared (sliding). Each shows the force arrows, the original dimension, and the deformation. Stress = F/A, strain = ΔL/L₀ labelled for each. Units: Pa = N/m². Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-708 — Young's modulus: why steel is stiffer than rubber

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** Youngs-modulus; elastic-modulus; stiffness; Hookes-law-engineering | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-707, BB-NEW-30]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Steel is stiff. Rubber is floppy. The number that captures this is Young's modulus — the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region. A high modulus means the material barely deforms under load. A low modulus means it stretches easily. Young's modulus is a material property, like density or melting point — it doesn't depend on the shape or size of the object.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A steel rod (A = 1.0 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m², L = 2.0 m) is loaded with 500 kg. F = 500 × 9.8 = 4900 N. Stress = 4900 / 10⁻⁴ = 4.9 × 10⁷ Pa. Measured stretch = 0.49 mm. Strain = 0.00049 / 2.0 = 2.45 × 10⁻⁴. Young's modulus E = stress / strain = 4.9×10⁷ / 2.45×10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa = 200 GPa. That's steel.</p>
<p>Compare common materials (E in GPa): Diamond = 1200, Steel = 200, Aluminium = 70, Glass = 70, Wood (along grain) = 10, Bone = 15, Nylon = 3, Rubber = 0.01–0.1. Steel is 2000–20,000 times stiffer than rubber. A steel wire 1 mm² under 10 N stretches 0.05%; a rubber band of the same dimensions would stretch 10,000% — impossible, it would snap first.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Young's modulus</strong> E (also called the elastic modulus or tensile modulus) is the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the linear elastic region: E = σ/ε = (F/A)/(ΔL/L₀). Units: Pa (typically GPa for structural materials). <strong>Hooke's law in engineering form:</strong> F/A = E × (ΔL/L₀), or equivalently F = (EA/L₀) × ΔL. The term EA/L₀ is the <strong>spring constant</strong> k of the specific specimen — unlike E, it depends on geometry. A thick, short rod is stiffer than a thin, long one of the same material. Young's modulus applies only in the elastic region, where removing the load returns the material to its original length. Beyond the yield point, the material deforms permanently (plastic deformation).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Young's modulus determines material selection in every engineering design. Aircraft wings are aluminium (E = 70 GPa) — stiff enough to hold shape, light enough to fly. A bicycle frame is often steel or carbon fibre — the tube diameter and wall thickness are chosen to give the right stiffness without excess weight. Concrete has E ≈ 30 GPa in compression but is weak in tension — that's why reinforced concrete uses steel rebar (E = 200 GPa) to carry the tensile loads. The Eiffel Tower (wrought iron, E ≈ 190 GPa) was designed so that the wind load produces a maximum stress of about 10% of the material's yield strength — the safety factor is 10. A violin string (steel, E ≈ 200 GPa) is tuned by adjusting its tension: higher tension = higher pitch, but also higher stress — too close to yield and it snaps.</p>

## BB-NEW-709 — Ultimate strength: when things break

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** ultimate-strength; yield-point; breaking-stress; safety-factor | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-707, BB-NEW-708]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every material has a breaking point. Pull hard enough on anything and it snaps. The ultimate strength is the maximum stress a material can withstand before it fails. Engineers design structures to operate at a fraction of this — the safety factor — because real loads are unpredictable, materials have flaws, and failure is usually unacceptable.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A steel bolt of diameter 10 mm (area = π × 5² = 78.5 mm² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ m²) is loaded in tension. Steel's ultimate tensile strength is about 500 MPa. Maximum load before breaking: F_max = σ_ult × A = 500 × 10⁶ × 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ = 39,250 N — about 4 tonnes. With a safety factor of 5, the allowable working load is 39,250 / 5 = 7,850 N (~800 kg). The bolt will hold 800 kg reliably, every day, for decades.</p>
<p>Common ultimate strengths (MPa): Steel (structural) = 400–550, Aluminium alloy = 300–500, Titanium = 900, Copper = 200, Wood (pine, along grain) = 40 in tension, 30 in compression, Concrete = 20–60 in compression (essentially 0 in tension), Glass = 30–100 (but brittle — fails without warning), Kevlar = 3600, Carbon fibre = 4000–7000, Spider silk = 1000–1500 (and also remarkably tough — absorbs energy without snapping).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Ultimate tensile strength</strong> (UTS) is the maximum stress a material can withstand while being stretched before necking and fracture. <strong>Yield strength</strong> is the stress at which permanent (plastic) deformation begins — typically 50–80% of UTS for metals. <strong>Compressive strength</strong> is the maximum stress under compression. Brittle materials (glass, ceramic, concrete) have high compressive strength but low tensile strength and fail suddenly. Ductile materials (steel, copper) stretch and neck before breaking — they give warning. <strong>Safety factor</strong> = ultimate strength / allowable working stress. Typical values: 1.5–2 for aircraft (weight-critical), 3–5 for buildings, 5–10 for elevators and bridges, 10+ for critical components.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The safety factor is why bridges don't collapse. The Golden Gate Bridge's main cables are made of 27,572 individual steel wires, each 5 mm in diameter, with a combined ultimate strength of about 1.5 million tonnes — far above the bridge's own weight plus maximum traffic load. The Titanic's hull steel had a high sulphur content that made it brittle in cold water — the impact with the iceberg produced stresses that exceeded the steel's reduced ultimate strength at −2°C. Modern ship steel is specified for low-temperature toughness. In medicine, hip implants use titanium (UTS ~900 MPa, biocompatible) with a safety factor that accounts for millions of loading cycles over decades — fatigue failure, not just peak stress, becomes the limiting factor.</p>

## BB-NEW-710 — Bulk modulus and shear modulus: the other stiffnesses

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** bulk-modulus; shear-modulus; compressibility; rigidity | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-707, BB-NEW-708]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Young's modulus measures resistance to stretching. But materials can be deformed in other ways — squeezed uniformly from all sides (bulk modulus) or twisted (shear modulus). A diamond is hard to scratch (high hardness) but also hard to compress (high bulk modulus). Jelly is easy to shear (low shear modulus) but resists compression (moderate bulk modulus — it's mostly water).</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p><strong>Bulk modulus B:</strong> Apply uniform pressure ΔP to a material. Its volume shrinks by ΔV. B = −ΔP / (ΔV/V₀). The negative sign makes B positive (pressure increase → volume decrease). For water: B ≈ 2.2 GPa — it takes 2.2 GPa (22,000 atmospheres) to compress water by 1% of its volume. Water is nearly incompressible. For steel: B ≈ 160 GPa. For air at atmospheric pressure: B ≈ 0.00014 GPa — air is 10,000–100,000 times more compressible than liquids and solids.</p>
<p><strong>Shear modulus G:</strong> Apply a shear stress τ = F/A parallel to a face. The material distorts by angle γ (in radians). G = τ/γ. For steel: G ≈ 80 GPa. For rubber: G ≈ 0.001 GPa. For jelly: G ≈ 0.0001 GPa. The three moduli are related: for isotropic materials, E = 2G(1+ν) = 3B(1−2ν), where ν is Poisson's ratio (≈ 0.3 for most metals).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Bulk modulus</strong> B = −V₀ (ΔP/ΔV) measures resistance to uniform compression. The reciprocal 1/B is <strong>compressibility</strong>. <strong>Shear modulus</strong> G (also called rigidity modulus) = τ/γ measures resistance to shape change at constant volume. Both are measured in Pa. A material can be stiff in compression but compliant in shear: cork (B ≈ 0.05 GPa, G ≈ 0.002 GPa) resists volume change (good for bottle stoppers) but shears easily. Liquids and gases have G = 0 — they cannot support a shear stress at rest; they flow. The speed of sound depends on these moduli: c_longitudinal = √((B + 4G/3)/ρ) in solids, c = √(B/ρ) in fluids.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The bulk modulus explains why hydraulic systems use oil, not air — oil's high bulk modulus means the force is transmitted immediately; air would compress and absorb the motion. Submarine hulls must withstand enormous uniform pressure — at 1000 m depth, ΔP ≈ 10 MPa, and the hull's bulk modulus determines how much it shrinks. Seismic waves come in two types: P-waves (compression, governed by B + 4G/3, faster) and S-waves (shear, governed by G, slower). The fact that S-waves don't travel through Earth's outer core tells us it's liquid (G = 0). The shear modulus is also why a steel spring works — it's actually twisting the wire (shear), not stretching it.</p>

Shear modulus: G = τ/γ. A table comparing B and G for steel, water, rubber, and air. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-711 — Boyle's law: squeezing gases

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** Boyles-law; pressure-volume-inverse; isothermal-compression | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-704, BB-NEW-268]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Take a bicycle pump, block the outlet with your thumb, and push. The air resists, and the harder you push, the smaller the trapped air becomes. Boyle's law: for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional. Squeeze the volume to half, and the pressure doubles. Release it, and it springs back. Gases are springs made of molecules.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A cylinder contains 2.0 L of air at atmospheric pressure (101 kPa). The piston is pushed in until the volume is 0.5 L — one quarter of the original. Temperature stays constant (slow compression, heat can escape). Boyle's law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → 101 × 2.0 = P₂ × 0.5 → P₂ = 404 kPa — about 4 atmospheres. The force on the piston (area 10 cm² = 0.001 m²): F = P × A = 404,000 × 0.001 = 404 N — about 41 kg. Your arm has to push that hard. Real compressors get hot — in rapid compression, the gas heats up (adiabatic), and pressure rises even more. Boyle's law assumes temperature stays constant — it's the isothermal case.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Boyle's law:</strong> For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, P ∝ 1/V, or PV = constant. Equivalently: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. The law holds for <strong>ideal gases</strong> and is a good approximation for real gases at moderate pressures and temperatures. It is a consequence of the kinetic theory: pressure comes from molecules colliding with the walls. Halving the volume doubles the number of collisions per unit area per second — hence double the pressure. Boyle discovered the law in 1662 using a J-shaped glass tube and mercury. The law combines with Charles's law (V ∝ T) and Gay-Lussac's law (P ∝ T) to give the ideal gas law PV = nRT.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Boyle's law is why your ears pop on an aeroplane. As the plane climbs, cabin pressure drops; the air trapped in your middle ear expands according to PV = constant, pushing against your eardrum until you swallow and equalise. Scuba divers live by Boyle's law: at 10 m depth, pressure is 2 atmospheres, so a lungful of air occupies half the volume it does at the surface. Ascending while holding your breath would cause the expanding air to rupture your lungs — the most important rule in diving is "never hold your breath." In medicine, a syringe draws fluid by increasing the volume inside (lowering pressure), creating suction. The humble bicycle pump is a Boyle's law machine: muscle force creates pressure; pressure reduces volume; the compressed air flows into the tyre.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a cylinder with a piston. At position 1: V₁ = 2 L, P₁ = 101 kPa. At position 2: V₂ = 0.5 L, P₂ = 404 kPa. The PV curve shown as a hyperbola. Equation: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. A small inset showing molecules in a smaller volume colliding more frequently. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-712 — Pascal's hydrostatic paradox: pressure depends only on depth

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** hydrostatic-pressure; P-equals-rho-gh; independent-of-shape | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-704, BB-NEW-711]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Fill a narrow tube and a wide barrel to the same height with water. The pressure at the bottom is exactly the same in both — even though the barrel contains far more water. Hydrostatic pressure depends only on depth and fluid density, not on the shape or total volume of the container. This is Pascal's paradox — counterintuitive but mathematically inevitable.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A swimming pool 2.0 m deep. At the bottom, gauge pressure P = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 2.0 = 19,600 Pa ≈ 0.19 atmospheres. The total force on the bottom (area 50 m²): F = P × A = 19,600 × 50 = 980,000 N — about 100 tonnes. The force on the side wall at the same 2.0 m depth: same pressure per unit area, but the pressure varies with depth — zero at the surface, maximum at the bottom. The average pressure on a vertical wall is ρgh/2, acting at a point 2h/3 from the surface.</p>
<p>Now the paradox: a narrow vertical pipe 10 m tall, 1 cm² in cross-section, filled with water. Pressure at the bottom: P = 1000 × 9.8 × 10 = 98,000 Pa. Force on the bottom plug: F = 98,000 × 0.0001 = 9.8 N — about 1 kg. But that same pressure, if connected to a wide chamber, could lift a car. The force multiplies with area, but the pressure is set by height alone.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Hydrostatic pressure</strong> at depth h in a fluid of density ρ: P = P₀ + ρgh, where P₀ is the pressure at the surface (usually atmospheric). The ρgh term is the <strong>gauge pressure</strong>. Key fact: pressure at a given depth is <strong>independent of container shape</strong> — it depends only on the vertical distance from the free surface. This follows from the fact that pressure in a static fluid must be the same at all points at the same horizontal level; otherwise, fluid would flow sideways until it equalised. The force on a submerged surface is the integral of pressure over area — for a vertical rectangle, F = ρg × (width) × (h²/2).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Hydrostatic pressure is why dams are thicker at the bottom than at the top — the pressure increases linearly with depth, and the structural requirement follows. A dam 100 m tall experiences gauge pressure of about 1 MPa at its base — roughly 10 atmospheres. Water towers use height, not pumps, to maintain pressure: a tower 30 m tall provides about 300 kPa (3 atm, ~44 psi) at the tap. The city water supply is pressurised by gravity, not electricity — which is why water still flows during a power outage. Deep-sea fish are adapted to pressures of 100 MPa at 10,000 m depth — a thousand atmospheres. Bring them to the surface, and the dissolved gases in their tissues expand according to Boyle's law, killing them. The pressure at the bottom of the Mariana Trench (11 km) would crush a human instantly.</p>

Pressure gauges at the bottom of each showing identical P = ρgh. The paradox: same pressure, vastly different volumes of water. Equation: P = P₀ + ρgh. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-713 — Bending and twisting: why I-beams work

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** bending-of-beams; twisting-of-rods; second-moment-of-area; torsion | **Ground:** g2 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-707, BB-NEW-708, BB-NEW-644]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A wooden plank laid flat bends easily under your weight. Turn it on edge, and it barely flexes at all. Same material, same cross-sectional area — completely different stiffness. Bending resistance depends on the shape of the cross-section, not just its area. An I-beam puts most of its material far from the neutral axis, where it does the most work resisting bending.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A simply supported beam of length L = 3.0 m carries a central load F = 2000 N. Material: steel (E = 200 GPa). Two cross-sections with the same area (20 cm²):</p>
<p>Case A — solid square, 4.47 cm × 4.47 cm. Second moment of area I = bh³/12 = (0.0447)(0.0447)³/12 = 3.33 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴. Maximum deflection: δ = FL³/(48EI) = 2000 × 27 / (48 × 2×10¹¹ × 3.33×10⁻⁷) = 54,000 / 3,196,800 ≈ 0.0169 m = 16.9 mm.</p>
<p>Case B — I-beam, 10 cm deep, flanges 2 cm × 10 cm, web 6 cm × 0.5 cm. I ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ — roughly 13.5 times larger. Deflection: 16.9/13.5 ≈ 1.25 mm. The I-beam is 13× stiffer with the same weight.</p>
<p>For twisting: a solid circular rod vs a hollow tube. The polar moment of area J: solid: J = πr⁴/2. Hollow (same area, larger radius): much larger J. Angle of twist θ = TL/(GJ). Hollow shafts are far stiffer in torsion per unit weight.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Bending</strong> of a beam: the curvature is proportional to the bending moment and inversely proportional to EI — the <strong>flexural rigidity</strong>. E is Young's modulus; I is the <strong>second moment of area</strong> (units: m⁴). I = ∫ y² dA — material far from the neutral axis contributes much more (y² weighting). Maximum deflection for common cases: cantilever with end load = FL³/(3EI); simply supported with centre load = FL³/(48EI). <strong>Torsion</strong> of a shaft: twist angle θ = TL/(GJ), where T is torque, G is shear modulus, J is the polar second moment of area. Hollow shafts maximise J for a given weight — almost all the torsional stiffness comes from the outer region.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Every steel-framed building, bridge, and crane uses I-beams. The cross-section is optimised so precisely that modern beams use about 30% less steel than their 1900 equivalents for the same load. Aircraft wings are hollow — the skin (far from the neutral axis) carries the bending loads; the interior is largely empty. Bicycle frames use oversized thin-walled tubes for the same reason — stiffness scales with diameter cubed. A human long bone (femur) is essentially a hollow cylinder — cortical bone on the outside, marrow within. Evolution discovered the hollow shaft long before engineers did. The Eiffel Tower's iron lattice uses triangulated beams — the diagonal braces carry shear, the verticals carry compression, and the overall shape approximates the bending moment diagram of a vertical cantilever loaded by wind.</p>

The cross-sections shown with I values. Formula: δ = FL³/(48EI). A torsion diagram: solid rod vs hollow tube, J values compared. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-714 — The spring: Hooke's law in practice

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** spring; Hookes-law; spring-constant; series-and-parallel-springs | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-30, BB-NEW-707]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Stretch a spring, and it pulls back. Hooke's law says the restoring force is proportional to the extension: F = kx. Double the stretch, double the force. Every elastic object — a rubber band, a steel beam, a diving board — is a spring at small deformations. The spring constant k tells you how stiff it is. Stiffer spring = larger k.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A spring stretches 5.0 cm when a 2.0 kg mass is hung from it. F = mg = 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N. k = F/x = 19.6 / 0.050 = 392 N/m. If you stretch it 10 cm, it pulls back with 39.2 N. The energy stored: U = ½kx² = ½ × 392 × (0.05)² = 0.49 J. Stretch it twice as far, and the energy quadruples: ½ × 392 × (0.10)² = 1.96 J — exactly 4×.</p>
<p><strong>Springs in series:</strong> Two identical springs (each k = 392 N/m) end to end. Equivalent k = (1/k₁ + 1/k₂)⁻¹ = k/2 = 196 N/m — half as stiff. The same force stretches twice as far. <strong>Springs in parallel:</strong> side by side. Equivalent k = k₁ + k₂ = 784 N/m — twice as stiff. Each spring carries half the load. Resistors in parallel add their conductances; springs in parallel add their stiffnesses.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Hooke's law:</strong> F = kx, where k is the spring constant (N/m) and x is the extension from the equilibrium position. The restoring force is <strong>opposite</strong> to the displacement: F⃗ = −kx⃗. <strong>Elastic potential energy:</strong> U = ½kx². <strong>Springs in series:</strong> 1/k_eq = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂ + ... (like capacitors in series, resistors in parallel). <strong>Springs in parallel:</strong> k_eq = k₁ + k₂ + ... (like capacitors in parallel, resistors in series). Any elastic structure under small deformation behaves as a spring — k = EA/L for a uniform rod in tension, where E is Young's modulus, A is area, L is length. Hooke's law is the small-deformation limit; beyond the elastic limit, it fails.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Springs are everywhere. Your car's suspension uses coil springs (typically k ≈ 20,000–50,000 N/m), with shock absorbers (dampers) to dissipate energy and prevent bouncing. A ballpoint pen's click mechanism uses a tiny spring. A mechanical watch's balance spring (hairspring) oscillates at a precise frequency, keeping time. Truck weigh stations use massive springs — the compression under the truck's weight measures the load. In earthquake engineering, base isolation systems use enormous springs and dampers to decouple a building from ground motion. The simplicity of F = kx hides its universality: from the bonds between atoms (which behave as tiny springs) to the suspension cables of a bridge, the same linear relationship governs.</p>

## BB-NEW-715 — The applied mechanics toolkit

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** applied-mechanics-summary; machines-fluids-materials-synthesis | **Ground:** g1 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-700 through BB-NEW-714]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>After 15 BBs of applied mechanics, here is the unified picture. Every machine — from a crowbar to a hydraulic press to a bridge — trades force for distance, or pressure for volume, or stress for strain. The governing principles are few: mechanical advantage, Pascal's principle, Archimedes' principle, Bernoulli's principle, Hooke's law, and the material moduli. Master these, and you can analyse any structure or machine you encounter.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Test yourself — identify the principles at work in each:</p>
<p>1. A car jack: inclined plane (screw thread) + lever (handle). MA = 2πr/pitch × lever ratio ≈ 100–200. Lifts 1 tonne with 5–10 kg of hand force.</p>
<p>2. An oil tanker floating: Archimedes' principle. Displaces 500,000 tonnes of seawater. Steel hull is hollow — average density < water.</p>
<p>3. A dam: hydrostatic pressure P = ρgh. Force on dam face = ½ρgwh² (w = width). Centre of pressure at 2h/3 from surface.</p>
<p>4. A bicycle frame: Young's modulus (E for aluminium ≈ 70 GPa), bending stiffness (EI), designed for minimum weight at required stiffness.</p>
<p>5. A syringe: Boyle's law (PV = constant) + Pascal's principle (pressure transmits undiminished). Pull plunger → volume increases → pressure drops → fluid drawn in.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Applied mechanics master principles:</strong></p>
<p>• <strong>Mechanical advantage:</strong> MA = F_out / F_in = d_in / d_out (ideal). Real: η = MA_actual / MA_ideal.</p>
<p>• <strong>Pascal:</strong> P = F/A, transmitted equally. Hydraulic MA = A₂/A₁.</p>
<p>• <strong>Archimedes:</strong> F_b = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g. Float if ρ_object < ρ_fluid.</p>
<p>• <strong>Bernoulli:</strong> P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant. Fast flow → low pressure.</p>
<p>• <strong>Hooke:</strong> F = kx, U = ½kx². Stress = E × strain.</p>
<p>• <strong>Materials:</strong> E (stiffness), UTS (strength), safety factor = UTS/working_stress.</p>
<p>• <strong>Beams:</strong> δ ∝ FL³/(EI). I = ∫ y² dA — put mass far from neutral axis.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>This toolkit analyses every mechanical system you'll ever encounter. The Golden Gate Bridge: the cables are in tension (UTS of steel), the towers in compression, the deck is a beam (bending stiffness EI), the foundations resist hydrostatic pressure and buoyancy. A hydraulic excavator: the engine drives a pump (Pascal), the fluid powers rams (hydraulic MA), the boom is a lever (mechanical advantage), the bucket teeth concentrate force on a small area (high pressure = easy digging). The human body: bones are hollow beams (max I for weight), joints are levers (MA < 1 — speed over force), the heart is a pump (Bernoulli + Pascal), the lungs obey Boyle's law. Physics isn't just in textbooks — it's in the crane outside your window and the spring inside your pen.</p>

A hydraulic press connected to a lever, a floating ship, a dam with pressure gradient, an I-beam under load, a spring with F = kx. The grand table of principles: MA, Pascal, Archimedes, Bernoulli, Hooke, E, UTS, I, J. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.
