# Lines & Numbers — Batch 1 (3 BBs for review)

Three foundational BBs introducing the line, the 2D coordinate system, and the types of numbers. These sit before the existing CG Tier 0 cards (Cards 38–70) and before Card 26 ("The infinite tightrope") — they are the geometric primitives and number classifications that everything else builds on.

---

## BB-NEW-53 — The simplest thing you can draw

**Subject:** maths | **Topic:** coordinate-geometry | **Concept:** line; line-segment; ray; number-line | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** []

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A line is the simplest thing you can draw — a perfectly straight path with no thickness and no ends, running forever in both directions. Take a piece of it between two stopping points and you have a line segment. Put numbers on it and you have the number line.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Pull a thread tight between your thumb and forefinger. That is a <strong>line segment</strong> — it starts at one finger and ends at the other. Now imagine the thread extending past your fingers in both directions forever, never bending, never ending. That is a <strong>line</strong>. A laser beam shooting through empty space is close to a line. The edge of a ruler is a line segment with marks on it. A <strong>ray</strong> is halfway between: it has one endpoint and then goes on forever in one direction — like a torch beam starting at the bulb and never stopping.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A line is an infinite set of points extending without end in two opposite directions, with zero thickness and perfect straightness. A <strong>line segment</strong> is the part of a line between two distinct endpoints. A <strong>ray</strong> starts at one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction. The <strong>number line</strong> is a line with a chosen zero point (the origin) and a chosen unit distance. Positive numbers lie to the right, negative numbers to the left. Every real number has exactly one point on the number line and every point has exactly one number.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The number line is the bridge between geometry and arithmetic. "Five is greater than three" becomes "five is further right than three." The distance between two points becomes subtraction: |a − b|. Adding 3 means stepping three units right. Subtracting 2 means stepping two units left. A quantity becomes a place you can point to — and that is the move that unlocks everything that follows. The entire coordinate geometry path starts here: with the realisation that a number is not just a count, it is a position.</p>

Dashed arrows extend beyond both ends labelled "extends forever." Points at −2, 0, 1, and 3 are marked, with −1 and 2 lying between them. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-54 — The second number line

**Subject:** maths | **Topic:** coordinate-geometry | **Concept:** cartesian-plane; x-axis; y-axis; origin; ordered-pair | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-53]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>One number line gives you left and right. That is a one-dimensional world — you can only go forward or backward. Add a second number line, stand it upright, and cross it through the first at zero. Now every point on a flat surface has an exact two-number address.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Finding your seat in a cinema: Row 5, Seat 8. Two numbers pin you to one spot. Row alone isn't enough — a whole row of people don't know which seat is theirs. Seat alone isn't enough — seat 8 exists in every row. The grid does exactly the same thing. The horizontal number tells you how far across. The vertical number tells you how far up. Together, the pair (x, y) is a precise address for any point on a flat surface — no two points share the same pair.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Two number lines crossed at right angles at their shared zero point form a <strong>Cartesian coordinate system</strong> (named after René Descartes). The horizontal line is the <strong>x-axis</strong>; the vertical line is the <strong>y-axis</strong>. Their crossing point is the <strong>origin</strong>, (0, 0). Every point in the plane is identified by an <strong>ordered pair</strong> (x, y) — x always comes first, y second. (3, 4) and (4, 3) are different points. The axes divide the plane into four <strong>quadrants</strong>, numbered I through IV anticlockwise from the top-right: (+,+), (−,+), (−,−), (+,−).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>This is the translation that powers modern mathematics. A shape becomes a set of number pairs. A straight line becomes an equation like y = 2x + 1. A circle becomes x² + y² = r². A parabola becomes y = x². Geometry and algebra stop being separate subjects — they become two languages for the same thing. When a physicist says "a spacecraft is at position (12,000, 8,500, 400)," they are using exactly this idea extended to three dimensions. The grid is not just a school tool — it is how we locate everything, from a pixel on your screen to a planet in the sky.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of two crossed number lines (x horizontal, y vertical) meeting at the origin (0,0). A point at (3,4) is marked with dashed lines running down to the x-axis and across to the y-axis. The four quadrants are labelled I, II, III, IV in pale yellow chalk. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.

## BB-NEW-55 — The cast of numbers

**Subject:** maths | **Topic:** coordinate-geometry | **Concept:** natural-numbers; integers; rational-numbers; irrational-numbers; real-numbers | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-53]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Not all numbers are the same kind. Some are for counting. Some are for measuring. Some are for owing. Some cannot be written down completely, no matter how long you try. Together they make up the full number line — a continuum with no gaps and no holes.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>You count three sheep in a field — that is a <strong>natural number</strong>: 1, 2, 3, and so on. The temperature drops below zero overnight — now you need numbers with a minus sign, the <strong>integers</strong>. You split a pizza equally among four people — each person gets one quarter, a <strong>rational number</strong>: any number you can write as one integer divided by another. Now measure the diagonal of a square tile with sides of exactly 1 metre. Pythagoras says the diagonal is √2 metres. But √2 = 1.414213562... — the decimal never ends and never repeats. It cannot be written as any fraction of two integers. It is an <strong>irrational number</strong>.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Natural numbers</strong> (ℕ) are the counting numbers: 1, 2, 3, ... <strong>Whole numbers</strong> add zero to that set: 0, 1, 2, 3, ... <strong>Integers</strong> (ℤ) add the negatives: ..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ... <strong>Rational numbers</strong> (ℚ) are numbers that can be expressed as p/q where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0. Their decimal expansions either terminate (1/4 = 0.25) or repeat (1/3 = 0.333...). <strong>Irrational numbers</strong> cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers — their decimal expansions never terminate and never repeat. Examples: √2, π, e, the golden ratio φ. <strong>Real numbers</strong> (ℝ) are the union of all rational and all irrational numbers. Every real number has one point on the number line and every point has one real number.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The rationals are dense — between any two rationals, there is always another rational. Between 0 and 1, you have 1/2. Between 0 and 1/2, you have 1/4. This nesting goes on forever. Yet rationals do not fill the line. There are gaps — infinitely many of them — and that is where the irrationals live. At the point √2 on the number line, there is no rational number, only an irrational. Together, rationals and irrationals form the real number line: a perfect continuum with no holes. Every segment of the number line, no matter how tiny, contains infinitely many rationals and infinitely many irrationals. The real numbers are <strong>complete</strong> — a technical term meaning the line has no missing points. This completeness is what makes calculus possible.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk drawing of a horizontal number line from −4 to 4. Natural numbers 1, 2, 3 are marked in pale green chalk. Integers ..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ... are marked in white chalk. The point for 1/2 is marked between 0 and 1. The point √2 ≈ 1.414 is marked in pale yellow chalk with a small right-triangle construction (legs of 1) showing where it comes from. π is marked near 3.14. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.
