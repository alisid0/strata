# Micro-Frye — Batch 03: Electricity Basics (15 BBs)

These 15 BBs break electricity into its smallest teachable units — from "what is charge?" to "how does a circuit work?" No prior knowledge assumed. The approach is relentlessly concrete: charge is like a fluid, voltage is like pressure, current is like flow rate. Every abstraction is grounded in something you can see, feel, or measure with a multimeter.

## BB-NEW-886 — What is electric charge?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** electric-charge; electron; proton; coulomb; attraction-repulsion | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** None — this is the beginning

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Everything is made of atoms. Atoms have a tiny, heavy centre (the nucleus) with protons, and lightweight electrons buzzing around the outside. Protons have a property called positive charge. Electrons have negative charge. Opposite charges attract. Like charges repel. This is the most fundamental fact about electricity. The unit of charge is the coulomb. 1 coulomb is about 6,240,000,000,000,000,000 electrons' worth of charge — a huge number. A single electron's charge is unimaginably small: 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Rub a balloon on your hair. Electrons rub off your hair onto the balloon. The balloon gets extra electrons (negatively charged); your hair loses electrons (positively charged). The balloon then sticks to the wall because its charge polarises the wall's atoms — the positive nuclei are pulled slightly closer. A lightning bolt transfers about 15 coulombs — 15 × 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons — in a fraction of a second. The current in a 60 W bulb carries about 0.25 coulombs per second. In 1 coulomb there are as many electrons as there are grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches combined.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electric charge q</strong> is a fundamental property of matter. Unit: coulomb (C). <strong>Electron charge:</strong> −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. <strong>Proton charge:</strong> +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Charge is quantised — every observable charge is an integer multiple of e (the elementary charge). <strong>Coulomb's Law:</strong> F = k|q₁q₂|/r² — the force between two charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. k = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². This is the electrical twin of Newton's law of gravity, but about 10³⁶ times stronger for particles of the same mass.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of an atom: nucleus (protons +, neutrons), electrons (−) in orbits. A balloon rubbing on hair — electrons transferring. "+ repels +, − repels −, + attracts −." Lightning bolt: "~15 C." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-887 — Current: charge on the move

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** electric-current; ampere; flow-of-charge; I=Q/t | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-886]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When charges move, you have an electric current. Current is the rate of flow of charge — how many coulombs pass a point each second. The unit is the ampere (amp). 1 ampere = 1 coulomb per second. A 60 W light bulb draws about 0.25 amps. A kettle draws 10 amps. A lightning bolt: about 30,000 amps — but only for microseconds. Current is what makes electricity useful: moving charges can do work.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 60 W bulb on a 240 V supply: current = P/V = 60/240 = 0.25 A. This means 0.25 coulombs of charge pass through the bulb every second. A 2 kW kettle: I = 2000/240 = 8.3 A. A phone charger: I = 5/5 = 1 A (at 5 V, so only 5 W). A USB port is typically limited to 0.5 A (USB 2.0) or 0.9 A (USB 3.0). Household wiring in the UK is fused at 13 A in plugs — meaning a single appliance can draw up to 13 A before the fuse blows (13 × 240 = 3120 W). A car battery can deliver 300–500 A briefly to crank the engine — that's 300 coulombs per second.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Current I = Q/t</strong> — charge per unit time. Unit: ampere (A) = C/s. Current is a scalar but is given a direction — conventionally, the direction positive charges would move (opposite to electron flow). In metals, electrons flow; in electrolytes, ions flow; in semiconductors, both can flow. An ammeter measures current and must be connected in series (all the current must pass through it). DC (direct current): flows one way. AC (alternating current): direction reverses periodically (50 or 60 times per second).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a wire with charge carriers. Arrow: I = Q/t. A cross-section with C/s labelled. A bulb circuit: 0.25 A. A kettle: 8.3 A. A lightning bolt: 30,000 A (with "microseconds" note). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-888 — Voltage: the electrical push

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** voltage; potential-difference; emf; volt | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-887]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Current is the flow. Voltage is the push that makes it flow. It's like water pressure in a pipe: high pressure pushes more water through. A 1.5 V AA battery gives a gentle push. A 9 V battery gives a stronger push. The mains (240 V) gives a dangerous push. Voltage is the energy given to each coulomb of charge — 1 volt means 1 joule of energy per coulomb. Higher voltage means each electron does more work.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1.5 V AA battery: each coulomb of charge gets 1.5 joules of energy as it passes through the battery. A 240 V mains socket: each coulomb gets 240 J — 160 times as much. That's why mains electricity is dangerous — the energy per charge is much higher. A 400,000 V power line: each coulomb gets 400,000 J. The same power can be delivered at high voltage with low current, reducing I²R losses — that's why power lines use high voltages. A car battery (12 V): to start the engine, the starter motor might draw 300 A. Power = 12 × 300 = 3600 W = 3.6 kW. Energy per coulomb: 12 J.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Voltage V = W/Q</strong> — work done per unit charge. Unit: volt (V) = J/C. Also called potential difference or electromotive force (emf for batteries/generators). Voltage is a scalar — it tells you the energy difference between two points. Measured with a voltmeter connected in parallel. A 12 V battery does 12 J of work for every coulomb that passes through it. Analogy: voltage ↔ water pressure, current ↔ flow rate, battery ↔ pump. The height of a waterfall is like voltage — the higher the drop, the more energy each litre of water releases.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a battery. One side: "1.5 V — 1.5 J per coulomb." Mains: "240 V — 240 J per coulomb." Power line: "400 kV." Water analogy: small pressure (1.5 V, gentle flow) vs high pressure (240 V, strong flow). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-889 — Resistance: the electrical friction

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** resistance; ohm; resistivity; opposition-to-current | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-887, BB-NEW-888]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Current faces opposition as it flows through a material. This opposition is resistance. Metals (copper, silver) have low resistance — they're good conductors. Plastics and glass have very high resistance — they're insulators. Resistance turns electrical energy into heat. This can be useful (a toaster) or wasteful (overheating wires). The unit is the ohm, symbol Ω (the Greek letter omega).</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A light bulb filament (tungsten): when hot, about 960 Ω (for a 60 W bulb at 240 V — R = V²/P = 240²/60 = 960 Ω). A metre of 2.5 mm² copper wire (domestic cable): about 0.007 Ω — negligible. A 500 m length of the same wire: about 3.5 Ω — enough to lose a few volts. Human body: dry skin, about 100,000 Ω. Wet skin: about 1000 Ω. At 240 V with wet skin, current = 240/1000 = 0.24 A — potentially fatal. A fuse: designed to melt at a specific current — its resistance creates heat (P = I²R), and when I²R exceeds the melting point, the fuse blows, breaking the circuit.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Resistance R = V/I</strong> (from Ohm's Law). Unit: ohm (Ω) = V/A. Resistance depends on: (1) material — resistivity ρ (Ω·m), (2) length l — longer = more resistance, (3) cross-sectional area A — thinner = more resistance. Formula: R = ρl/A. Resistivity of copper: 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Resistivity of glass: ~10¹² Ω·m. Temperature affects resistance: metals increase resistance when hot, semiconductors decrease. Superconductors have zero resistance below a critical temperature.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a wire with electrons bouncing off atoms. "Resistance = electrical friction." Formula R = ρl/A. A long thin wire (high R) vs short thick wire (low R). A toaster: resistance turns electricity to heat. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-890 — Ohm's Law: V = IR

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** ohms-law; V=IR; relationship-between-voltage-current-resistance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-887, BB-NEW-888, BB-NEW-889]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The voltage across a component equals the current through it multiplied by its resistance. V = IR. This is Ohm's Law — the single most used equation in all of electricity. If you know two of V, I, R, you can find the third. It's a linear relationship: double the voltage, and (for a fixed resistance) the current doubles. Like a pipe: double the pressure, and the flow rate doubles.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 6 V battery connected to a 3 Ω resistor. Current: I = V/R = 6/3 = 2 A. A 240 V heater has a resistance of 24 Ω. Current: I = 240/24 = 10 A. Power: P = IV = 10 × 240 = 2400 W. A phone charger: 5 V output, and the phone draws 1 A. The phone's equivalent resistance (while charging) = 5/1 = 5 Ω. A 1.5 V AA battery connects to a tiny bulb with 15 Ω resistance. Current = 0.1 A. The bulb glows dimly. Two AA batteries in series (3 V): current = 0.2 A — the bulb is brighter. V = IR explains why.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Ohm's Law: V = IR</strong>, valid for ohmic materials (where R is constant). For non-ohmic materials (diodes, filaments), V is not proportional to I — R varies with temperature or voltage. The three forms: V = IR, I = V/R, R = V/I. Ohm's Law is a definition of resistance for ohmic conductors, but it's also a powerful tool for circuit analysis. In series circuits: same I, voltages split. In parallel circuits: same V, currents split. Combined with Kirchhoff's rules, Ohm's Law solves essentially every DC circuit.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a circuit: battery (V), resistor (R), ammeter (I). V = IR in large letters. Three labelled examples: 6V/3Ω = 2A, 240V/24Ω = 10A, 5V/5Ω = 1A. A V-I graph: straight line through origin for ohmic, curved for non-ohmic. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-891 — Series circuits: one path

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** series-circuit; same-current; voltage-division; R-total=R1+R2 | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-890, BB-NEW-889]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In a series circuit, components are connected one after another in a single loop. There's only one path for current to flow. The current is the same everywhere. The voltage from the battery is split among the components. If one bulb burns out, the circuit is broken — all bulbs go out. Old-fashioned Christmas tree lights were wired in series. The total resistance is simply the sum: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 9 V battery connected to three resistors in series: 2 Ω, 3 Ω, and 4 Ω. Total resistance = 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 Ω. Current = 9/9 = 1 A — same through all three. Voltage drop across R₁: V₁ = I × R₁ = 1 × 2 = 2 V. Across R₂: V₂ = 1 × 3 = 3 V. Across R₃: V₃ = 1 × 4 = 4 V. Sum = 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 V — all the voltage is used. This is Kirchhoff's Voltage Law: the sum of voltage drops around a closed loop equals the sum of voltage sources. A string of 20 small bulbs on a 240 V supply: each bulb drops 12 V (assuming equal resistance).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Series circuit properties:</strong> (1) Current is the same through all components: I = I₁ = I₂ = ... (2) Total resistance: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ... (3) Total voltage: V_total = V₁ + V₂ + ... (4) Voltmeters are always connected in parallel with the component being measured — they have very high internal resistance so they draw negligible current. Ammeters are always in series. The series rule explains why long extension leads can cause voltage drop — the wire resistance adds to the circuit.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a series circuit: battery, three resistors (R1, R2, R3), ammeter showing same I everywhere. Labels: V1+V2+V3 = V_total. R_total = R1+R2+R3. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-892 — Parallel circuits: many paths

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** parallel-circuit; same-voltage; current-splitting; 1/R=1/R1+1/R2 | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-890, BB-NEW-891]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In a parallel circuit, components are connected on separate branches. Each branch gets the full battery voltage. The current splits among the branches. If one bulb burns out, the others stay on — this is how your house is wired. The total resistance is LESS than the smallest individual resistance because there are more paths for current to flow. Formula: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 12 V battery with two 6 Ω resistors in parallel. 1/R_total = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 1/3, so R_total = 3 Ω (half the resistance of one — makes sense: two identical paths). Total current = V/R = 12/3 = 4 A. Current through each resistor: I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A. Sum = 2 + 2 = 4 A — Kirchhoff's Current Law. A 240 V household circuit: a 2400 W heater (10 A) and a 60 W lamp (0.25 A) in parallel. Equivalent resistance of the heater: R = V²/P = 240²/2400 = 24 Ω. Lamp: R = 240²/60 = 960 Ω. Parallel combination: 1/R = 1/24 + 1/960 ≈ 0.0427, R ≈ 23.4 Ω — the heater dominates.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Parallel circuit properties:</strong> (1) Voltage is the same across all branches: V = V₁ = V₂ = ... (2) Total current splits: I_total = I₁ + I₂ + ... (3) Total resistance: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... — always less than the smallest branch. (4) For two resistors: R_total = (R₁R₂)/(R₁+R₂). (5) Ammeters are always in series with the branch; voltmeters in parallel. All domestic and industrial wiring is parallel — series wiring would be impractical and unsafe.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a parallel circuit: battery, two resistors on separate branches with their own currents (I₁, I₂). V same across both. Formula 1/R = 1/R₁+1/R₂. A house: heater (10A) and lamp (0.25A) on parallel branches. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-893 — Electric power: P = IV

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** electric-power; P=IV; P=I²R; P=V²/R; kilowatt-hour | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-888, BB-NEW-890, BB-NEW-881]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When current flows through a voltage, power is delivered. Power = current × voltage. P = IV. You can also write it as P = I²R (using V = IR) or P = V²/R. These three forms are all the same equation, just rearranged. A 60 W bulb on 240 V draws 60/240 = 0.25 A. A 2000 W kettle on 240 V draws 8.3 A. The power tells you how much energy per second the device uses.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 2 kW electric kettle on 240 V: I = 2000/240 = 8.33 A. R = V²/P = 240²/2000 = 28.8 Ω. If the kettle runs for 3 minutes (180 s): energy used = 2000 × 180 = 360,000 J = 0.1 kWh. At 30p/kWh, that's 3p to boil a kettle. A fridge: maybe 100 W average (cycling on/off). Runs 24 hours: 100 × 24 = 2400 Wh = 2.4 kWh per day. Per month: 72 kWh. Cost: about £22/month. A phone charger left plugged in (but not charging) might draw 0.3 W. Per year: 0.3 × 365 × 24 = 2.6 kWh — barely £0.80/year, but multiply by a billion chargers and it's significant.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electric power P = IV = I²R = V²/R</strong>. Units: watt (W) = J/s = A·V. These three forms are mathematically identical; use whichever is most convenient given known quantities. P = IV: useful when you know V and I. P = I²R: useful for transmission line losses (current is the driver). P = V²/R: useful when R is fixed and V varies. <strong>Energy = power × time</strong>. The kilowatt-hour (kWh) is a unit of energy: 1 kWh = 3,600,000 J = 3.6 MJ. Domestic electricity is billed in kWh.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of the three power formulas: P = IV, P = I²R, P = V²/R — all connected to show they're equivalent. A kettle: 2000 W, 8.33 A, 28.8 Ω. A kWh meter spinning. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-894 — Batteries: portable electricity

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** battery; electrochemical-cell; voltage-source; internal-resistance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-886, BB-NEW-888]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A battery turns chemical energy into electrical energy. Two different metals (electrodes) sit in a chemical paste or liquid (electrolyte). A chemical reaction pushes electrons from one terminal to the other, creating voltage. An AA battery: 1.5 V. A car battery: 12 V (six 2 V cells in series). A phone battery: about 3.7 V (lithium-ion). All batteries have internal resistance — they're not perfect voltage sources. As a battery ages, its internal resistance rises and its voltage drops.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A fresh AA alkaline battery: 1.5 V, internal resistance ~0.2 Ω. Draw 0.5 A: voltage at the terminals = 1.5 − 0.5 × 0.2 = 1.4 V. The 0.1 V is lost inside the battery as heat. Draw 5 A: terminal voltage = 1.5 − 5 × 0.2 = 0.5 V — most of the energy is wasted inside. Car battery: 12 V, internal resistance ~0.02 Ω. Draw 300 A to crank the engine: terminal voltage = 12 − 300 × 0.02 = 6 V — half the battery's energy goes to internal heat. That's why starter cables get warm. A lithium-ion phone battery (3.7 V, 4000 mAh): energy capacity = 3.7 × 4 = 14.8 Wh — enough to keep a 5 W phone running for about 3 hours.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>A battery</strong> is a device that converts chemical energy to electrical energy via redox reactions. Terminal voltage V = emf − Ir, where emf is the ideal voltage, I is the current, and r is the internal resistance. Energy capacity is measured in amp-hours (Ah) or watt-hours (Wh). Batteries in series: voltages add (three 1.5 V AAs → 4.5 V). Batteries in parallel: voltage stays the same, capacity adds. Primary cells: non-rechargeable (alkaline). Secondary cells: rechargeable (Li-ion, lead-acid).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a battery: two plates in electrolyte. Electron flow from one terminal to the other driving a bulb. Label: "Chemical energy → Electrical energy." Internal resistance shown as a small resistor inside the battery symbol. AA (1.5V), car battery (12V), phone (3.7V). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-895 — AC and DC: two kinds of current

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** AC; DC; alternating-current; direct-current; frequency; rms | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-887, BB-NEW-893]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>DC (direct current) flows in one direction only — batteries produce DC. AC (alternating current) reverses direction many times per second — the mains supply is AC. In the UK, it reverses 50 times per second (50 Hz). In the US, 60 times (60 Hz). AC is used for power distribution because it's easy to change voltage with transformers. DC is used in electronics because circuits need a steady, one-way flow.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1.5 V battery: pure DC — steady 1.5 V, current always flows the same way. UK mains: 230 V AC (nominally — it was harmonised from 240 V, but in practice it's still ~240 V). The voltage oscillates between +325 V and −325 V (the peak). The "230 V" is the RMS value — the equivalent DC voltage that would deliver the same power. Peak = RMS × √2 ≈ 325 V. US mains: 120 V RMS, peak = 170 V, 60 Hz. A phone charger converts AC to DC: a tiny box contains a rectifier (diodes that only allow current one way), a capacitor (smooths the bumps), and a voltage regulator (keeps output steady at 5 V).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>DC (Direct Current):</strong> charge flows in one direction at a constant rate. Produced by batteries, solar cells, DC generators. <strong>AC (Alternating Current):</strong> charge flow reverses periodically, usually sinusoidal: V(t) = V_peak sin(2πft). <strong>RMS (root mean square):</strong> the effective value — V_rms = V_peak/√2. For 230 V RMS, V_peak = 325 V. AC power delivery is more efficient over long distances because transformers can step voltage up (high V, low I → low I²R losses). The "War of the Currents" (Edison DC vs Tesla/Westinghouse AC) was settled by the 1890s — AC won for the grid.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of DC: a straight line (I vs t). AC: a sine wave (I vs t), labelled with peak, RMS, period. A battery (DC). A mains plug (AC). A phone charger: AC in, DC out. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-896 — Conductors and insulators

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** conductor; insulator; free-electrons; band-theory-intuition | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-886, BB-NEW-889]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Some materials let electricity flow easily — these are conductors. Copper, silver, aluminium, gold are the best. Their atoms have loosely held outer electrons that are free to move. Other materials block electricity — insulators like rubber, plastic, glass, and wood. Their electrons are tightly bound. Between them are semiconductors (silicon, germanium), which can be switched between conducting and insulating — the basis of all modern electronics.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Copper is the standard for wiring: high conductivity, reasonably cheap, and ductile. Silver conducts slightly better but is too expensive. Aluminium is used for overhead power lines — lighter than copper, though slightly less conductive. Gold is used in connectors because it doesn't corrode — a thin gold plating keeps the connection reliable for decades. Rubber and PVC are the standard insulators — they wrap around copper wires. Overhead power lines are bare aluminium — air is the insulator. Glass insulators on old telegraph poles kept the wire from shorting to the wooden pole. A 1 cm rubber mat can insulate against tens of thousands of volts; a 1 cm copper rod conducts amps with almost no voltage drop.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Conductor:</strong> material with many free electrons — low resistivity (ρ < 10⁻⁶ Ω·m). <strong>Insulator:</strong> material with no free electrons — high resistivity (ρ > 10¹⁰ Ω·m). <strong>Semiconductor:</strong> intermediate resistivity that can be controlled by doping (adding impurities). Conductivity σ = 1/ρ. In band theory: conductors have overlapping or partially filled bands; insulators have a large band gap; semiconductors have a small band gap (typically < 2 eV). Superconductors have zero resistance — but only below a critical temperature (e.g., −196°C for certain ceramics).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of copper wire with free electrons moving. Rubber insulation blocking electrons. A spectrum: conductor (copper) → semiconductor (silicon) → insulator (glass). Resistivity values. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-897 — Circuit symbols and simple diagrams

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** circuit-diagrams; schematic-symbols; battery-resistor-switch-bulb | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-890, BB-NEW-891, BB-NEW-892]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Circuit diagrams use standard symbols to show how components are connected. A battery is two parallel lines (long = positive, short = negative). A resistor is a zigzag. A bulb is a circle with a cross. A switch is a gap with a line that can close it. These symbols let you draw any circuit without drawing realistic pictures — just like a map uses symbols for roads and cities.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Draw a simple circuit: battery → switch (open) → bulb → battery. Close the switch: current flows, bulb lights. This is a series circuit. Add a second bulb in series: both are dimmer (voltage splits). Now draw them in parallel: each bulb gets full voltage and glows at full brightness, but the battery drains twice as fast. A voltmeter is a circle with V — always across the component being measured. An ammeter is a circle with A — always in the path of the current. A fuse symbol is a rectangle with the wire passing through. Earth/ground is three decreasing horizontal lines.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Standard circuit symbols</strong> (IEC/BS): Battery (—|||—), Resistor (— ∿ —), Variable resistor (— ∿ — with arrow), Bulb (circle with ×), Switch (SPST: — · —), Fuse (rectangle with wire), Voltmeter (circle with V), Ammeter (circle with A), Earth (three lines), Diode (— ▷|—), LED (diode with arrows for light), Capacitor (—||—), Inductor (— ⸻ —), Transformer (two coils with lines between). Practice drawing and reading these — it's the electrical equivalent of reading music. Every circuit from a doorbell to a supercomputer can be drawn with these symbols.</p>

## BB-NEW-898 — Fuses, breakers, and safety

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** fuse; circuit-breaker; overcurrent-protection; earthing; electrical-safety | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-893, BB-NEW-889]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Too much current can melt wires and start fires. A fuse is a deliberate weak point that melts and breaks the circuit before the wires get dangerously hot. A circuit breaker does the same job but can be reset. Earthing provides a safe path for current if something goes wrong — the metal case of an appliance is connected to the ground, so if a live wire touches it, the current flows to earth and the fuse blows instead of flowing through you.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A UK plug fuse is rated at 3 A, 5 A, or 13 A. A 2000 W kettle (I = 2000/240 = 8.3 A) needs a 13 A fuse. A 60 W lamp (0.25 A) uses a 3 A fuse. If the kettle develops a short circuit, current surges to perhaps 1000 A — the 13 A fuse melts in milliseconds. Without a fuse, the cable would overheat and the insulation would melt or catch fire. A residual current device (RCD) detects imbalance between live and neutral current (current leaking to earth through you) and trips in under 30 milliseconds at 30 mA — fast enough to prevent electrocution. Circuit breakers in a consumer unit (fuse box) are rated at 6 A (lights), 32 A (ring mains), 40 A (cooker).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Fuse:</strong> a thin wire that melts when current exceeds its rating. Based on P = I²R — the heat generated melts the wire. Fuses are rated for current (A) and breaking capacity (kA). <strong>Circuit Breaker (MCB):</strong> an electromechanical switch that trips on overcurrent — reusable. <strong>RCD/GFCI:</strong> trips on earth leakage current (typically 30 mA). <strong>Earthing:</strong> connecting exposed metal parts to the earth (zero volts) so that a fault sends current to ground, not through a person. The combination of fuses, breakers, earthing, and double insulation makes modern electrical systems far safer than even the mid-20th century's.</p>

A person touching a faulty appliance: current passing through earth wire (safe) vs through body (dangerous). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-899 — Static electricity: charge at rest

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** static-electricity; triboelectric-effect; discharge; lightning-strike | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-886, BB-NEW-896]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Rub a plastic comb on a wool jumper. The comb picks up small bits of paper. This is static electricity — electric charge that stays on a surface rather than flowing through a circuit. Rubbing transfers electrons from one material to another. The comb becomes charged, and the charge attracts neutral objects by polarising them. "Static" means the charges aren't moving — but when they do discharge, the spark can be thousands of volts.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A comb rubbed on wool can reach several thousand volts (but the charge is tiny — microcoulombs — so the energy is small and it doesn't hurt you). Walking across a nylon carpet on a dry day can charge your body to 10,000–20,000 V. Touching a metal door handle: the spark is about 1 mm long, meaning about 3000 V (breakdown of air is ~3 kV/mm). The energy is only a few millijoules — annoying but harmless. Lightning is static electricity on a colossal scale: cloud bottom at −100 million volts, current 30 kA, energy ~500 MJ per strike. A Van de Graaff generator in a science museum demonstrates the same principle — a moving belt transfers charge to a metal sphere, building up to hundreds of thousands of volts.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Static electricity</strong> is the accumulation of electric charge on an insulated surface. Generated by the triboelectric effect — electron transfer when two different materials are rubbed together and separated. The triboelectric series ranks materials by their tendency to gain or lose electrons. <strong>Electrostatic discharge (ESD)</strong> is the rapid transfer of stored charge — can damage sensitive electronics at voltages as low as 100 V. <strong>Lightning</strong> is nature's biggest spark — a dielectric breakdown of air (normally an insulator) when the electric field exceeds ~3 MV/m.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a comb rubbed on wool, attracting bits of paper. The triboelectric series (rabbit fur, glass, wool, silk, wood, rubber, Teflon). A Van de Graaff generator with charge building up. A lightning bolt between cloud and ground — "100 MV, 30 kA." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-900 — The electricity toolkit

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** electricity | **Concept:** electricity-summary; charge-current-voltage-resistance-power-safety | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-886 through BB-NEW-899]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Electricity is the flow of charge. Charge (coulombs) comes from electrons and protons. Current (amperes) is charge per second. Voltage (volts) is energy per coulomb — the push. Resistance (ohms) is the opposition to flow. Ohm's Law (V = IR) ties them together. Power (watts) is VI — current times voltage. AC alternates direction; DC flows one way. Circuits can be series (one path) or parallel (many paths). Safety uses fuses and earthing. That's the foundation.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Key numbers to carry:</p>
<p>• Copper resistance: ~0.007 Ω per metre (2.5 mm² cable).</p>
<p>• Human body: 100,000 Ω (dry), 1,000 Ω (wet).</p>
<p>• UK mains: 230 V RMS, 50 Hz. US: 120 V RMS, 60 Hz.</p>
<p>• AA battery: 1.5 V. Car battery: 12 V.</p>
<p>• Fuse ratings: 3A (lamp), 13A (kettle).</p>
<p>• RCD trips at 30 mA.</p>
<p>• P = IV = I²R = V²/R — the power triangle.</p>
<p>• Series: same I, R_total = R₁ + R₂.</p>
<p>• Parallel: same V, 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.</p>
<p>• kWh = energy. 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ. ~30p/kWh (UK).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electricity toolkit:</strong></p>
<p>• <strong>Charge Q (C):</strong> fundamental property. 1 C = 6.24×10¹⁸ electrons.</p>
<p>• <strong>Current I (A):</strong> I = Q/t. Flow rate of charge.</p>
<p>• <strong>Voltage V (V):</strong> V = W/Q. Energy per coulomb — the push.</p>
<p>• <strong>Resistance R (Ω):</strong> R = V/I. Opposition to current. R = ρl/A.</p>
<p>• <strong>Ohm's Law:</strong> V = IR. </p>
<p>• <strong>Power P (W):</strong> P = IV = I²R = V²/R.</p>
<p>• <strong>Energy (kWh):</strong> E = Pt. 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.</p>
<p>• <strong>Series:</strong> same I, additive R, split V.</p>
<p>• <strong>Parallel:</strong> same V, split I, reduced R.</p>
<p>• <strong>AC:</strong> V(t) = V_peak sin(2πft). RMS = peak/√2.</p>
