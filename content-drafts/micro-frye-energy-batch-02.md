# Micro-Frye — Batch 02: Energy Basics (15 BBs)

These 15 BBs build the concept of energy from nothing — starting with the simple observation that "things can make things happen" and ending with the full conservation law. Energy is famously hard to define (even Feynman said so), so Micro-Frye defines it operationally: energy is the ability to do work, and work is force × distance. Every BB is one small step, with concrete numbers and zero jargon until the jargon is earned.

---

## BB-NEW-876 — What is energy? A first feel

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** energy-intuition; ability-to-do-work; forms-of-energy; Joule | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** None — but pairs well with [BB-NEW-860]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Energy is the ability to make things happen — to move something, heat something, light something. A moving car has energy because it can crush a barrier. A stretched spring has energy because it can launch a toy. A hot cup of tea has energy because it can warm your hands. You eat food to get energy for your muscles. The unit is the joule. 1 joule is roughly the energy needed to lift a small apple from the floor onto a table (about 1 metre up).</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Lift a 100 g apple 1 metre: that takes 1 joule of energy. A 60 W light bulb uses 60 joules every second. A chocolate bar (200 kcal) contains roughly 840,000 joules — enough energy to lift an 84 kg person 1000 metres vertically. A litre of petrol contains about 35,000,000 joules (35 MJ). The same energy would keep a 60 W bulb lit for 162 hours. The human body at rest burns about 100 W — 100 joules per second — just to stay alive (heart, brain, temperature). Energy is everywhere, always being converted from one form to another.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Energy is physics's main currency. It can't be created or destroyed — only changed from one form to another. In a car engine: chemical energy (petrol) → thermal energy (explosion) → kinetic energy (motion). In a solar panel: light energy → electrical energy. In a battery: chemical energy → electrical energy. In a loudspeaker: electrical energy → sound energy. Every machine, every living thing, every star is an energy converter. The total energy before and after is always the same. This is the law of conservation of energy — the most powerful, most universal principle in all of science.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of many energy conversions. An apple being lifted (1 J). A light bulb (60 J/s). A chocolate bar (840,000 J). A car: petrol → motion. A solar panel: sun → electricity. Arrows between forms. "Energy = the ability to do work." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-877 — Work: force that moves something

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** work; force-x-distance; W=Fd; Joule-definition | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-876]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In physics, "work" has a precise meaning: work is done when a force moves an object. If you push on a wall and it doesn't move, you've done zero work — even if you're exhausted. Work = force × distance. Push a box with 10 newtons of force over 3 metres, and you've done 30 joules of work. No movement means no work, no matter how hard you push.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Push a 20 kg crate across a floor with 80 N of force. It moves 5 metres. Work = 80 × 5 = 400 J. Now lift a 10 kg box 2 metres up. Force needed = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N. Distance = 2 m. Work = 98 × 2 = 196 J. A person pushing a stalled car: 500 N force, car moves 10 m. Work = 5000 J. But hold a 20 kg bag without moving — force = 196 N, distance = 0, work = 0. You feel tired because your muscles are constantly contracting and relaxing microscopically, doing internal work — but on the bag, no physics work is done.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Work W = F × d × cos θ</strong>, where θ is the angle between the force and the direction of motion. If the force is in the same direction as the motion: W = Fd. If perpendicular: W = 0 (no work — e.g., the normal force when sliding horizontally). If opposite: W = −Fd (negative work — e.g., friction). Work is measured in joules (J). 1 J = 1 N·m. Work is a scalar — it has magnitude but no direction. Work is one way energy is transferred from one system to another.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a person pushing a box. Arrow: F = 80 N. Distance: d = 5 m. W = Fd = 400 J. Another panel: lifting a box: F = mg = 98 N, d = 2 m, W = 196 J. A third panel: pushing a wall — d = 0, W = 0. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-878 — Kinetic energy: the energy of motion

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** kinetic-energy; KE=½mv²; speed-squared; stopping-distance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-866, BB-NEW-877]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Anything that moves has kinetic energy. A rolling bicycle, a thrown ball, a falling leaf — all carry energy because they're moving. The formula is KE = ½mv². The important part is v²: double the speed, and the kinetic energy goes up four times. That's why a car at 100 km/h needs four times the stopping distance of the same car at 50 km/h, not twice. Speed matters enormously.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1000 kg car at 10 m/s (36 km/h): KE = ½ × 1000 × 10² = 50,000 J. The same car at 20 m/s (72 km/h): KE = ½ × 1000 × 20² = 200,000 J — four times as much. At 30 m/s (108 km/h): KE = 450,000 J — nine times as much as at 10 m/s. A 70 kg sprinter at 10 m/s: KE = ½ × 70 × 100 = 3500 J. A 0.06 kg tennis ball served at 60 m/s (216 km/h): KE = ½ × 0.06 × 3600 = 108 J. The speed-squared rule explains why high-speed collisions are so devastating and why braking distances grow so fast.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Kinetic energy KE = ½mv²</strong>. Units: joules (kg·m²/s²). Derived from work: the work needed to accelerate an object from rest to speed v is W = Fd = ma × d. Using v² = 2ad, d = v²/(2a), so W = ma × v²/(2a) = ½mv². Kinetic energy is always positive (mass is positive, v² is positive). At everyday speeds, this formula is accurate. At speeds close to light speed, relativistic corrections are needed — but at 30 m/s (0.00001% of light speed), the error is negligible.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a car with three speed labels: 36 km/h (KE arrow small), 72 km/h (KE arrow 4× bigger), 108 km/h (KE arrow 9× bigger). Formula KE = ½mv² prominently. A braking distance comparison: 10 m, 40 m, 90 m. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-879 — Gravitational potential energy: the energy of position

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** potential-energy; GPE=mgh; height-as-stored-energy; reference-level | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-861, BB-NEW-877]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Lift a rock. You've given it energy — not kinetic, because it's not moving. This is potential energy: energy stored in position. If you drop the rock, the potential energy turns into kinetic energy as it falls. The higher you lift it, the more potential energy it stores. A dam stores water high up to turn turbines; a clock stores energy by winding a weight upward. Gravitational potential energy = mgh — mass × gravity × height.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 2 kg brick lifted 3 metres up: GPE = 2 × 9.8 × 3 = 58.8 J. Drop it: just before it hits the ground, all 58.8 J has become kinetic energy. Speed at impact: v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 3) = √58.8 = 7.67 m/s (27.6 km/h). A 70 kg person climbs 100 stairs (each 15 cm, total 15 m): GPE gained = 70 × 9.8 × 15 = 10,290 J = 10.3 kJ. That's about 2.5 food calories of mechanical work — though the body burns much more due to inefficiency. Hoover Dam: water drops 180 m. Each cubic metre (1000 kg) of water releases 1000 × 9.8 × 180 = 1.76 MJ of potential energy as it falls — enough to light a 60 W bulb for 8 hours.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Gravitational potential energy GPE = mgh</strong>, measured from an arbitrary reference level (usually the ground or lowest point). Only changes in height matter — the absolute value has no physical significance. The work done against gravity to lift something equals mgh. On a slope, h = d sin θ, so GPE = mgd sin θ. GPE is a form of mechanical energy. Combined with kinetic energy, it forms the mechanical energy of an object: E = KE + GPE. In the absence of friction, E is conserved — this is the principle of conservation of mechanical energy.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a rock at various heights: ground (GPE = 0), 1 m (GPE = mg), 3 m (GPE = 3mg). Dropping from 3 m: GPE → KE. Formula KE = ½mv² = mgh shown. A dam: water high, falling to turbine. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-880 — Conservation of energy: nothing is lost

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** energy-conservation; KE-to-GPE; GPE-to-KE; closed-system | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-878, BB-NEW-879]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Energy can't be created or destroyed — only transformed. A pendulum swings: at the top, all energy is potential; at the bottom, all energy is kinetic; in between, it's a mixture. The total never changes. If there's friction, some energy becomes heat — but it's not lost, just spread out. This is the law of conservation of energy: the total energy of a closed system is constant. No exception has ever been found.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1 kg pendulum bob swings from a height of 0.5 m. At the highest point: GPE = 1 × 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 J, KE = 0. Total mechanical energy = 4.9 J. At the lowest point: GPE = 0, KE = 4.9 J. Speed: v = √(2 × 4.9) = 3.13 m/s. At a point halfway down (h = 0.25 m): GPE = 2.45 J, KE = 2.45 J. Everywhere: GPE + KE = 4.9 J (ignoring air resistance). A roller coaster: the first hill is the highest — all subsequent hills must be lower because friction converts some energy to heat. Without the initial lift, nothing happens.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Conservation of mechanical energy:</strong> KE_initial + GPE_initial + W_friction = KE_final + GPE_final. If no friction or external work: KE + GPE = constant. <strong>General conservation of energy:</strong> including thermal energy, sound, light, chemical, nuclear — the total energy of an isolated system never changes. This is physics's most tested and trusted law. It implies that perpetual motion machines are impossible — you can't get more energy out than you put in.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a pendulum at three positions. Top: GPE = max, KE = 0. Middle: GPE = KE = half. Bottom: GPE = 0, KE = max. A bar chart showing energy transfer with total staying constant. A roller coaster with the first hill highest. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-881 — Power: how fast the energy flows

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** power; energy-per-time; watts; horsepower | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-877, BB-NEW-876]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Power is the rate of doing work — how fast energy is transferred. A small engine can do the same work as a big engine, but it takes longer. The formula is power = work/time. The unit is the watt: 1 watt = 1 joule per second. Your kettle draws 2000 watts (2 kilowatts). A human at rest uses about 100 watts — roughly the same as a bright old-fashioned light bulb. A Formula 1 engine produces about 750,000 watts (750 kW, about 1000 horsepower).</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Lift a 100 kg weight 1 metre. Work = 100 × 9.8 × 1 = 980 J. If you do it in 1 second: power = 980 W (about 1.3 horsepower). If you do it in 10 seconds: power = 98 W. Same work, different power. Usain Bolt's 100 m sprint (9.58 s): his mass ~94 kg. KE = ½ × 94 × 10.44² = 5120 J. Average power = 5120/9.58 = 534 W. Peak power (during acceleration) is higher — about 2000 W (2.7 hp) for a fraction of a second. A Tesla Model 3 motor: 200 kW = 200,000 W. The Three Gorges Dam in China: 22,500,000,000 W (22.5 GW).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Power P = W/t = ΔE/Δt</strong> (work done or energy transferred per unit time). Units: watt (W) = J/s. Also: P = Fv (force × velocity) when force and velocity are constant and parallel. <strong>1 horsepower (hp) = 746 W</strong> — roughly the sustained output of a strong horse, or the peak output of a fit human for a few seconds. A 100 W light bulb uses 100 J of electrical energy per second — of which about 5 J becomes light and 95 J becomes heat. In electricity: P = IV (current × voltage). In mechanics: P = Fv. In any system: P = ΔE/Δt.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a weightlifter lifting a 100 kg barbell. "1 second: 980 W. 10 seconds: 98 W. Same work, different power." A kettle (2000 W), a light bulb (100 W), a human at rest (100 W). A Formula 1 car: 750,000 W. A dam: 22.5 GW. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-882 — Efficiency: what you get vs what you put in

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** efficiency; useful-work-out-vs-energy-in; losses-to-heat | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-881, BB-NEW-880]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>No machine is perfect. Some energy always turns into unwanted heat. Efficiency is the ratio of useful energy out to total energy in, usually expressed as a percentage. A car engine is about 25% efficient — 75% of the petrol's energy becomes heat, not motion. An LED light is about 40% efficient — 40% of the electricity becomes light, 60% becomes heat. A human muscle is about 20–25% efficient. You can't beat 100% — that would violate conservation of energy.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A petrol car engine: 100 J of chemical energy in → 25 J of kinetic energy to wheels + 75 J of heat. Efficiency = 25%. A diesel engine: slightly better, about 35%. An electric car motor: about 90% efficient — 100 J in → 90 J to wheels + 10 J of heat. A bicycle: human input ~100 W, output to wheels ~20–25 W (20–25% muscle efficiency). An incandescent light bulb: 100 J in → 5 J light + 95 J heat (5% efficient). LED: 100 J in → 40 J light + 60 J heat (40% efficient). A gas power station: 100 J of chemical energy in → 40 J electricity out + 60 J heat. Combined-cycle power stations capture some of that heat and can reach 60%.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Efficiency η = useful energy output / total energy input</strong>. Always less than 1 (or 100%). The "lost" energy is not destroyed — it becomes thermal energy (heat) spread into the environment. This is the Second Law of Thermodynamics at work: in any real process, some energy becomes unavailable for doing useful work. Efficiency of simple machines (levers, pulleys) can be close to 100% with low friction. Efficiency of heat engines (cars, power plants) has a theoretical maximum (Carnot efficiency) set by the temperature difference — typically well below 100%.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a car engine: 100 J in (petrol), 25 J out (motion), 75 J out (heat). Label: 25% efficient. An LED: 100 J in, 40 J light, 60 J heat. A person on a bike: 100 J food energy, 20 J motion, 80 J heat. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-883 — Elastic potential energy: springs and stretch

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** elastic-energy; Hookes-law; spring-constant; ½kx² | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-879, BB-NEW-877]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Stretch a rubber band or squash a spring. You've stored energy. Let go, and the energy is released — the rubber band flies across the room, the spring snaps back. This is elastic potential energy. The formula: E = ½kx², where k is the spring's stiffness (how hard it is to stretch) and x is how far you've stretched it. Like kinetic energy, it depends on the square — double the stretch, and you store four times the energy.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A spring with stiffness k = 200 N/m is stretched 0.1 m. Energy stored = ½ × 200 × 0.1² = 1 J. Same spring stretched 0.3 m: E = ½ × 200 × 0.09 = 9 J — nine times as much for three times the stretch. A car suspension spring (k ≈ 20,000 N/m) compressed by 0.1 m when you hit a bump: E = ½ × 20,000 × 0.01 = 100 J. A bow (archery): draw weight ~200 N at 0.7 m draw length. Average force ~100 N. Energy stored ≈ 100 × 0.7 = 70 J — enough to launch a 20 g arrow at about 84 m/s (300 km/h). A retractable pen has a tiny spring with k ≈ 10 N/m — stretch it 2 cm and you've stored 0.002 J, which is still enough to click the pen.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Elastic energy is everywhere. Shock absorbers in cars use springs and dampers to convert bumps into heat safely. Catapults and trebuchets stored elastic or gravitational energy to hurl projectiles — medieval engineering at its finest. A wind-up toy stores energy in a coiled spring. Even your arteries are elastic — they stretch when the heart pumps and recoil between beats, smoothing the blood flow. The pogo stick, the trampoline, the diving board — all trade elastic energy for kinetic, over and over. k measures stiffness: a steel spring has high k, a rubber band has low k. The stiffer the spring, the more energy it stores for the same stretch.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a spring. Unstretched (x=0). Stretched to x — arrow showing force F = kx. Energy formula E = ½kx². A second panel: archery bow — draw length d, energy stored as elastic. A car suspension: spring compressed by a bump. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-884 — Work-energy theorem: work changes speed

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** work-energy-theorem; W=ΔKE; net-work-equals-change-in-KE | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-877, BB-NEW-878]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When you do work on something, you change its kinetic energy. Push a stationary car, and the work you do becomes its kinetic energy. Brake a moving car, and friction does negative work, reducing its kinetic energy. The work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. W_net = ΔKE = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i². This is a direct consequence of F = ma — it's the same physics in energy language.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1000 kg car is at rest. A net force of 2000 N pushes it over 50 m. Work done = 2000 × 50 = 100,000 J. Final kinetic energy = 100,000 J. Speed: v = √(2 × 100,000/1000) = √200 = 14.14 m/s (51 km/h). Same car at 20 m/s (72 km/h): brakes apply 5000 N of friction. How far to stop? Initial KE = ½ × 1000 × 400 = 200,000 J. Work done by friction = 5000 × d = 200,000 J. d = 40 m. A 0.15 kg baseball pitched at 40 m/s: KE = ½ × 0.15 × 1600 = 120 J. A bat does work of 120 J (negative on the ball) to stop it, then more work to accelerate it in the opposite direction.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Work-Energy Theorem: W_net = ΔKE</strong> = KE_final − KE_initial. W_net is the sum of ALL forces' work (push, friction, gravity, etc.). Positive net work → speed increases. Negative net work → speed decreases. Zero net work → speed constant (equilibrium). The theorem holds for any force, constant or variable. For variable forces, use integration: W = ∫F·dx. If gravity is the only force doing work: ΔKE + ΔGPE = 0 — mechanical energy is conserved. The work-energy theorem is more fundamental than you might think — it's a special case of the conservation of energy.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a car being pushed. Initial v=0. Arrow: F = 2000 N over d = 50 m. W = 100,000 J. Final KE = 100,000 J, v = 14.14 m/s. Second panel: braking from 20 m/s — friction work = −200,000 J, d = 40 m. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-885 — The energy toolkit so far

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** energy-summary; work-KE-GPE-power-efficiency-conservation | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-876 through BB-NEW-884]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Energy is the single most useful idea in physics. It's measured in joules. Energy can be kinetic (motion), gravitational (height), elastic (stretched springs), chemical (fuel, food), thermal (heat), electrical, and nuclear. Work transfers energy: W = Fd. Power is the rate of transfer: P = W/t. Energy is conserved: it never disappears, only transforms. Efficiency measures how much of the input becomes useful output. The tools are simple, but they explain everything from a bouncing ball to a power grid.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Key numbers to carry:</p>
<p>• 1 joule: lifting a small apple 1 metre.</p>
<p>• 1 watt: 1 joule per second.</p>
<p>• Human at rest: ~100 W (same as a bright old-style bulb).</p>
<p>• Human sprinting: ~1000 W peak.</p>
<p>• Car engine: ~100 kW (petrol), ~200 kW (electric).</p>
<p>• Car efficiency: ~25% (petrol), ~90% (electric).</p>
<p>• Human muscle efficiency: ~20%.</p>
<p>• 1 litre of petrol: ~35 MJ (35,000,000 J).</p>
<p>• 1 food calorie: 4184 J.</p>
<p>• Hoover Dam: enough for ~1.3 million homes.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Energy toolkit summary:</strong></p>
<p>• <strong>Energy:</strong> ability to do work. Measured in J (joules), kWh (3.6 MJ), cal (4.184 J), eV (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J).</p>
<p>• <strong>Work:</strong> W = Fd cos θ. Energy transferred by a force.</p>
<p>• <strong>Kinetic energy:</strong> KE = ½mv². Energy of motion.</p>
<p>• <strong>Gravitational potential:</strong> GPE = mgh. Energy of position.</p>
<p>• <strong>Elastic potential:</strong> EPE = ½kx². Energy of deformation.</p>
<p>• <strong>Power:</strong> P = W/t = Fv. Rate of energy transfer.</p>
<p>• <strong>Efficiency:</strong> η = E_out/E_in. Always < 1.</p>
<p>• <strong>Conservation:</strong> E_total is constant in a closed system.</p>
<p>• <strong>Work-energy theorem:</strong> W_net = ΔKE.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a grand summary. Centre: "ENERGY" with units (J, kWh, cal). Branching out: KE (½mv²), GPE (mgh), EPE (½kx²), Work (Fd), Power (W/t), Efficiency (out/in), Conservation (total = constant). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.
