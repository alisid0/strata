## BB-NEW-860 — What is a force?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** force-definition; push-pull; contact-force; Newton | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** None — this is the beginning

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A force is a push or a pull. When you push a door open, pull a suitcase, or kick a football, you're applying a force. Forces can make things speed up, slow down, change direction, or change shape. The unit of force is the newton. 1 newton is roughly the weight of a small apple. Your body weighs about 700 newtons. This is where physics starts: every movement, every change, begins with a force.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Pick up a 100 g apple. The force your hand exerts to hold it steady against gravity is about 1 newton. Push a 1 kg bag of sugar across a table — you feel resistance. That's friction, another force. A 70 kg person weighs 70 × 9.8 = 686 N. A car crash at 50 km/h generates forces of tens of thousands of newtons. Forces are everywhere — gravity pulls you down, the chair pushes you up, the wind pushes the trees.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Force F is a push or pull measured in newtons (N). 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². Force is a vector — it has both magnitude (how strong) and direction (which way). Types: contact forces (push, pull, friction, normal, tension) and field forces (gravity, electric, magnetic). Newton's laws describe how forces affect motion. The key phrase: "A force changes motion or shape."</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of hands pushing a box, pulling a rope, kicking a ball. Arrows showing push and pull directions. A small apple next to "1 N" label. A person standing with "700 N" arrow pointing down. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-861 — Gravity: the force you can't see

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** gravity; weight; gravitational-force; mass-vs-weight | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Drop a pen. It falls. Every single object on Earth is pulled downward by gravity. The Earth is enormous — 6,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 kg — and its gravity pulls on everything near it. The force of gravity on an object is called its weight. On the Moon, you'd weigh one-sixth as much because the Moon is smaller. Your mass (how much stuff you're made of) is the same everywhere. Your weight changes depending on where you are.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1 kg bag of sugar has mass 1 kg everywhere — on Earth, on the Moon, in space. On Earth, it weighs 1 × 9.8 = 9.8 N. On the Moon, it weighs 1 × 1.6 = 1.6 N. In deep space, far from any planet, it weighs almost 0 N — but it still has mass 1 kg. A 70 kg person weighs 686 N on Earth, 113 N on the Moon, and 1736 N on Jupiter. Mass is the same; weight depends on where you are. Gravity pulls everything downward at 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface. Every second of falling, your speed increases by 9.8 m/s. After 3 seconds: 29.4 m/s (106 km/h).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Gravity is why we stay on the ground, why the Moon orbits Earth, and why Earth orbits the Sun. Without gravity, the atmosphere would float away into space. Bridges and buildings must withstand gravity's pull on every beam and brick. Even your own body is shaped by gravity — astronauts in space grow 3–5 cm taller because their spines decompress without gravity's constant squeeze. Weight = mass × gravity. That's all. But that simple multiplication explains everything from falling apples to orbiting planets.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of Earth with an apple falling. Arrow labelled "weight = mg = 9.8 N/kg." A person on Earth (686 N) and the same person on the Moon (113 N). "Mass = same everywhere (kg). Weight = different (N)." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-862 — Friction: why things stop

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** friction; sliding-friction; roughness; heat-from-friction | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Slide a book across a table. It slows down and stops. Why? Friction. The bottom of the book and the top of the table are rough — microscopically rough, with tiny peaks and valleys that catch on each other. Friction always opposes motion. Without it, the book would slide forever. Friction turns kinetic energy into heat — rub your hands together fast and they get warm. That's friction in action.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 2 kg book on a wooden table. The weight is 19.6 N. The friction force that opposes sliding is roughly 0.3 × 19.6 = 5.9 N (for wood on wood). Push with 4 N — the book doesn't move (static friction holds it). Push with 7 N — the book slides (kinetic friction is slightly less than static). Once sliding, the friction is about 4.9 N, so the net force is 7 − 4.9 = 2.1 N, and the book accelerates slowly. A car tyre on dry asphalt: friction coefficient ≈ 0.8. On ice: ≈ 0.1. That's why you can't stop on ice — there's almost no friction.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Friction is everywhere. Car brakes use friction to stop — brake pads clamp onto a metal disc, converting kinetic energy to heat. If brakes overheat, the friction drops (brake fade) — that's why trucks use engine braking on long descents. Without friction, you couldn't walk (your foot would slip backward), hold a pencil, or drive a car. Even screws and nails stay in place because of friction. Friction is usually the enemy of efficiency (it wastes energy as heat), but it's also what makes controlled motion possible. A world without friction is a world where nothing stays where you put it.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a book sliding on a table. Arrows: push forward, friction backward. Magnified view of surfaces: rough peaks and valleys. Ice (smooth) vs asphalt (rough) with friction coefficients. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-863 — Normal force: why you don't fall through the floor

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** normal-force; support-force; perpendicular-reaction; N=mg-on-flat-surface | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-861]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Stand on the floor. Gravity pulls you down. Why don't you fall through? The floor pushes you up. This upward push is the normal force — "normal" because it's perpendicular to the surface. It's the reason tables hold plates, chairs hold people, and bridges hold cars. The normal force adjusts itself — it's exactly as strong as it needs to be to stop things from passing through each other.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 70 kg person stands on a flat floor. Weight = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N downward. The floor pushes up with exactly 686 N — the normal force N. If the person carries a 10 kg backpack (total mass 80 kg, weight 784 N), the normal force increases to 784 N. The floor doesn't care — it just pushes harder. If the floor tilts (like a ramp), the normal force decreases. On a 30° ramp: N = mg cos 30° = 686 × 0.866 = 594 N. The remaining component of weight (mg sin 30° = 343 N) pulls the person down the slope — that's what friction must oppose.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The normal force N is the force a surface exerts perpendicular to itself to support an object. It's a reaction force — it exists only when something pushes against the surface. N = mg for a flat horizontal surface with no other vertical forces. On an incline: N = mg cos θ. The normal force is NOT always equal to weight — in an accelerating lift: N = m(g + a) going up, N = m(g − a) going down. If a > g (accelerating downward faster than free fall), N becomes zero — you lose contact with the floor.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a person standing on a floor. Arrow down: mg. Arrow up: N. N = mg. A person on a ramp: N = mg cos θ (smaller arrow), mg sin θ down the slope. A lift accelerating up: N bigger. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-864 — Tension: the force in a rope

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** tension; rope-force; pulling-force; ideal-rope-assumptions | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Tie a rope to a box and pull. The rope pulls the box. The force inside the rope — the pull transmitted from your hand to the box — is tension. A rope can only pull; it can never push. If the rope is light (negligible mass) and doesn't stretch, the tension is the same everywhere along it. This is the ideal rope assumption that makes tension problems solvable.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>You lift a 10 kg box using a rope over a pulley. The tension in the rope is T = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N (if lifting at constant speed). If you accelerate the box upward at 2 m/s²: T = m(g + a) = 10 × (9.8 + 2) = 118 N. The rope must be strong enough to handle 118 N without breaking. A clothesline with 50 N of tension sags in the middle — the tension in each half is much larger than 25 N because the vertical component (T sin θ) must balance the weight, and sin θ is small when the line is nearly horizontal.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Tension holds up bridges, elevators, and cranes. A suspension bridge's main cables are under enormous tension — the Golden Gate Bridge's two main cables each carry about 1.2 million kN of tension, supported by 27,572 individual steel wires. An elevator cable must support the car's weight plus acceleration forces with a safety factor of 10 or more. In rock climbing, a rope can hold several kilonewtons — but a fall generates dynamic forces much higher than the climber's weight, which is why ropes are designed to stretch (dynamic ropes) to reduce peak tension. The simple rule — a rope pulls, it never pushes — underlies all of these applications.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a hand pulling a rope attached to a box. Arrow along the rope labelled T. A pulley system: rope over a wheel, person pulling down, box going up — T same on both sides. A sagging clothesline with T sin θ components. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-865 — Speed: how fast?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** speed; velocity; distance-over-time; units | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Speed tells you how fast something is moving. You calculate it by dividing the distance travelled by the time it took: speed = distance ÷ time. If you run 100 metres in 12 seconds, your speed is about 8.3 metres per second. The speedometer in a car shows instantaneous speed — how fast right now. Average speed accounts for speeding up, slowing down, and stopping.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A car travels 240 km in 3 hours. Average speed = 240 ÷ 3 = 80 km/h. But the car probably went faster on the highway and slower in town. Instantaneous speed at any moment could be 120 km/h or 30 km/h — the average smooths out the variations. Usain Bolt's 100 m world record (9.58 s) gives an average speed of 100 ÷ 9.58 = 10.44 m/s = 37.58 km/h. His top speed during that race was about 44.7 km/h. Speed of sound: 343 m/s (1235 km/h). Speed of light: 300,000,000 m/s.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Speed = distance/time. Units: m/s (SI), km/h, mph. 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h. <strong>Average speed:</strong> v_avg = total distance / total time. <strong>Instantaneous speed:</strong> the speed at a single instant — what a speedometer shows. <strong>Velocity</strong> is speed with a direction (50 km/h north). Speed is a scalar (just a number); velocity is a vector (number + direction). In physics, "speed" and "velocity" are not the same — velocity cares which way you're going.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a car with a speedometer showing 80 km/h. A distance-time graph: straight line (constant speed), curved line (changing speed). Formula: speed = distance/time. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-866 — Acceleration: speeding up and slowing down

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** acceleration; deceleration; rate-of-change-of-velocity; m/s² | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-865]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When you press the accelerator in a car, you speed up. When you brake, you slow down. Both are acceleration — any change in velocity. Acceleration tells you how quickly the speed is changing. If your speed increases by 3 metres per second every second, your acceleration is 3 m/s². A sports car accelerates fast (high acceleration); a loaded truck accelerates slowly (low acceleration).</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A car goes from 0 to 100 km/h (27.8 m/s) in 5.0 seconds. Acceleration = change in speed / time = 27.8/5.0 = 5.56 m/s². This means every second, the speed increases by 5.56 m/s. A loaded truck might do 0–100 in 15 seconds: a = 27.8/15 = 1.85 m/s². A Formula 1 car does it in 2.5 seconds: a = 11.1 m/s². Braking: a car stops from 100 km/h in 40 metres. Using v² = u² + 2as: a = (0 − 27.8²)/(2 × 40) = −9.66 m/s² — about −1g. The negative sign means deceleration (slowing down). At 1g, you feel your own weight pushing you forward.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Acceleration is what you feel. In an aeroplane taking off, you're pushed back into your seat — that's about 0.3g (3 m/s²). On a roller coaster, peaks of 3–4g are common. Fighter pilots endure 7–9g in tight turns. At 9g, a 70 kg pilot "weighs" 630 kg — blood drains from the brain, and a G-suit inflates to push it back. The human limit for sustained acceleration is about 10g. Your phone has an accelerometer that measures acceleration — it's how it knows when you rotate the screen or shake to undo. Every time you feel pushed, pulled, or thrown, you're feeling acceleration.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a car speedometer with needle moving from 0 to 100. A stopwatch showing 5.0 s. Formula: a = Δv/Δt = 5.56 m/s². A braking car with negative a. A roller coaster with "3g" label. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-867 — Mass: how much stuff?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** mass; inertia; kilogram; resistance-to-acceleration | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-861]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Mass is how much "stuff" an object is made of. A brick has more mass than a sponge the same size. Mass is measured in kilograms. More importantly, mass measures inertia — an object's resistance to being accelerated. It's much harder to push a car than a bicycle because the car has more mass. In deep space, far from any gravity, a 1000 kg spacecraft still has 1000 kg of mass. It just weighs nothing.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 1 kg bag of sugar has the same mass everywhere in the universe. To accelerate it at 1 m/s² requires 1 N of force. A 1000 kg car requires 1000 N to accelerate at 1 m/s². The car has 1000× more inertia. Your own mass (perhaps 70 kg) is mostly water (about 60%) — roughly 42 litres of water's worth. The rest is protein, fat, and minerals. The Earth's mass is 6 × 10²⁴ kg — that's a 6 followed by 24 zeros. The Sun's mass is 2 × 10³⁰ kg — about 330,000 Earths.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Mass m is measured in kilograms (kg). It is a scalar — no direction. Mass is distinct from weight: mass is intrinsic (same everywhere), weight is the gravitational force on that mass (w = mg, different on different planets). <strong>Inertia</strong> is the tendency of mass to resist changes in motion — Newton's First Law. The more mass, the harder to accelerate (F = ma). In the British engineering system, mass is measured in slugs (1 slug ≈ 14.59 kg).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a scale comparing a brick and a sponge — same size, different mass. A 1 kg mass labelled. A person pushing a bicycle (easy) vs a car (hard) — "more mass = harder to push." Earth and Sun size comparison. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-868 — Newton's First Law: things keep doing what they're doing

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** inertia; newtons-first-law; rest-and-motion; no-force-needed | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-867]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A book on a table stays there. A hockey puck on ice slides in a nearly straight line. Both are following the same rule: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion, unless a force acts on it. You don't need a force to keep moving — you need a force to CHANGE the motion. This is Newton's First Law, and it's the simplest, most profound statement in physics.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>In a car crash at 60 km/h: the car stops. Your body keeps moving at 60 km/h — until the seatbelt (a force) stops it. Without the seatbelt, you hit the dashboard. The seatbelt doesn't stop the car; it stops YOU. In space, a spacecraft with engines off keeps coasting at the same speed forever. The Voyager probes, launched in 1977, are still moving at ~17 km/s — no engine, no fuel, just inertia. On Earth, friction stops things, which is why Aristotle (and common sense) got this wrong for 2000 years. In an ideal frictionless world, a pushed box would slide forever.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia):</strong> An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion with constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force. ΣF = 0 ⇔ v = constant. This defines <strong>inertial reference frames</strong> — frames that are not accelerating. In an accelerating car, objects slide without any force — the car is not an inertial frame. The First Law is not a consequence of the Second Law; it's a separate statement about the existence of reference frames where the laws of physics take their simplest form.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a book on a table (at rest, stays at rest). A hockey puck on ice (in motion, stays in motion — nearly). A car crash: car stops, person continues forward (dashed arrow). Spacecraft coasting in deep space with v = constant. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-869 — Newton's Second Law: F = ma

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** newtons-second-law; F=ma; force-mass-acceleration-relationship | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-866, BB-NEW-867]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Push a shopping cart: it accelerates. Push twice as hard: it accelerates twice as much. Fill it with bricks: the same push gives less acceleration. Newton's Second Law says: F = ma. The acceleration is proportional to the force and inversely proportional to the mass. This one equation predicts the motion of everything from a thrown baseball to a rocket launching into space.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 2000 kg car accelerates from rest. The engine provides 4000 N of force. a = F/m = 4000/2000 = 2 m/s². After 5 seconds: v = at = 2 × 5 = 10 m/s (36 km/h). A 0.15 kg baseball thrown with 75 N of force: a = 75/0.15 = 500 m/s². The force lasts about 0.02 seconds, so the ball leaves at v = 500 × 0.02 = 10 m/s (36 km/h — not very fast for a baseball; a professional pitcher applies more like 150 N for a 90 mph fastball). A 50,000 kg rocket with 1,000,000 N of thrust: a = 1,000,000/50,000 = 20 m/s² — about 2g. Astronauts feel their weight double during launch.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Newton's Second Law:</strong> F_net = ma. The net (total) force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Units: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². The law is vector: acceleration is in the same direction as the net force. If multiple forces act: add them as vectors first, then apply F = ma. The equation works for: (1) constant forces (use SUVAT), (2) variable forces (use calculus), (3) any object from an atom to a galaxy. It does NOT work at speeds close to the speed of light (special relativity needed) or at atomic scales (quantum mechanics needed).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a shopping cart being pushed. Three panels: empty (small m, large a), half-full (medium), full of bricks (large m, small a). Formula: F = ma. Force arrow and acceleration arrow in the same direction. A rocket with thrust = 1,000,000 N, mass = 50,000 kg, a = 20 m/s². Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-870 — Newton's Third Law: equal and opposite

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** newtons-third-law; action-reaction; force-pairs; momentum-conservation | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-868]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Push on a wall. The wall pushes back on you — with exactly the same force. That's why your hand doesn't go through the wall. Jump off a boat onto a dock, and the boat slides backward. Every force comes in a pair: if A pushes B, B pushes A with equal force in the opposite direction. Forces never act alone. This is Newton's Third Law, and it's the reason rockets work in space where there's nothing to push against.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 70 kg person jumps off a 20 kg boat. Person pushes boat backward with 300 N. Boat accelerates backward: a_boat = 300/20 = 15 m/s². Boat pushes person forward with 300 N. Person accelerates forward: a_person = 300/70 = 4.3 m/s². Both move — the lighter boat moves faster. A rifle fires a bullet: the bullet (10 g) flies forward at 800 m/s; the rifle (4 kg) kicks backward at 2 m/s. The forces are equal; the masses determine the accelerations. A helicopter's rotor pushes air down; the air pushes the helicopter up. A rocket engine expels exhaust at high speed backward; the exhaust pushes the rocket forward.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Newton's Third Law:</strong> When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts a force on object A of equal magnitude and opposite direction. F_AB = −F_BA. The forces are the same type (both gravitational, both contact, etc.) and act on DIFFERENT objects — they do NOT cancel each other out. Common confusion: "If forces are equal, why does the bullet accelerate more than the gun?" Because F is equal, but m is different — a = F/m. The Third Law is equivalent to conservation of momentum: in the absence of external forces, total momentum is constant.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a person pushing a wall. Two arrows: person on wall (→), wall on person (←). A boat jump: person leaps right, boat slides left. A rifle: bullet→, rifle←. A rocket: exhaust↓, thrust↑. All pairs labelled "F" and "−F." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-871 — Free-body diagrams: drawing the forces

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** free-body-diagram; force-isolation; problem-solving-tool | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860, BB-NEW-868, BB-NEW-869]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Before you solve any force problem, draw the object alone — isolated from everything around it — and draw every force acting ON it as an arrow. This is a free-body diagram. It turns a messy real-world situation (a box being pushed, a chandelier hanging from chains, a car on a slope) into a simple diagram you can solve with F = ma. This one skill separates people who can do physics from people who get lost in word problems.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 10 kg box rests on a table. Free-body diagram: (1) Draw the box as a dot or a simple rectangle. (2) Draw weight arrow pointing DOWN: mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N. (3) Draw normal force arrow pointing UP: N = 98 N (since it's not accelerating vertically). (4) If someone pushes it right with 30 N: draw that arrow. (5) If friction opposes with 15 N: draw that arrow left. Net horizontal force: 30 − 15 = 15 N right. Acceleration: a = 15/10 = 1.5 m/s² right.</p>
<p>A chandelier hanging from two chains at 30°: three forces — weight down, tension T₁ up-left, tension T₂ up-right. Solve using components: T₁ sin 30° + T₂ sin 30° = mg.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Every physics problem starts with a free-body diagram. It's the first thing an engineer draws before calculating whether a bridge will hold. It's how a structural analyst checks whether a building can withstand an earthquake. It's the first step in designing a roller coaster, a rocket, or a prosthetic limb. The rules are simple: isolate the object, draw ALL forces acting ON it (never include forces BY it), label them clearly, choose coordinate axes, and apply ΣF = ma. Master the free-body diagram, and you've mastered half of mechanics.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a box on a table. The box isolated as a dot with four arrows: weight (↓), normal (↑), push (→), friction (←). Each labelled with its value. Below: the equation ΣF_x = push − friction = ma. A second diagram: a chandelier with two angled tension arrows. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-872 — Balanced forces: when nothing moves

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** equilibrium; balanced-forces; static; net-force-zero | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-868, BB-NEW-871]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When all the forces on an object cancel out perfectly, the net force is zero. The object doesn't accelerate — it stays at rest or moves in a straight line at constant speed. This is equilibrium. A chair holding a person, a bridge supporting cars, a picture hanging on a wall — all are in equilibrium. The forces are balanced, even though they're not zero individually.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 5 kg picture hangs from a single wire. Weight = 49 N downward. The wire pulls up with 49 N — equilibrium. A 80 kg person stands on a floor. Weight = 784 N down. Normal force = 784 N up. ΣF = 0 — equilibrium. A car moving at constant 100 km/h on a flat highway: engine force forward ≈ 500 N, air resistance backward ≈ 500 N. Net force = 0 — the car is in dynamic equilibrium (constant velocity). A skydiver at terminal velocity (about 55 m/s = 200 km/h): weight down = 700 N, air resistance up = 700 N. Net force = 0. The forces are balanced, so speed is constant.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Equilibrium</strong> means ΣF = 0 — the net force is zero. <strong>Static equilibrium:</strong> object at rest (book on a table). <strong>Dynamic equilibrium:</strong> object moving at constant velocity (car cruising, skydiver at terminal velocity). In both cases, a = 0. For problems: (1) Draw free-body diagram, (2) set ΣF_x = 0 and ΣF_y = 0, (3) solve for unknowns. Equilibrium is the simplest type of mechanics problem — all the forces add to zero. The moment an object accelerates, you need ΣF = ma instead.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of three equilibrium examples. A hanging picture: weight down, tension up — equal. A car cruising: engine forward, air resistance backward — equal. A skydiver: weight down, drag up — equal. Each with ΣF = 0 labelled. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-873 — Unbalanced forces: when things speed up

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** unbalanced-forces; net-force; acceleration; dynamic-motion | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-869, BB-NEW-871, BB-NEW-872]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When forces don't cancel, the object accelerates. A car speeding up, a plane taking off, a ball falling — all have unbalanced forces. The net force (what's left after adding all the forces as vectors) is not zero, and by F = ma, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. This is dynamic motion — the most common situation in the real world.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 50 kg crate is pushed with 200 N across a floor. Friction pushes back with 80 N. Net force = 200 − 80 = 120 N forward. a = 120/50 = 2.4 m/s². After 3 seconds: v = at = 2.4 × 3 = 7.2 m/s (26 km/h). A skydiver has just jumped — weight down = 700 N, air resistance initially 0 N. Net force = 700 N down. a = 700/70 = 10 m/s² ≈ g. Speed increases rapidly. As speed builds, air resistance grows. At terminal velocity (55 m/s), air resistance = 700 N, net force = 0, a = 0 — back to equilibrium. A rocket at launch: thrust 1,000,000 N up, weight 500,000 N down. Net force 500,000 N up. a = 500,000/50,000 = 10 m/s² — about 1g. As fuel burns, mass decreases, acceleration increases.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Unbalanced forces drive every vehicle, every sport, every natural phenomenon. A sprinter bursts from the blocks: the ground pushes forward (friction) > air resistance backward — net force forward. A plane climbs: lift > weight, thrust > drag — net force upward and forward. An earthquake shakes a building: the ground acceleration creates unbalanced forces at every floor, and the structure must withstand them. The skill is always the same: find all the forces, add them as vectors, and if the sum isn't zero, divide by the mass to find the acceleration. F = ma — the equation that launched a thousand rockets.</p>

A skydiver sequence: t=0 (a=g), t=5s (a<g, drag building), t=15s (a=0, terminal velocity). A rocket: thrust up, weight down, net force up. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-874 — The three laws together: how forces rule motion

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** newtons-laws-synthesis; force-motion-unification | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-868, BB-NEW-869, BB-NEW-870, BB-NEW-871]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Newton's three laws work together. The First Law defines the default: no force means no change. The Second Law quantifies change: F = ma relates force, mass, and acceleration. The Third Law ensures forces come in pairs: every push has an equal push back. Together, they explain every motion in the universe — from the fall of an apple to the orbit of a galaxy. These are the rules of the game.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A rocket launch uses all three laws. First Law: the rocket sits on the pad — at rest, stays at rest — until the engines fire. Second Law: F = ma — thrust of 1,000,000 N on a 50,000 kg rocket gives a = 20 m/s² upward. Third Law: the engines push exhaust down; the exhaust pushes the rocket up. All three laws operate simultaneously, in every frame of the launch video. A car accelerating from a stoplight: engine pushes wheels backward (Third Law — wheels push ground, ground pushes car), net force forward (Second Law — a = F/m), and without the force it would stay at rest (First Law). They're not three separate ideas — they're three aspects of one unified description of how forces cause motion.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Every machine ever built obeys these three laws. An aircraft in flight: wings push air down (Third), air pushes wings up — when lift > weight, the plane accelerates upward (Second). In cruise, lift = weight and thrust = drag, so a = 0 (First — dynamic equilibrium). A bridge: every beam and cable is in static equilibrium — First Law, with forces resolved and summed to zero. A collision: two cars hitting each other — the forces are equal (Third), the accelerations depend on mass (Second), and after the crash, the wreckage slides to a stop as friction (an unbalanced force) decelerates it (Second again). Newton's three laws are not museum pieces — they are the operating system of the physical world.</p>

Third Law: "exhaust down = rocket up." A car, a bridge, and a collision with the same annotations. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-875 — The forces toolkit

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** mechanics | **Concept:** forces-summary; gravity-friction-normal-tension-newton-synthesis | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-860 through BB-NEW-874]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>After 15 BBs, the foundation is laid. Force is a push or pull measured in newtons. The main forces are gravity (weight = mg), friction (opposes motion), normal (surface pushes back), and tension (rope pulls). Mass is how much stuff; weight is the pull of gravity on that stuff. Speed is distance/time; acceleration is change in speed/time. Newton's three laws unify everything: things keep doing what they're doing unless pushed, F = ma tells you how much they accelerate, and forces always come in equal opposite pairs.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Identify the principle:</p>
<p>• You push a stalled car: you're applying a force. The car's mass resists (inertia). F = ma determines how fast it accelerates.</p>
<p>• A book on a table: weight down, normal up. Balanced — no acceleration.</p>
<p>• A skydiver: initially weight >> air resistance → accelerating down. Eventually equal → constant speed.</p>
<p>• A rocket: thrust up, weight down. If thrust > weight → accelerating up. Exhaust down = rocket up (Third Law).</p>
<p>• A car braking: friction between tyres and road decelerates the car. F = ma → a = F_friction/m.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Forces toolkit:</strong></p>
<p>• <strong>Force:</strong> push or pull (N). <strong>Weight:</strong> w = mg — gravitational force.</p>
<p>• <strong>Friction:</strong> f = μN — opposes motion. Static (before motion) vs kinetic (during).</p>
<p>• <strong>Normal:</strong> N = mg cos θ — surface pushes perpendicular.</p>
<p>• <strong>Tension:</strong> T — rope pulls, never pushes.</p>
<p>• <strong>Mass:</strong> m (kg) — amount of matter. Inertia.</p>
<p>• <strong>Speed:</strong> v = d/t (m/s). <strong>Acceleration:</strong> a = Δv/Δt (m/s²).</p>
<p>• <strong>Newton I:</strong> ΣF = 0 ⇔ v constant. <strong>Newton II:</strong> F = ma. <strong>Newton III:</strong> F_AB = −F_BA.</p>
<p>• <strong>Free-body diagram:</strong> isolate object, draw ALL forces, ΣF = ma.</p>
<p>• <strong>Equilibrium:</strong> ΣF = 0. <strong>Unbalanced:</strong> ΣF ≠ 0 → a ≠ 0.</p>

A person pushing a box (force), an apple falling (gravity), hands rubbing (friction), a rope pulling (tension), a floor supporting (normal). The three laws written beside each.
