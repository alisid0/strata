# Micro-Frye — Batch 05: Heat & Matter (15 BBs)

These 15 BBs explain what heat actually is — the microscopic motion of atoms — and how it flows. From the difference between temperature and heat, through conduction/convection/radiation, to the gas laws and kinetic theory. Every BB is anchored in everyday experience: a hot drink cooling, a metal spoon in boiling water, a car radiator, the Sun's warmth on your skin. No prior thermodynamics assumed.

## BB-NEW-916 — Temperature: how hot is it?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** temperature; Celsius; Kelvin; thermometer; thermal-equilibrium | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** None — this is the beginning

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Temperature tells you how hot or cold something is — specifically, it measures the average kinetic energy of the particles inside. The faster the atoms and molecules are jiggling, the higher the temperature. A thermometer works by letting its liquid (mercury or alcohol) expand or contract until it matches the temperature of whatever it's touching. This is thermal equilibrium: when two things are at the same temperature, no net heat flows between them.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Water freezes at 0°C (32°F) and boils at 100°C (212°F) — at sea level. Human body temperature: about 37°C. Room temperature: about 20°C. A fridge: about 4°C. A freezer: about −18°C. Liquid nitrogen: −196°C. The surface of the Sun: about 5500°C. The coldest possible temperature is −273.15°C — absolute zero — where all particle motion stops. In the Kelvin scale (the scientific one), water freezes at 273.15 K, and absolute zero is 0 K. A change of 1 K is the same as a change of 1°C — only the starting point differs.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Temperature</strong> is a measure of the average translational kinetic energy of particles. Unit: kelvin (K) — the SI base unit. Also degrees Celsius (°C): T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15. Fahrenheit (°F): T(°F) = (9/5)T(°C) + 32. <strong>Thermal equilibrium:</strong> when two objects are at the same temperature — the zeroth law of thermodynamics. Temperature is NOT the same as heat — a spark from a grinding wheel can be 1000°C but has almost no heat energy; a swimming pool at 25°C contains enormous heat energy.</p>

## BB-NEW-917 — Heat: the energy of jiggling atoms

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** heat; thermal-energy; heat-vs-temperature; Joule; calorie | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-916, BB-NEW-876]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Heat is thermal energy — the total kinetic energy of all the randomly jiggling atoms and molecules in a substance. It's measured in joules, like all energy. Temperature tells you the average speed of the particles; heat tells you how much total energy is in the motion. A teaspoon of water at 100°C has high temperature but little heat. A swimming pool at 25°C has modest temperature but enormous heat — there are so many more water molecules.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A teaspoon (5 mL = 5 g) of water at 100°C has thermal energy roughly 5 × 4200 × 100 = 2.1 MJ — but that's relative to 0 K, which is not what matters. The heat energy available to burn you is much less. A swimming pool: 50 m × 25 m × 2 m = 2,500,000 L = 2.5 × 10⁶ kg of water. At 25°C above absolute zero, the thermal energy is astronomical — but the temperature is only 25°C. This is why a hot spark (very high T, tiny mass) doesn't burn you, but hot bath water (moderate T, large mass) does. The old unit, the calorie: 1 cal = energy to raise 1 g of water by 1°C. 1 cal = 4.184 J. A food Calorie (with capital C) = 1000 cal = 4184 J.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Heat Q</strong> is thermal energy transferred due to a temperature difference. Measured in joules (J). <strong>Temperature T</strong> is proportional to average particle KE: KE_avg = (3/2)k_B T, where k_B = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann's constant). Heat capacity C = Q/ΔT — energy needed to raise temperature by 1 K. Specific heat capacity c = C/m — energy to raise 1 kg by 1 K. Water: c = 4200 J/(kg·K) — unusually high. Heat and temperature are distinct: a match flame (~600°C) has little heat; a warm bath (~40°C) has much more.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing showing the difference: a teaspoon of very hot water (small mass, high T) vs a swimming pool (huge mass, moderate T). Each with many jiggling atoms. Formula Q = mcΔT. 1 cal = 4.184 J. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-918 — Specific heat: why water takes so long to boil

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** specific-heat-capacity; c=Q/mΔT; water-high-c; practical-importance | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-917]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Different materials take different amounts of energy to heat up. Water has one of the highest specific heat capacities of any common substance: 4200 joules to raise 1 kg by 1°C. Metals have much lower specific heats — aluminium needs only 900 J/(kg·K), iron about 450. That's why a metal spoon in hot water heats up fast, but the water itself takes ages to boil. Water's high specific heat is why oceans moderate Earth's climate and why water is used as a coolant in car engines.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>To boil 1 litre (1 kg) of water from 20°C to 100°C: Q = mcΔT = 1 × 4200 × 80 = 336,000 J = 336 kJ. A 2 kW kettle delivers 2000 J/s, so time = 336,000/2000 = 168 s ≈ 2.8 minutes. In practice it takes longer because some heat escapes. To heat 1 kg of iron from 20°C to 100°C: Q = 1 × 450 × 80 = 36,000 J — about one-tenth the energy. A car engine: water circulates through the engine block, absorbs heat (thanks to high c), and releases it in the radiator. Without water's high specific heat, engines would overheat in seconds. Coastal cities stay cooler in summer and warmer in winter than inland cities — the ocean acts as a giant thermal buffer.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Specific heat capacity c:</strong> the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K. Units: J/(kg·K). Formula: Q = mcΔT. <strong>Water:</strong> c = 4200 J/(kg·K). <strong>Aluminium:</strong> 900. <strong>Iron:</strong> 450. <strong>Copper:</strong> 385. <strong>Air:</strong> ~1000. <strong>Lead:</strong> 128 (heats up very fast — good for soldering). Specific heat explains why landmasses heat up and cool down faster than oceans (continental climate vs maritime climate), and why the same amount of solar energy produces different temperature changes on different surfaces.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a kettle with 1 L water. Labels: m = 1 kg, c = 4200 J/(kg·K), ΔT = 80 K, Q = 336 kJ, time = 168 s. A comparison: 1 kg water (336 kJ to heat) vs 1 kg iron (36 kJ). A map of coastal vs inland temperatures. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-919 — Latent heat: melting and boiling without getting hotter

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** latent-heat; phase-change; melting; boiling; plateau | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-917]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When ice melts, its temperature stays at 0°C until all of it has melted. When water boils, it stays at 100°C until all of it has boiled away. The energy you're adding doesn't raise the temperature — it breaks the bonds between molecules. This "hidden" energy is latent heat. Latent heat of fusion (melting) for water: 334,000 J/kg. Latent heat of vaporisation (boiling): 2,260,000 J/kg — nearly seven times as much. This is why steam burns are so bad: steam at 100°C contains a huge amount of latent heat that it releases as it condenses on your skin.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>To melt 1 kg of ice at 0°C into water at 0°C: Q = mL_f = 1 × 334,000 = 334 kJ. That's roughly the same energy as heating the resulting water from 0°C to 80°C. To boil away 1 kg of water at 100°C into steam: Q = mL_v = 1 × 2,260,000 = 2260 kJ — enough to heat the original water from 0°C to over 500°C (if it didn't boil). A 2 kW kettle: to boil 1 L dry takes 2,260,000/2000 = 1130 s ≈ 19 minutes — which is why you don't boil a kettle dry without noticing. The latent heat of vaporisation for water is the reason sweating cools you: evaporating 1 gram of sweat removes 2260 J from your skin.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Latent heat L:</strong> energy absorbed or released during a phase change without temperature change. <strong>Latent heat of fusion L_f:</strong> solid ↔ liquid. Water: 334 kJ/kg. <strong>Latent heat of vaporisation L_v:</strong> liquid ↔ gas. Water: 2260 kJ/kg. Formula: Q = mL. During phase change, T is constant — the plateau on a heating curve. Latent heat is the energy needed to overcome intermolecular forces: in melting, to break the crystal structure; in boiling, to completely separate molecules against attraction. Different substances have different latent heats: liquid nitrogen is an effective coolant because of its low L_v (it boils off quickly, carrying away heat).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a heating curve for water. Ice warming (slope), plateau at 0°C (melting — latent heat of fusion), water warming (slope), plateau at 100°C (boiling — latent heat of vaporisation), steam warming. Values: L_f = 334 kJ/kg, L_v = 2260 kJ/kg. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-920 — Thermal expansion: why things get bigger

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** thermal-expansion; expansion-of-solids; expansion-of-liquids; bimetallic-strip | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-916]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled. The atoms jiggle more vigorously and need more space. Metal railway tracks have expansion gaps to stop them buckling in summer. Power lines sag more on hot days. Bridges have expansion joints. A liquid-in-glass thermometer works because the liquid expands more than the glass. Water is unusual: it expands when it freezes, which is why ice floats and why frozen pipes burst.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A 100 m steel rail on a railway line. Temperature rises from 10°C to 35°C (ΔT = 25°C). Coefficient of linear expansion for steel: α ≈ 12 × 10⁻⁶ /°C. Expansion ΔL = α L ΔT = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 × 25 = 0.03 m = 3 cm. That's why rails have expansion gaps. The Eiffel Tower is about 300 m tall and expands by about 15 cm from winter to summer. The Forth Bridge's expansion joints allow about 1.8 m of movement. A bimetallic strip (brass + steel) bends when heated — used in thermostats to switch heating on and off. Mercury in a thermometer expands by about 0.018% per °C, but in a narrow tube that's enough to produce a visible rise.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Linear thermal expansion:</strong> ΔL = α L₀ ΔT, where α is the coefficient of linear expansion (units: /°C or /K). <strong>Volume expansion:</strong> ΔV = β V₀ ΔT, where β ≈ 3α for isotropic solids. <strong>Water anomaly:</strong> water expands on freezing (β is negative between 0°C and 4°C — water is densest at 4°C). This is why lakes freeze from the top down, allowing aquatic life to survive underneath. Thermal expansion must be accounted for in every engineering structure exposed to temperature variations: bridges, pipelines, power lines, concrete slabs, even dental fillings.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a railway track with an expansion gap. Labels: L=100m, ΔL=3cm for ΔT=25°C. A bimetallic strip bending — brass top, steel bottom. A thermometer: liquid expanding in the tube. Water anomaly: ice floating on liquid water. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-921 — Conduction: heat travelling through touch

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** conduction; thermal-conductivity; metals-vs-insulators; Fourier-law | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-917]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Touch a metal spoon in a hot drink — the handle gets hot. Heat travels from the hot end to the cold end through the metal. This is conduction: heat transfer through direct contact, from particle to particle. Metals are excellent conductors because their free electrons carry energy rapidly. Wood, plastic, and air are poor conductors — they're insulators. A metal spoon in boiling water gets too hot to touch; a wooden spoon stays cool.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Thermal conductivity k (W/(m·K)): copper ≈ 400, aluminium ≈ 200, steel ≈ 50, glass ≈ 1, wood ≈ 0.15, air ≈ 0.025, styrofoam ≈ 0.03. Copper is 16,000 times better at conducting heat than dry air. A copper-bottomed pan spreads heat evenly; a cast-iron pan retains heat (lower k, more mass). A double-glazed window: a 12 mm gap of still air (or argon, k ≈ 0.016) between two panes of glass cuts heat loss dramatically. The air gap is the real insulator, not the glass. A down jacket: the tiny air pockets trapped between feathers have very low conductivity — you're wearing trapped air.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Heat conduction:</strong> energy transfer through a material without bulk motion. <strong>Fourier's Law:</strong> Q/t = kA ΔT/L, where k = thermal conductivity, A = cross-sectional area, ΔT = temperature difference, L = thickness. Rate of heat transfer ∝ k, ∝ A, ∝ ΔT, and ∝ 1/L. <strong>Good conductors:</strong> metals (high k, electrons carry heat). <strong>Good insulators:</strong> gases and foams (low k, trapped air). The same principle governs electrical conductivity and thermal conductivity — the Wiedemann-Franz Law says their ratio is proportional to temperature (for metals).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a metal spoon in a cup of hot tea. Heat flowing from hot end to cold end — arrows showing particle-to-particle transfer. Formula Q/t = kAΔT/L. A wall cross-section: double glazing with air gap. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-922 — Convection: heat on the move with the material

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** convection; natural-convection; forced-convection; fluid-motion; radiator | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-921]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Heat can travel by the movement of the heated material itself — convection. Hot air rises because it's less dense. Cold air sinks. A radiator heats a room not by radiation (mostly) but by convection — it warms the air next to it, the warm air rises, circulates around the room, cools, and sinks back down. This pattern is a convection current. Boiling water in a pan shows convection: water at the bottom heats, rises, cools at the surface, and sinks.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A radiator at 60°C in a 20°C room. Air next to the radiator warms to ~30°C, expands (density drops from ~1.2 to ~1.16 kg/m³), and rises at about 0.5–1 m/s. A full circulation takes a minute or two for a typical room. The radiator also emits some radiation, but mostly convection. In a refrigerator: the cold element (freezer compartment) is at the top — cold air sinks, cooling the whole fridge. A fan oven forces convection with a fan (forced convection), cooking food faster and more evenly. Ocean currents and weather systems are gigantic convection cycles driven by the Sun heating the tropics more than the poles.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Convection:</strong> heat transfer by bulk fluid movement. <strong>Natural (free) convection:</strong> density differences drive the flow (hot fluid rises). <strong>Forced convection:</strong> a pump or fan drives the flow (car radiator fan, CPU cooler). Heat transfer rate depends on fluid properties (k, c, viscosity, coefficient of thermal expansion) and flow conditions. <strong>Convection coefficient h:</strong> Q/t = hA ΔT — an empirical value that depends on geometry and flow speed. Typically, h ≈ 5–25 W/(m²·K) for natural convection in air, and 100–1000 for forced convection.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a radiator with air rising (warm), circulating around the room, and sinking (cool). A convection loop. A boiling pot: bubbles rising, water circulating. "Hot rises, cold sinks." Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-923 — Radiation: heat that travels through nothing

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** thermal-radiation; infrared; electromagnetic; Stefan-Boltzmann; emissivity | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-921, BB-NEW-911]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The Sun heats the Earth across 150 million km of empty space. That's radiation — heat transfer by electromagnetic waves, mostly in the infrared. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation needs no medium. Everything above absolute zero emits thermal radiation. The hotter the object, the more and shorter-wavelength radiation it emits. A person glows in the infrared; a red-hot piece of metal glows in visible light; the Sun glows across the spectrum.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A person at 37°C (310 K): surface area ~1.8 m². Radiated power (Stefan-Boltzmann) ≈ εσAT⁴. For skin (ε ≈ 0.97): P ≈ 0.97 × 5.67×10⁻⁸ × 1.8 × 310⁴ ≈ 950 W. That's nearly a kilowatt of invisible radiation pouring off you. But you also absorb radiation from the room (~20°C = 293 K), net loss ≈ 950 − 750 = 200 W — close to the actual basal metabolic rate (~100 W plus losses). A red-hot iron at 800°C (1073 K): the radiation is in the visible — it glows red. At 1300°C: bright yellow-white. The Sun's surface (5778 K): peak emission is green-yellow light (λ_max = b/T = 500 nm). Earth, at 288 K: peak emission at λ ≈ 10 μm (thermal infrared).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Thermal radiation:</strong> EM radiation emitted by matter due to its temperature. All objects above 0 K emit radiation. <strong>Stefan-Boltzmann Law:</strong> P = εσAT⁴, where P = radiated power (W), ε = emissivity (0–1, 1 for a perfect blackbody), σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴), A = surface area, T = absolute temperature. <strong>Wien's Displacement Law:</strong> λ_max = b/T, where b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K. Hotter → shorter peak wavelength. Net radiative heat transfer between two surfaces: P_net = εσA(T_hot⁴ − T_cold⁴). Radiation dominates at high temperatures (T⁴ dependence makes it skyrocket).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of the Sun shining on Earth through empty space. "Radiation needs no medium." A person glowing in infrared. A piece of metal glowing red. The Stefan-Boltzmann equation: P = εσAT⁴. Wien's law: λ_max = b/T. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-924 — The three heat transfer modes together

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** heat-transfer-modes-combined; conduction-convection-radiation; real-world-examples | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-921, BB-NEW-922, BB-NEW-923]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>In the real world, conduction, convection, and radiation usually happen together. A hot cup of coffee loses heat by all three: conduction through the mug to your hand, convection into the air above it, and radiation to the cooler surroundings. A car engine uses all three: conduction through the metal block, convection via water circulating through the radiator, and radiation from the hot engine surfaces. Understanding which mode dominates helps you insulate (or cool) anything.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A hot cup of coffee (80°C) in a 20°C room. Convection: the air above the cup warms and rises, carrying heat away at maybe 5–10 W. Conduction: through the ceramic mug to the table and your hand — maybe 2–5 W. Radiation: from the surface of the coffee (ε ≈ 0.95, area ~0.005 m²), P ≈ 0.95 × 5.67×10⁻⁸ × 0.005 × (353⁴ − 293⁴) ≈ 1.5 W. Total heat loss rate: roughly 10–20 W. A flask (vacuum flask) reduces all three: silvered walls reduce radiation (low ε), the vacuum between walls eliminates conduction/convection through air, and a stopper stops convection out the top. That's why a flask keeps coffee hot for hours. A car radiator: forced convection dominates — the fan blows air through the fins, transferring heat at perhaps 20–50 kW.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Heat transfer summary:</strong> conduction (particle-to-particle, no bulk motion, ∝ kAΔT/L), convection (bulk fluid motion, ∝ hAΔT), radiation (EM waves, no medium, ∝ εσAT⁴). The dominant mode depends on the situation: conduction in solids at moderate ΔT, convection in fluids, radiation at high T (T⁴ dependence). Insulation strategies: for conduction — use low-k materials (foam, air gaps); for convection — stop air movement (double glazing, seals); for radiation — use reflective surfaces (low ε, like aluminium foil on insulation).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a cup of coffee with three coloured arrows: conduction (→), convection (↑), radiation (↗ waves). Labels with approximate power values. A flask cross-section: silvered walls, vacuum, stopper — blocking all three. A car radiator: fan blowing — forced convection. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-925 — Solids, liquids, gases: the three states of matter

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** states-of-matter; solid-liquid-gas; particle-model; arrangement-and-motion | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-916, BB-NEW-917]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Matter exists in three common states: solid, liquid, and gas. The difference is how the particles are arranged and how they move. In a solid, particles are tightly packed in a regular pattern — they vibrate but don't move past each other. In a liquid, particles are still close but can slide past each other. In a gas, particles are far apart and whiz around at high speed, filling whatever container they're in. Temperature is the same thing in all three: the average kinetic energy of the particles.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Water at different temperatures shows all three. Below 0°C: ice — crystalline structure, each water molecule locked in place by hydrogen bonds. At 0°C: melting — bonds break, molecules flow. At 20°C: liquid water — molecules slide past each other, about 0.3 nm apart. At 100°C: boiling — molecules break free entirely, shooting off as steam. Steam at 100°C: each molecule has about 0.04 eV of kinetic energy, zipping around at about 500 m/s on average. A solid keeps its shape. A liquid takes the shape of its container but keeps its volume. A gas expands to fill its container completely. These behaviours all follow from the particle model and the strength of intermolecular forces relative to thermal energy (k_B T).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>State is determined by the competition between intermolecular forces (which want order) and thermal energy k_B T (which wants disorder). In solids: forces dominate — regular lattice, particles vibrate. In liquids: comparable — particles slide, no long-range order. In gases: k_B T dominates — particles move independently, widely spaced. <strong>Plasma:</strong> the fourth state — gas so hot that electrons are stripped from atoms (the Sun, fluorescent lamps). Phase changes occur at temperatures where k_B T becomes comparable to the bond energy. Melting: ~0.1 eV/molecule for many materials. Boiling: ~0.4 eV for water (the energy per hydrogen bond).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of three panels: solid (particles in a grid, vibrating), liquid (particles sliding), gas (particles far apart, flying). Water as the example — molecular spacing labelled: ice (regular), water (close but disordered), steam (spread out). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-926 — Gas pressure: why balloons stay inflated

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** pressure; gas-pressure; kinetic-model; particle-collisions | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-925, BB-NEW-916]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container because billions of molecules are constantly smashing into the walls. Each collision delivers a tiny impulse. Summed over trillions of collisions per second, the force per unit area is the pressure. Inflate a balloon: the rubber stretches until the pressure inside balances the elastic tension plus the outside atmospheric pressure. Heat the air inside a sealed container: the molecules move faster, hit harder, and pressure goes up.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Atmospheric pressure at sea level: 101,325 Pa = 101.3 kPa = 1 atmosphere = 14.7 psi. A car tyre: about 220 kPa (32 psi) above atmospheric — so the absolute pressure inside is 220 + 101 = 321 kPa. A pressure cooker: at 120°C, water's vapour pressure is about 200 kPa — the sealed pot allows the temperature to exceed 100°C, cooking food faster. The ideal gas law: PV = nRT. For 1 mole of gas (22.4 L at STP): P = 101,325 Pa, V = 0.0224 m³, n = 1, R = 8.31 J/(mol·K), T = 273 K. Check: (101325 × 0.0224)/(1 × 8.31) = 273. Good. At double the temperature (546 K): if volume is fixed, pressure doubles to 202.6 kPa.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Pressure P = F/A.</strong> Units: pascal (Pa) = N/m². 1 atm = 101,325 Pa. In the kinetic model: P = (1/3)(N/V) m v_rms², where N/V = number density, m = molecular mass, v_rms = root-mean-square speed. Combining with KE_avg = (3/2)k_B T: P = (N/V) k_B T — this is the ideal gas law in molecular form. <strong>Ideal Gas Law:</strong> PV = nRT = Nk_B T, where n = moles, R = 8.31 J/(mol·K), N = number of molecules, k_B = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a balloon with gas molecules bouncing off the inner walls. Arrows showing molecular impacts generating pressure. Formula P = F/A. The macro law PV = nRT and the micro formula P = (N/V)k_B T. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-927 — Boyle, Charles, and the gas laws

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** Boyles-law; Charles-law; Gay-Lussac; gas-laws; PV=constant | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-926]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The behaviour of gases is described by simple laws. <strong>Boyle's Law:</strong> at constant temperature, pressure × volume is constant — squeeze a gas into half the volume and its pressure doubles. <strong>Charles's Law:</strong> at constant pressure, volume is proportional to temperature — heat a balloon and it expands. <strong>Gay-Lussac's Law:</strong> at constant volume, pressure is proportional to temperature — heat a sealed can and the pressure rises until it explodes. Together, they form the Ideal Gas Law: PV = nRT.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p><strong>Boyle:</strong> a 10 L container of air at 200 kPa — if you compress it to 5 L at constant T, pressure doubles to 400 kPa. A syringe with the nozzle sealed: push the plunger in, it resists harder — P ∝ 1/V. <strong>Charles:</strong> a helium balloon at 20°C (293 K) has volume 1 L. Cool it to −73°C (200 K) and volume shrinks to 1 × 200/293 = 0.68 L. Leave a balloon in a cold car overnight — it looks deflated. <strong>Combined (all three vary):</strong> air in a bike pump — as you push the plunger, V decreases, P increases, and T rises (the pump gets warm). The ideal gas law PV = nRT handles all three at once.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Boyle's Law:</strong> PV = constant (T and n fixed). <strong>Charles's Law:</strong> V/T = constant (P and n fixed). <strong>Gay-Lussac's (Amontons's) Law:</strong> P/T = constant (V and n fixed). <strong>Ideal Gas Law:</strong> PV = nRT, where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). <strong>Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP):</strong> T = 273.15 K, P = 1 atm = 101.325 kPa. One mole of ideal gas at STP occupies 22.4 L. The gas laws are statistical: they work for large numbers of molecules (N ≫ 1) and for gases that don't interact strongly. Real gases deviate at high pressure or low temperature (van der Waals equation adds corrections).</p>

## BB-NEW-928 — Kinetic theory: the microscopic view of gases

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** kinetic-theory; molecular-speed; Maxwell-Boltzmann-distribution; equipartition | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-926, BB-NEW-916]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Kinetic theory explains gas behaviour by treating gas molecules as tiny, perfectly elastic balls whizzing around. It connects the microscopic world (molecular mass, speed) to the macroscopic world (pressure, temperature). The average kinetic energy of a gas molecule is (3/2)k_B T — about 0.04 eV at room temperature. The speed of the molecules is stunning: nitrogen molecules in air at 20°C average about 500 m/s — faster than a bullet from a handgun. But they travel only about 70 nanometres before hitting another molecule.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Air at 20°C: nitrogen (N₂, mass 28 u = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg). KE_avg = (3/2)k_B T = (3/2) × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 293 = 6.07 × 10⁻²¹ J. v_rms = √(2KE/m) = √(2 × 6.07 × 10⁻²¹ / 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 511 m/s. Fastest molecules in the distribution: ~1500 m/s. Slowest: ~200 m/s. Mean free path (average distance between collisions): λ = 1/(√2 π d² n_V), where d = molecular diameter (~0.37 nm for N₂), n_V = number density (~2.5 × 10²⁵ m⁻³). λ ≈ 70 nm — 70 billionths of a metre. Each molecule undergoes about 7 billion collisions per second.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Kinetic theory assumptions:</strong> (1) Gas consists of many identical molecules in random motion. (2) Molecules are point-like compared to their separation. (3) Collisions are perfectly elastic. (4) No intermolecular forces except during collisions. (5) Newton's laws apply. <strong>Key results:</strong> KE_avg = (3/2)k_B T (equipartition — ½k_B T per degree of freedom). v_rms = √(3k_B T/m) = √(3RT/M). <strong>Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution:</strong> the spread of molecular speeds — not all molecules move at the same speed. Speed of sound in a gas: v = √(γRT/M) — linked to molecular speed (v_rms × √(γ/3)).</p>

## BB-NEW-929 — Refrigeration and heat pumps: moving heat uphill

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** refrigeration; heat-pump; phase-change-cooling; reverse-thermal-flow | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-919, BB-NEW-922]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A fridge doesn't "make cold" — it pumps heat from inside to outside. It does this by cycling a refrigerant gas that evaporates inside (absorbing heat, like sweating) and condenses outside (releasing heat). The compressor does the work of squeezing the gas, raising its temperature above room temperature so it can dump its heat. The same cycle in reverse is a heat pump for heating a house — extracting heat from the cold outdoor air and dumping it inside. A heat pump can deliver 3–4 times more heat energy than the electrical energy it consumes.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A typical fridge: the refrigerant (e.g., R-134a) evaporates in the cold coils inside the freezer at about −30°C, absorbing about 200 kJ per kg of refrigerant. The compressor raises its pressure and temperature to about 70°C. In the warm coils at the back of the fridge, it condenses, releasing heat into the room. The liquid then expands through a valve, dropping its pressure and temperature, and the cycle repeats. A modern fridge might use 150 W of electricity average, pumping about 300 W of heat out of the interior. A domestic heat pump: for 1 kW of electrical input, it delivers 3–4 kW of heat into the house — the extra 2–3 kW comes from the outside air or ground. Coefficient of Performance (COP) = heat_out / work_in ≈ 3–4.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Refrigeration cycle:</strong> (1) Evaporator — refrigerant evaporates at low P and T, absorbing Q_cold. (2) Compressor — work W compresses the gas, raising P and T. (3) Condenser — refrigerant condenses at high P and T, releasing Q_hot = Q_cold + W. (4) Expansion valve — pressure drops, T drops. <strong>Coefficient of Performance:</strong> COP = Q_cold/W (refrigerator) or Q_hot/W (heat pump). Theoretical max (Carnot): COP_max = T_cold/(T_hot − T_cold). Real systems achieve 40–60% of Carnot. Heat pumps are more efficient than resistive heating because they move existing heat rather than creating it — the electricity only powers the pump.</p>

## BB-NEW-930 — The heat toolkit

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** heat | **Concept:** heat-summary; temperature-heat-conduction-convection-radiation-phase-change-gas-laws | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-916 through BB-NEW-929]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Heat is thermal energy — the random kinetic energy of atoms and molecules. Temperature measures the average. Heat flows from hot to cold by conduction (touch), convection (fluid motion), and radiation (EM waves). Phase changes absorb or release latent heat at constant temperature. Gases follow PV = nRT. Kinetic theory connects the microscopic molecular motion to macroscopic pressure and temperature. Refrigeration and heat pumps move heat uphill using phase-change cycles.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Key numbers to carry:</p>
<p>• Water specific heat c = 4200 J/(kg·K).</p>
<p>• Water L_f (fusion) = 334 kJ/kg. L_v (vaporisation) = 2260 kJ/kg.</p>
<p>• Atmospheric pressure: 101.3 kPa = 1 atm.</p>
<p>• Speed of air molecules (N₂) at 20°C: ~500 m/s.</p>
<p>• Copper thermal conductivity: k ≈ 400 W/(m·K). Air: 0.025.</p>
<p>• Human radiative output: ~950 W (absorbed: ~750 W, net: ~200 W).</p>
<p>• Ideal gas constant R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). Boltzmann k_B = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K.</p>
<p>• MEan free path in air: ~70 nm.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Heat toolkit:</strong></p>
<p>• <strong>Temperature T (K, °C):</strong> average KE. KE_avg = (3/2)k_B T.</p>
<p>• <strong>Heat Q (J):</strong> thermal energy transferred. Q = mcΔT. Q = mL (phase change).</p>
<p>• <strong>Conduction:</strong> Q/t = kA ΔT/L. <strong>Convection:</strong> Q/t = hA ΔT. <strong>Radiation:</strong> P = εσAT⁴.</p>
<p>• <strong>States:</strong> solid (ordered, vibrating), liquid (sliding), gas (free, fast).</p>
<p>• <strong>Gas laws:</strong> Boyle (PV = constant), Charles (V/T = constant), Ideal (PV = nRT).</p>
<p>• <strong>Pressure:</strong> P = F/A = (N/V)k_B T. From molecular impacts.</p>
<p>• <strong>Kinetic theory:</strong> v_rms = √(3RT/M). Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution.</p>
<p>• <strong>Refrigeration:</strong> evaporator (absorbs Q_cold) → compressor → condenser (releases Q_hot). COP = Q_cold/W.</p>
