# Micro-Frye — Batch 04: Waves & Oscillations (15 BBs)

These 15 BBs build wave thinking from zero. Start with a single vibration (oscillation), then see how that vibration travels (wave), then encounter the big ideas — frequency, wavelength, speed, reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference, resonance. Every BB uses concrete, visible examples: ripples in a pond, a guitar string, a pendulum, your own voice. No prior knowledge of waves is assumed beyond having noticed them.

## BB-NEW-901 — What is an oscillation?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** oscillation; vibration; periodic-motion; pendulum; spring-mass | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-883, BB-NEW-872]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>An oscillation is something moving back and forth, repeating. A playground swing, a guitar string after being plucked, a pendulum in a clock, your chest as you breathe — all oscillate. An oscillation has a regular, repeating pattern. The time for one complete back-and-forth is the period (T). The number of complete swings per second is the frequency (f). f = 1/T. If a swing takes 2 seconds for one swing, its frequency is 0.5 Hz (hertz — cycles per second).</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A pendulum: a 1 m string with a weight on the end swings with a period of about 2.0 seconds (T = 2π√(L/g) = 2π√(1/9.8) ≈ 2.01 s). Frequency = 0.5 Hz. A shorter 0.25 m pendulum: T ≈ 1.0 s, f = 1 Hz. A spring with mass: T = 2π√(m/k). A car's suspension (m ≈ 300 kg per corner, k ≈ 20,000 N/m) has T ≈ 2π√(300/20000) = 0.77 s — about 1.3 Hz. This is the bounce frequency over bumps. Your heartbeat: about 1.2 Hz (72 bpm). A hummingbird's wings: 50–80 Hz. The fastest oscillation you'll encounter: the clock in your phone's processor at about 3,000,000,000 Hz (3 GHz).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Oscillation:</strong> repetitive variation around a central value. <strong>Period T:</strong> time for one complete cycle (seconds). <strong>Frequency f = 1/T:</strong> cycles per second (hertz, Hz). <strong>Amplitude A:</strong> maximum displacement from equilibrium. <strong>Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM):</strong> a special oscillation where the restoring force is proportional to displacement (F = −kx). In SHM: T = 2π√(m/k) for mass-spring, T = 2π√(L/g) for pendulum. The motion is sinusoidal: x(t) = A sin(2πft).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a pendulum at three positions: centre (equilibrium), far left, far right. Labels: amplitude A, period T. A spring-mass system. The equation T = 2π√(L/g). A sine wave emerging from the motion. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-902 — What is a wave?

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** wave; disturbance-propagation; transverse-vs-longitudinal; medium | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-901]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium (or through space), carrying energy without carrying matter. Drop a pebble in a pond: the ripples spread outward, but the water molecules don't travel with them — they just bob up and down as the wave passes. A Mexican wave in a stadium: the people stand up and sit down in place, but the "wave" travels around the stadium. Energy moves; matter doesn't.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Two kinds: <strong>Transverse waves:</strong> the vibration is perpendicular to the direction of travel. Water ripples (the surface moves up and down, the wave moves sideways), light, radio waves, and plucked guitar strings. <strong>Longitudinal waves:</strong> the vibration is parallel to the direction of travel. Sound waves in air — air molecules vibrate back and forth in the same direction the sound travels. A slinky spring demonstrates both: stretch it out, wiggle one end side-to-side (transverse), or push-and-pull (longitudinal). Earthquakes produce both types: P-waves (primary, longitudinal, faster) and S-waves (secondary, transverse, slower).</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Wave:</strong> a propagating disturbance that transfers energy through a medium or vacuum. <strong>Mechanical waves</strong> require a medium (sound, water, seismic, string). <strong>Electromagnetic waves</strong> do not (light, radio, X-rays) — they can travel through a vacuum. <strong>Key quantities:</strong> wavelength λ (distance between crests), frequency f (cycles per second), amplitude A (height of disturbance), speed v (how fast the disturbance moves — v = fλ). The wave speed depends on the medium's properties, not on the frequency or amplitude. Particles of the medium oscillate around fixed positions — they don't travel with the wave.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a pebble dropping into water. Circular ripples spreading. Labels: direction of wave travel (horizontal), direction of water motion (up/down). A slinky: transverse (side-to-side wiggle) and longitudinal (push-pull). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-903 — Frequency and wavelength: how to describe a wave

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** frequency; wavelength; wave-speed; v=fλ; period | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-901, BB-NEW-902]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every wave has a frequency (how often the peaks pass a point) and a wavelength (the distance from one peak to the next). They're linked by the wave speed: v = fλ. If the frequency doubles and the speed stays the same, the wavelength halves. Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz). Wavelength is in metres. Sound and light are both waves, but their frequencies and wavelengths are vastly different.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Middle C on a piano: sound frequency = 261.6 Hz. Speed of sound in air ≈ 343 m/s. Wavelength λ = v/f = 343/261.6 = 1.31 m — about the length of a trumpet. A dog whistle: 30,000 Hz (ultrasonic). λ = 343/30000 = 0.011 m = 1.1 cm. Visible light: green light has f ≈ 5.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz and λ ≈ 550 nanometres (0.00000055 m). Speed of light c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Check: v = fλ = 5.5 × 10¹⁴ × 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.025 × 10⁸ ≈ c. Radio waves: BBC Radio 4 on 198 kHz. λ = 3 × 10⁸/198,000 = 1515 m — the wavelength is longer than a kilometre!</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Frequency f:</strong> number of complete cycles per second. Units: hertz (Hz) = s⁻¹. <strong>Wavelength λ:</strong> distance between two consecutive identical points (crest to crest, trough to trough). Units: metres. <strong>Wave speed v = fλ:</strong> the fundamental wave equation. For a given medium, v is constant — f and λ are inversely proportional. <strong>Human hearing:</strong> 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. <strong>Visible light:</strong> 4.3×10¹⁴ Hz (red, λ = 700 nm) to 7.5×10¹⁴ Hz (violet, λ = 400 nm).</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a sine wave with λ labelled (crest to crest) and f labelled (cycles per second). v = fλ prominently. A comparison: sound wave (λ ≈ 1.3 m), green light (λ = 550 nm), radio wave (λ = 1.5 km). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-904 — Sound waves: vibrating air

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** sound; longitudinal-wave; compression-rarefaction; speed-of-sound | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-902, BB-NEW-903]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Sound is a longitudinal wave in air. When you speak, your vocal cords vibrate, pushing and pulling on the air molecules. The molecules bunch together (compression) and spread apart (rarefaction) in a pattern that travels outward at about 343 metres per second. Sound can't travel in a vacuum — there are no molecules to vibrate. That's why space is silent. Sound travels faster in water (~1500 m/s) and even faster in steel (~5000 m/s) because the molecules are closer together.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Thunder and lightning: light reaches you almost instantly (300,000,000 m/s). Sound follows at 343 m/s. Count the seconds between flash and boom, multiply by 343 — that's how far away the lightning is. 3 seconds ≈ 1 km. A jet at Mach 2: twice the speed of sound — 686 m/s = 2470 km/h. The sonic boom is a shock wave from the aircraft overtaking its own sound waves. Echolocation: bats emit ultrasonic clicks (up to 100 kHz) and time the echo. In water, sound travels ~1500 m/s, so an echo taking 0.1 seconds means the object is 1500 × 0.1/2 = 75 m away. Sonar works the same way.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Sound</strong> is a longitudinal mechanical wave. Speed in air: v ≈ 331 + 0.6T (m/s), where T is temperature in °C. At 20°C: 343 m/s. <strong>Pitch = frequency:</strong> high frequency = high pitch. <strong>Loudness = amplitude:</strong> large amplitude = loud. Sound intensity is measured in decibels (dB) — a logarithmic scale. 0 dB = threshold of hearing. 60 dB = normal conversation. 120 dB = threshold of pain. <strong>Infrasound:</strong> <20 Hz (elephants communicate). <strong>Ultrasound:</strong> >20 kHz (medical imaging, 1–15 MHz). <strong>Speed depends on the medium:</strong> faster in denser, stiffer materials.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of air molecules: compressions (bunched together) and rarefactions (spread apart) travelling outward. A tuning fork vibrating. Speed of sound in air (343 m/s), water (1500 m/s), steel (5000 m/s). Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-905 — How waves reflect and refract

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** reflection; refraction; boundary-behaviour; speed-change | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-902, BB-NEW-903]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When a wave hits a boundary, two things can happen. It can bounce back — reflection (like a mirror, or an echo). Or it can pass through but change direction — refraction (like a straw appearing bent in a glass of water). Reflection happens at any boundary. Refraction happens when the wave speed changes — e.g., light going from air to water, or sound going from warm air to cold air.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p><strong>Reflection:</strong> sound echoes off a cliff. If you shout and the echo returns after 2 seconds, the cliff is 343 × 2/2 = 343 m away. Light reflects off water — you see the sky mirrored on a lake. Radar works by reflecting radio waves off aircraft. <strong>Refraction:</strong> a pool looks shallower than it is because light bends as it enters the water (and speeds up as it leaves). A prism splits white light into colours because different frequencies refract by different amounts. Sound refracts downward on a warm day (sound travels faster in warm air, so waves curve downward), which is why sound carries farther over water on a calm morning — the air above cool water bends sound waves back toward the surface.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Law of reflection:</strong> angle of incidence = angle of reflection (θ_i = θ_r). Measured from the normal (perpendicular to the surface). <strong>Refraction:</strong> when a wave passes into a medium where its speed is different, it bends toward or away from the normal. Snell's Law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n = c/v is the refractive index. Light slows in water (n = 1.33) and glass (n ≈ 1.5), so it bends toward the normal. <strong>Total internal reflection:</strong> when light in a dense medium hits a boundary at a steep enough angle, it reflects completely — this is how fibre optics work.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a light ray hitting a mirror: θ_i = θ_r. A straw in a glass: bent appearance. The prism: white light in, rainbow out. A fibre optic cable: light bouncing inside. Snell's Law written beside. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-906 — Diffraction: waves bend around corners

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** diffraction; wave-bending; gap-size; wavelength-dependence | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-902, BB-NEW-903]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Waves don't just go in straight lines. When a wave passes through a narrow gap or around an obstacle, it spreads out — it bends around corners. This is diffraction. You can hear someone talking in the next room even though you can't see them — sound waves diffract through the doorway because their wavelength (about 1 m) is similar to the door width. Light doesn't noticeably diffract through a doorway because its wavelength is 500 billionths of a metre — the door is enormous compared to λ.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Water waves in a ripple tank: a straight wave passes through a gap. If the gap is wide compared to the wavelength, it passes through mostly straight. If the gap is about the same size as the wavelength, the wave spreads into semicircles. Sound: λ ≈ 1 m. A doorway (~1 m wide) diffracts sound strongly — that's why you can hear around corners. Light: λ ≈ 0.0005 mm. A human hair (~0.1 mm wide) is about 200 wavelengths across — it barely diffracts light. A narrow slit (~0.01 mm) diffracts visible light into a pattern of bright and dark bands. X-rays (λ ≈ 0.1 nm = atom-size) diffract off the regular spacing of atoms in a crystal — this is how we discovered the structure of DNA.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Diffraction:</strong> the bending and spreading of waves when they encounter an obstacle or aperture. Significant when the gap size is comparable to the wavelength. Larger wavelength → more diffraction. This is why AM radio (λ ~ 300 m) can be received behind hills that block FM radio (λ ~ 3 m). <strong>Single-slit diffraction</strong> produces a central bright maximum flanked by dimmer bands. <strong>Diffraction gratings</strong> (many parallel slits) split light into its component wavelengths — used in spectrometers. The resolution of microscopes and telescopes is limited by diffraction — you can't see details smaller than about half the wavelength of the light you're using.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of ripples passing through a narrow gap — spreading into semicircles. A wide gap: mostly straight. A door with sound waves (λ ≈ 1 m) diffracting through. Light (λ ≈ 500 nm) not diffracting through the same door. X-rays diffracting off a crystal lattice. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-907 — Interference: waves adding and cancelling

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** interference; constructive; destructive; superposition; beats | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-902, BB-NEW-903]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>When two waves meet, they add together. If their peaks align, you get a bigger wave — constructive interference. If a peak aligns with a trough, they cancel — destructive interference. This is why two waves from two pebbles dropped in a pond create a complex pattern of bright and calm spots. It's also how noise-cancelling headphones work: they generate a sound wave that's exactly the inverse of the outside noise, cancelling it out.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Two speakers playing the same tone: if one is placed half a wavelength farther from your ear, the waves arrive out of phase and you hear silence (destructive interference). Move your head slightly and the sound comes back. Noise-cancelling headphones: tiny microphones capture external noise, a circuit inverts the waveform, and a speaker plays the anti-noise — the superposition at your eardrum is silence. Soap bubbles and oil slicks show coloured interference patterns: light reflects from the top and bottom of the thin film. If the film thickness is such that a particular colour's waves cancel, you see its complementary colour. A thin film of oil on water (thickness ~ 0.0001 mm) produces vivid rainbows via thin-film interference.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Superposition principle:</strong> when waves overlap, the net displacement is the sum of the individual displacements. <strong>Constructive interference:</strong> waves are in phase — amplitude adds. <strong>Destructive interference:</strong> waves are 180° out of phase — amplitudes subtract (can cancel completely). <strong>Coherent sources:</strong> same frequency, constant phase difference — produce stable interference patterns. <strong>Young's double-slit experiment</strong> (1801) demonstrated light is a wave by producing interference fringes. <strong>Beats:</strong> two slightly different frequencies produce a pulsing loudness at the difference frequency — f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of two wave sources (circles radiating). Where crests overlap: bright spots (constructive). Where crest meets trough: calm spots (destructive). Noise-cancelling headphones: noise + anti-noise = silence. A soap bubble with thin-film colours. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-908 — Resonance: when you push at the right time

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** resonance; natural-frequency; forced-oscillation; standing-waves | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-901, BB-NEW-902]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every object has a natural frequency — the rate at which it "likes" to vibrate. Push a swing at its natural frequency, and the amplitude grows with each push. This is resonance. A glass shatters when an opera singer hits its resonant frequency. A bridge collapsed (Tacoma Narrows, 1940) because wind drove it at its resonant frequency. Resonance can be catastrophic — or useful, as in a radio receiver tuned to one station's frequency.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A child on a swing: natural period ~2 seconds, f = 0.5 Hz. A small push at 0.5 Hz makes the amplitude grow from a few cm to several metres. A wine glass: natural frequency ~500–800 Hz (depending on size). Tap it with a fingernail — it rings at that frequency. An opera singer at the right pitch with enough volume can drive the glass's vibration until it shatters. A microwave oven: the microwaves are at 2.45 GHz — the resonant frequency of water molecules. The water in food absorbs the energy and heats up. A radio: you tune the circuit's resonant frequency to match the station's broadcast frequency — resonance amplifies that one signal while rejecting all others.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Resonance:</strong> the tendency of a system to oscillate with maximum amplitude at its natural frequency when driven by an external force. <strong>Natural frequency:</strong> determined by the system's physical properties (mass, stiffness, length). <strong>Standing wave:</strong> a pattern that forms when reflected waves interfere with incoming waves at the natural frequency — nodes (zero motion) and antinodes (maximum motion). Standing waves on a string: λ = 2L/n. <strong>Q factor (quality factor):</strong> measures how sharply a system resonates — high Q = very narrow, strong resonance.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a child on a swing being pushed at the right moment — amplitude growing. A wine glass shattering with sound waves. The Tacoma Narrows bridge twisting. A standing wave on a string: nodes and antinodes labelled. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-909 — Music and harmonics: why instruments sound different

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** harmonics; overtones; standing-waves; timbre; fundamental | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-904, BB-NEW-908]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>A guitar and a flute can play the same note, but they sound different. Why? Because a real vibrating string or air column doesn't just vibrate at one frequency — it vibrates at many frequencies simultaneously: the fundamental (the note you hear) plus a series of higher harmonics (overtones). The mix of harmonics — their relative strengths — is the timbre, the "colour" of the sound. It's what makes a violin sound like a violin.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A guitar string (length L, tension T, mass per length μ): fundamental frequency f = (1/2L)√(T/μ). The string also vibrates at 2f, 3f, 4f, ... — the 2nd, 3rd, 4th harmonics. Pluck it near the bridge: more high harmonics, brighter sound (like a banjo). Pluck it near the middle: mostly fundamental, softer sound. A clarinet (closed at one end): only the odd harmonics (f, 3f, 5f, ...) are produced — giving it a hollow, woody tone. An organ pipe (open at both ends): all harmonics. Change the length (fingers on a guitar fret, valves on a trumpet, sliding a trombone) and you change the fundamental frequency — and all the harmonics scale with it.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Fundamental frequency f₀:</strong> the lowest frequency produced by a vibrating system — determines the perceived pitch. <strong>Harmonics:</strong> integer multiples of the fundamental (2f₀, 3f₀, 4f₀, ...). <strong>Timbre:</strong> the characteristic sound quality determined by the relative amplitudes of harmonics. <strong>Standing wave patterns:</strong> string fixed at both ends: λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/2L. Pipe open at both ends: same formula. Pipe open at one end: λ_n = 4L/n (n odd), f_n = nv/4L. <strong>Octave:</strong> doubling of frequency (f and 2f). A440 (A above middle C) = 440 Hz. One octave up: 880 Hz. One down: 220 Hz.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a guitar string vibrating in its fundamental (one antinode), second harmonic (two antinodes), third harmonic (three antinodes). A frequency spectrum: peaks at f, 2f, 3f — different heights for guitar vs flute. A piano keyboard with octaves labelled. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-910 — Measuring the speed of sound: a classic experiment

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** speed-of-sound-measurement; echo-timing; practical-physics | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-904, BB-NEW-905]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You can measure the speed of sound with a stopwatch and a cliff. Stand a known distance from a wall, clap loudly, and time how long the echo takes. Speed = 2 × distance / time. It's direct, it's simple, and it works. A more precise version uses two microphones a known distance apart, or a tuning fork over a tube of water to find the resonance positions. Galileo tried this with cannons and lanterns; modern students do it with a phone and a pair of headphones.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Stand 170 m from a large wall. Clap. Echo returns after 1.0 second. Total distance travelled = 340 m. Speed = 340 m / 1.0 s = 340 m/s. Closer to 343 m/s at 20°C, but this is close. <strong>Resonance tube method:</strong> a tuning fork (known frequency, e.g., 512 Hz) is held over a tube of water. Lower the water level until the tube resonates (loud sound). The air column length L = λ/4. Measure L (e.g., 16.8 cm = 0.168 m), then λ = 4L = 0.672 m. v = fλ = 512 × 0.672 = 344 m/s. Next resonance at L = 3λ/4 = 0.504 m. The difference gives λ/2 = 0.336 m, λ = 0.672 m — independent of the end correction.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Two main methods:</strong> (1) Echo timing: v = 2d/t — needs a large distance for accuracy. (2) Resonance tube: v = fλ, λ found from standing wave spacings. For an open-closed tube, resonance at L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, ... The spacing between successive resonances = λ/2. <strong>Source of error:</strong> end correction (the antinode is slightly outside the tube — ~0.6× tube radius). Using the difference between successive resonances eliminates this error. <strong>Temperature dependence:</strong> v = 331 + 0.6T — so a 10°C change shifts the speed by 6 m/s, about 1.7%.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of a person clapping in front of a wall. Distance d, echo time t, formula v = 2d/t. A resonance tube: tuning fork over water column, L1 = λ/4, L2 = 3λ/4. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-911 — The electromagnetic spectrum: from radio to gamma

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** electromagnetic-spectrum; radio-microwave-infrared-visible-UV-Xray-gamma | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-903]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Light is just one small slice of a vast family. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all the same thing — electromagnetic waves — differing only in frequency and wavelength. They all travel at the speed of light (300,000,000 m/s in a vacuum). The only difference between a radio wave and a gamma ray is how fast the electric and magnetic fields oscillate. Radio: low frequency, long wavelength. Gamma: high frequency, short wavelength.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p><strong>Radio waves:</strong> f = 3 kHz–300 MHz, λ = kilometres to metres. AM radio, FM radio, TV broadcast. <strong>Microwaves:</strong> f = 300 MHz–300 GHz, λ = 1 m to 1 mm. Wi-Fi (2.4 GHz, λ = 12.5 cm), microwave oven (2.45 GHz), radar. <strong>Infrared:</strong> f = 300 GHz–430 THz, λ = 1 mm to 0.7 μm. Heat lamps, TV remotes, thermal imaging. <strong>Visible light:</strong> λ = 0.7 μm (red) to 0.4 μm (violet). The only part we can see. <strong>Ultraviolet:</strong> λ = 400 nm to 10 nm. Sunburn, black lights, sterilisation. <strong>X-rays:</strong> λ = 10 nm to 0.01 nm. Medical imaging, airport scanners. <strong>Gamma rays:</strong> λ < 0.01 nm. Nuclear decay, cancer radiotherapy, the most energetic photons in the universe.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Electromagnetic wave:</strong> a self-propagating oscillation of electric and magnetic fields. Travels at c = 299,792,458 m/s in vacuum. No medium required. <strong>Photon energy:</strong> E = hf, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck's constant). Higher frequency = higher photon energy. Radio photon: ~10⁻²⁷ J. Gamma photon: ~10⁻¹³ J — a trillion times more energetic. The boundaries between bands are conventional, not physical — the spectrum is continuous.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of the EM spectrum as a long horizontal bar from long wavelength (radio) to short (gamma). Each band labelled with f and λ ranges. A zoom-in on the visible slice: tiny sliver between IR and UV. E = hf equation. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-912 — Doppler effect: why sirens change pitch

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** doppler-effect; frequency-shift; relative-motion; redshift-blueshift | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-903, BB-NEW-904]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>An ambulance siren sounds higher as it approaches you and lower as it moves away. This is the Doppler effect. When the source moves toward you, the wave crests are compressed — the wavelength gets shorter, so the frequency gets higher. When it moves away, the crests are stretched — longer wavelength, lower frequency. The effect works for all waves: sound, light, radio, water.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Police speed radar: a 10.5 GHz microwave beam bounces off a car. If the car is moving toward the radar at 100 km/h (27.8 m/s), the reflected frequency is shifted by about 1900 Hz. The radar measures this shift and calculates speed: v = (Δf × c) / (2 × f_source). Weather radar uses the same principle to track the speed of raindrops. <strong>Redshift:</strong> light from distant galaxies is stretched (shifted redder) because the galaxies are moving away from us — this is how we know the universe is expanding. A galaxy moving away at 30,000 km/s (10% of light speed) shifts its hydrogen spectral lines from 656.3 nm (red) to about 722 nm (near-infrared) — a clear signature of cosmic expansion.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Doppler effect:</strong> observed frequency changes when source and observer move relative to each other. <strong>Sound:</strong> f' = f × (v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s), where v is the speed of sound, v_o is the observer's velocity, v_s is the source's velocity. Signs depend on direction. <strong>Light:</strong> relativistic Doppler — Δλ/λ ≈ v/c for v ≪ c. Moving toward: blueshift (shorter λ). Moving away: redshift (longer λ). Radar speed guns, medical ultrasound (blood flow measurement), ultrasound foetal heart monitors, astronomical redshift surveys — all rely on the Doppler effect.</p>

**Image prompt:** A simple chalk drawing of an ambulance with siren waves. Approaching: compressed waves (higher f). Moving away: stretched waves (lower f). A radar gun: waves hitting a car and reflecting with frequency shift. A galaxy with redshift arrow. Chalk on dark green board. Square 1:1.


## BB-NEW-913 — Water waves: ripples, tsunamis, and surf

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** water-waves; deep-vs-shallow; wave-speed; tsunami | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-902, BB-NEW-903]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Water waves are the most visible waves, but they're also more complex than sound or light. In deep water, water particles move in circles — and the wave speed depends on wavelength (longer waves travel faster). In shallow water, the particles move in flattened ellipses, and the speed depends only on depth. A tsunami in the deep ocean travels at jet-plane speeds (~800 km/h) but with a wavelength of hundreds of kilometres — the amplitude is small, so ships don't notice it. When it reaches shallow water, the wave slows, piles up, and rises into a wall of water.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Deep-water waves: ocean swell with λ = 100 m. Speed v = √(gλ/2π) = √(9.8 × 100 / 6.28) = 12.5 m/s (45 km/h). A surfer's wave travelling at 45 km/h — you need a jet ski to tow onto waves that fast. <strong>Tsunami:</strong> λ ≈ 200 km. In 4000 m deep water, v = √(g × 4000) = 198 m/s = 713 km/h. At the shore, depth drops to 10 m — v drops to √(9.8 × 10) = 9.9 m/s. The energy piles into a smaller volume — the wave height grows from <1 m in the open ocean to 10–30 m at the coast. A ripple in a puddle: λ ≈ 1 cm, v = √(gλ/2π) = 0.12 m/s — you can out-walk it.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Deep-water waves:</strong> depth > λ/2. Speed v = √(gλ/2π) — longer waves travel faster (dispersive). <strong>Shallow-water waves:</strong> depth < λ/20. Speed v = √(gh) — depends only on depth (non-dispersive). <strong>Intermediate depth:</strong> a more complex formula involving tanh(kh). <strong>Tsunami:</strong> a shallow-water wave even in the deep ocean (λ ≈ 200 km, depth ≈ 4 km, so depth ≪ λ). Generated by undersea earthquakes, landslides, or volcanic eruptions. <strong>Capillary waves (ripples):</strong> very short λ — surface tension dominates over gravity.</p>

## BB-NEW-914 — The wave equation and wave speed

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** wave-speed-on-string; v=√(T/μ); wave-equation; tension-and-density | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-903, BB-NEW-909]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>The speed of a wave on a string depends on two things: how tight the string is (tension T) and how heavy it is (linear density μ — mass per metre). The formula is v = √(T/μ). A tighter string → faster waves. A heavier string → slower waves. This is why guitar strings are tuned by adjusting tension (tuning pegs) and why bass strings are thicker/heavier than treble strings.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>A guitar low E string: T ≈ 80 N, μ ≈ 0.005 kg/m. v = √(80/0.005) = √16000 = 126.5 m/s. Length = 0.65 m. Fundamental frequency: f = v/2L = 126.5/1.3 = 97.3 Hz (close to E2 = 82.4 Hz — the real tension is a bit lower). The high E string: μ ≈ 0.0004 kg/m, T ≈ 70 N. v = √(70/0.0004) = √175000 = 418 m/s. f = 418/1.3 = 322 Hz (close to E4 = 329.6 Hz). The speed on the thin string is more than 3× the speed on the thick string — that's how you get the two-octave range across six strings. A skipping rope: if T ≈ 50 N, μ ≈ 0.02 kg/m, v = √(50/0.02) = 50 m/s — you can easily make standing waves.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Wave speed on a string:</strong> v = √(T/μ), where T = tension (N) and μ = mass per unit length (kg/m). Derived from the wave equation: ∂²y/∂t² = (T/μ) ∂²y/∂x². The general solution is y(x,t) = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt) — any shape travelling at speed v. For a given string, v is constant — frequency is set by length: f_n = nv/2L = (n/2L)√(T/μ). This is how all string instruments work: adjust T (tuning), L (fingering), or μ (thicker strings for lower notes).</p>

## BB-NEW-915 — The waves toolkit

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** waves | **Concept:** waves-summary; oscillation-wave-frequency-wavelength-interference-resonance-spectrum | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-901 through BB-NEW-914]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Waves are disturbances that carry energy without carrying matter. They can be transverse (vibration ⟂ to travel) or longitudinal (vibration ∥ to travel). The key numbers are frequency (f, in Hz), wavelength (λ, in m), and speed (v = fλ). Waves reflect, refract, diffract, and interfere. Resonance amplifies vibrations at the right frequency. Sound, light, water, and earthquakes are all wave phenomena — the same basic rules apply to all of them.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Key numbers to carry:</p>
<p>• Speed of sound in air: 343 m/s (at 20°C). In water: 1500 m/s. In steel: 5000 m/s.</p>
<p>• Speed of light: 3 × 10⁸ m/s (all EM waves).</p>
<p>• Middle C: 261.6 Hz, λ ≈ 1.31 m (sound).</p>
<p>• Green light: f ≈ 5.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz, λ ≈ 550 nm.</p>
<p>• Human hearing: 20 Hz–20 kHz.</p>
<p>• Visible light: λ = 400–700 nm.</p>
<p>• Pendulum (1 m): T ≈ 2 s, f ≈ 0.5 Hz.</p>
<p>• Guitar string speed: ~100–400 m/s.</p>
<p>• Tsunami in deep water: ~200 m/s (700 km/h).</p>
<p>• Doppler shift: Δf/f ≈ v/c.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Waves toolkit:</strong></p>
<p>• <strong>Oscillation:</strong> repetitive motion. T = 1/f. SHM: F = −kx.</p>
<p>• <strong>Wave:</strong> propagating disturbance. v = fλ.</p>
<p>• <strong>Sound:</strong> longitudinal mechanical wave. v ≈ 331 + 0.6T.</p>
<p>• <strong>EM spectrum:</strong> radio→microwave→IR→visible→UV→X-ray→gamma.</p>
<p>• <strong>Reflection:</strong> θ_i = θ_r. <strong>Refraction:</strong> Snell's Law.</p>
<p>• <strong>Diffraction:</strong> waves bend around obstacles. Significant when gap ≈ λ.</p>
<p>• <strong>Interference:</strong> constructive (in phase) / destructive (out of phase).</p>
<p>• <strong>Resonance:</strong> driving at natural frequency → large amplitude.</p>
<p>• <strong>Standing waves:</strong> nodes and antinodes. λ_n = 2L/n (both ends fixed).</p>
<p>• <strong>String speed:</strong> v = √(T/μ). <strong>Water (shallow):</strong> v = √(gh).</p>
<p>• <strong>Doppler:</strong> frequency shift due to relative motion.</p>
