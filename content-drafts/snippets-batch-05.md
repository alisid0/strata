# Qubix Snippets — Batch 05: Tesla vs Edison (9 Snippets)

The War of the Currents was not just a battle between two men or two technologies — it was a collision of two philosophies of invention, two theories of business, and two irreconcilable personalities. These 9 Snippets trace the arc from Tesla's arrival in America through the electrocutions, the World's Fair, Niagara Falls, and the long shadow both men cast over the 20th century.

Every Snippet is tagged `kind:"snippet"` with `buildsOn` pointing at relevant BBs in the electricity and magnetism sequence (BB-NEW-200–216, BB-NEW-567–574).

---

## SNIPPET-TESLA-01 — The immigrant who arrived with four cents and a letter

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-201 (Electric Current)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

Nikola Tesla arrived in New York City on 6 June 1884, aged 27. He had four cents in his pocket, a book of his own poetry, calculations for a flying machine, and a letter of introduction to Thomas Edison. The letter, from Charles Batchelor — Edison's former manager in Paris — read: "I know two great men. One is yourself. The other is this young man." Edison hired him immediately. Tesla worked for Edison for six months, redesigning the company's direct-current dynamos. According to Tesla's later account, Edison promised him $50,000 for the work — roughly $1.5 million today — and when Tesla completed it and asked for payment, Edison laughed and said: "Tesla, you don't understand our American humour." Tesla quit on the spot. The two men never worked together again, and the line between them hardened into a war that would determine how electricity would power the 20th century.

**Sources:** Seifer, *Wizard: The Life and Times of Nikola Tesla* (1996); Carlson, *Tesla: Inventor of the Electrical Age* (2013); Tesla's unpublished autobiography, *My Inventions* (1919).


## SNIPPET-TESLA-02 — Two philosophies of electric current

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-201 (Current), BB-NEW-202 (Voltage)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

Edison's direct current was simple and intuitive: electrons flow in one direction, like water through a pipe. The problem was distance. DC cannot be easily stepped up or down in voltage — to send power more than about two kilometres, the wires had to be impractically thick or the voltage fatally high at the consumer end. Edison's solution was hyper-local generation: a power station every few blocks. Tesla's alternating current, by contrast, reversed direction 120 times per second (60 Hz). AC could be stepped up to hundreds of thousands of volts for transmission — thin wires, low losses, long distances — and stepped down to safe levels at the destination using a transformer. The physics was unambiguous: AC was the superior system for a continental power grid. Edison knew this. He fought it anyway — not on technical grounds but on fear, publicity, and his reputation as America's greatest inventor.

**Sources:** Jonnes, *Empires of Light* (2003); Hughes, *Networks of Power* (1983); Carlson, *Tesla: Inventor of the Electrical Age* (2013).


## SNIPPET-TESLA-03 — The electric chair and the war of fear

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-204 (Ohm's Law)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

Edison's campaign against alternating current was one of the most ruthless marketing operations in industrial history. He staged public demonstrations in which stray dogs, cats, calves, and eventually a horse were electrocuted with AC — to prove it was deadly, as if high-voltage DC were any safer. In 1890, he secretly financed the construction of the first electric chair at Auburn Prison in New York, ensuring it used a Westinghouse AC generator — not his own DC. The execution of William Kemmler on 6 August 1890 was a horror: the first jolt failed to kill him; the second took eight agonising minutes and left the room reeking of burned flesh. Edison's associate Harold Brown coined a verb for it: "to Westinghouse" someone. The campaign worked — in the short term. It terrified the public and stalled AC adoption. But the physics couldn't be defeated by a propaganda war, and by the mid-1890s, AC had won on the numbers.

**Sources:** Essig, *Edison and the Electric Chair* (2003); Jonnes, *Empires of Light* (2003); Moran, *Executioner's Current* (2002).


## SNIPPET-TESLA-04 — The World's Fair that turned the tide

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-573 (AC Generator)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

The 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago was the moment alternating current became unavoidable. Westinghouse, using Tesla's polyphase AC system, underbid Edison's General Electric by nearly half to electrify the entire fairground — over 600 acres. On 1 May 1893, President Grover Cleveland pressed a golden telegraph key, and 100,000 incandescent lamps ignited simultaneously across the White City, powered by Tesla's generators 40 kilometres away. The spectacle was overwhelming: for the first time, the American public saw electric light on a scale that DC could never achieve. Inside the Electricity Building, Tesla himself stood on a stage and passed 250,000 volts through his own body, lighting lamps held in his hands, his hair crackling with corona discharge — the ultimate demonstration that high-voltage AC, properly controlled, was not only safe but spectacular. The fair drew 27 million visitors. After it closed, the question was settled: the future was AC.

**Sources:** Jonnes, *Empires of Light* (2003); Larson, *The Devil in the White City* (2003); Tesla's Colorado Springs notes and 1893 lecture transcripts.


## SNIPPET-TESLA-05 — Niagara Falls: the power station that proved it

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-573 (AC Generator), BB-NEW-571 (Transformer)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

In 1895, the first large-scale AC hydroelectric power plant in the world opened at Niagara Falls, using Tesla's patented polyphase generators and motors. The plant sent power 32 kilometres to Buffalo, New York — a distance that would have been economically impossible with DC. By 1896, Buffalo's streetcars ran on Niagara power. Within a decade, the plant's capacity had expanded tenfold, and the model had been replicated worldwide. The Niagara project was the proof of concept for the entire electrical age: centralised generation, high-voltage transmission, local distribution. Every power grid on Earth today descends from that plant. Edison's DC, confined to a two-kilometre radius, became a niche technology — used in subways, in industrial processes, and, ironically, in the very long-distance transmission lines that AC's own limits eventually made uneconomical. But at Niagara, in 1895, the war was over, and Tesla had won.

**Sources:** Jonnes, *Empires of Light* (2003); Hughes, *Networks of Power* (1983); archival records of the Niagara Falls Power Company.


## SNIPPET-TESLA-06 — The forgotten man who made it work

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-571 (Mutual Inductance / Transformer)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

Between Tesla and Westinghouse stood a third figure without whom the War of the Currents would have been lost: William Stanley. In 1885, Stanley built the first practical AC transformer in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, lighting the town's main street with AC power — a full year before Westinghouse acquired Tesla's patents. Stanley solved the problem Tesla's rotating-field motor didn't address: how to efficiently change voltage levels. His transformer was the device that made AC transmission viable. Stanley, not Tesla, is the reason your wall socket delivers 230 volts instead of 230,000. He received far less fame than either Tesla or Edison, but his transformer — refined over the next decade by Westinghouse engineers — was the silent partner that decided the war. The transformer and the polyphase motor together were the one-two punch that DC could not answer.

**Sources:** Skrabec, *George Westinghouse: Gentle Genius* (2007); Hughes, *Networks of Power* (1983); IEEE Historical Milestone: "AC Electrification, Great Barrington, 1886."


## SNIPPET-TESLA-07 — Tesla's unravelling

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-567 (Faraday's Law)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

After winning the War of the Currents, Tesla should have been wealthy and celebrated for life. He was neither. In 1897, Westinghouse was near bankruptcy, strangled by the debt taken on to fight Edison. Tesla tore up his royalty contract — which would have paid him roughly $2.50 per horsepower of electricity sold, a sum that would eventually have been worth billions — to save Westinghouse's company. He received a lump sum of $216,000 instead. Without royalties, and without the business instincts to commercialise his later inventions, Tesla's finances collapsed. His Wardenclyffe Tower project — a visionary attempt to transmit power wirelessly across the Atlantic — consumed his remaining fortune and was demolished in 1917 when funding dried up. He spent his final decades alone in a series of New York hotel rooms, feeding pigeons in Bryant Park, issuing increasingly eccentric press releases about death rays and interplanetary communication. He died in 1943, aged 86, in room 3327 of the Hotel New Yorker. The FBI seized his papers within hours.

**Sources:** Seifer, *Wizard* (1996); Carlson, *Tesla: Inventor of the Electrical Age* (2013); Cheney, *Tesla: Man Out of Time* (1981).


## SNIPPET-TESLA-08 — Edison's empire and its cost

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-201 (Current), BB-NEW-208 (Power)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

Edison lost the War of the Currents, but he won the business war. General Electric, formed in 1892 from the merger of Edison's company with a rival, became one of the largest corporations on Earth — and by the 1900s, it was manufacturing AC equipment under Westinghouse's and Tesla's patents. Edison himself had been sidelined from the company he founded; the board forced his name out of the corporate title in 1894, and by the time he died in 1931, he was far more famous as the inventor of the phonograph and the motion picture camera than as a power-grid visionary. His real genius was not invention but industrialisation: he built the first research laboratory, the first patent portfolio as a competitive weapon, the first celebrity-inventor brand. The mythology that grew around him — the lone genius, the Wizard of Menlo Park, the lightbulb as his singular invention (he improved it; others, including Joseph Swan, invented it independently) — was itself his greatest creation.

**Sources:** Stross, *The Wizard of Menlo Park* (2007); Israel, *Edison: A Life of Invention* (1998); Freeberg, *The Age of Edison* (2013).


## SNIPPET-TESLA-09 — The myth and the man

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-204 (Ohm's Law), BB-NEW-573 (AC Generator)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

In the popular imagination, Tesla has become a secular saint — the misunderstood genius crushed by corporate greed, the man who invented the 20th century and died penniless while Edison became a household name. The reality is more complicated and more interesting. Tesla was a genius, but he was also a showman, a terrible businessman, and in his later years, a man whose claims often outpaced his physics. Edison was a ruthless capitalist, but he was also a brilliant systems thinker who understood that an invention without a market is a hobby. Neither man was a saint; neither was a villain. The War of the Currents wasn't won by personal virtue — it was won by physics. AC is better at travelling long distances; DC is better at underwater cables and point-to-point links. The modern grid uses both, in the places each is suited for. The war that consumed two lives for a decade resolved, a century later, into a draw. Tesla's name now graces an electric car company. Edison's name lights up nothing in particular. That might be the final verdict history renders, even if neither man would recognise it as justice.

**Sources:** Seifer, *Wizard* (1996); Stross, *The Wizard of Menlo Park* (2007); Hughes, *Networks of Power* (1983); contemporary analysis of global HVDC vs HVAC transmission.
