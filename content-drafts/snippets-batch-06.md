# Qubix Snippets — Batch 06: The Golden Age (1900–1955)

There has never been a generation like it. Between 1900 and 1955, a group of physicists and mathematicians — most born within 25 years of each other, many fleeing the same war, all corresponding, arguing, and building on each other's work — dismantled the classical universe and built a new one. Relativity, quantum mechanics, nuclear fission, the digital computer, and the foundations of artificial intelligence all emerged from this single cohort, in a single human lifespan. These 20 Snippets capture the people, the places, and the collision of brilliance that made it happen.

Every Snippet is tagged `kind:"snippet"`. Builds on BBs throughout the physics, chemistry, and problem-solving curricula.

## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-01 — The photograph that captured a century

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-580 (What Differentiation Is — gateway to the physics that follows)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In October 1927, twenty-nine of the world's greatest physicists assembled in Brussels for the Fifth Solvay Conference on Electrons and Photons. A photograph was taken. In the front row, seated: Max Planck (the reluctant revolutionary who started quantum theory in 1900), Marie Curie (two Nobel Prizes, in physics and chemistry), Hendrik Lorentz (the grand old man of classical physics, whose transformations Einstein had borrowed), and Albert Einstein (already the most famous scientist on Earth, already deeply uncomfortable with the quantum theory he had helped create). Behind them stood Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg (25 years old, already a Nobel laureate), Wolfgang Pauli, Erwin Schrödinger, Paul Dirac, Louis de Broglie, and the young American Arthur Compton. Seventeen of the twenty-nine attendees had already won or would win Nobel Prizes. The photograph is the single most concentrated image of human scientific genius ever captured — and it was taken at the exact moment the old physics was dying and the new one was being born.

**Sources:** Straumann, "The Solvay Conference 1927," *European Physical Journal H* (2011); Pais, *Subtle is the Lord* (1982); the Solvay photograph itself (Benjamin Couprie, 1927).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-02 — Planck's desperate act

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-526 (Thermal Radiation)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In December 1900, Max Planck solved a problem that had defeated every physicist of the 19th century: the spectrum of black-body radiation. His solution required a single, desperate assumption — that energy is not continuous but comes in discrete packets, *quanta*. Planck hated this. He called it "an act of desperation" and spent years trying to derive the same result without quantisation, hoping to reconcile it with classical physics. He never succeeded. At 42, a conservative physicist who had built his career on thermodynamics, he had inadvertently started the quantum revolution. In 1918, he received the Nobel Prize. In 1944, his son Erwin was executed by the Gestapo for involvement in the plot to assassinate Hitler. Planck's home and his lifetime of scientific papers were destroyed in Allied bombing. He died in 1947, aged 89, having lived long enough to see his "desperate act" become the foundation of all modern physics — and long enough to lose everything else.

**Sources:** Heilbron, *The Dilemmas of an Upright Man* (1986); Kuhn, *Black-Body Theory and the Quantum Discontinuity* (1978); Planck's Nobel Lecture (1920).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-03 — Einstein's miracle year

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-580 (Differentiation), BB-NEW-526 (Radiation), BB-NEW-500 (Gravitation)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In 1905, Albert Einstein was 26 years old, working as a patent clerk in Bern, Switzerland — a job he'd taken because no university would hire him. That year, he published four papers in the *Annalen der Physik*. The first explained the photoelectric effect using Planck's quanta — the paper that won him the Nobel Prize in 1921. The second explained Brownian motion, providing the first conclusive evidence that atoms existed. The third introduced special relativity: E = mc². The fourth, a short follow-up, derived the mass-energy equivalence explicitly. Any one of these papers would have made a career. Four in one year, by an unknown 26-year-old working outside academia, is unmatched in the history of science. Einstein himself said of that year: "A storm broke loose in my mind." The storm never fully subsided — but neither did his discomfort with the quantum theory that flowed from his own work.

**Sources:** Pais, *Subtle is the Lord* (1982); Isaacson, *Einstein: His Life and Universe* (2007); Stachel, *Einstein's Miraculous Year* (1998).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-04 — Bohr's atom: the impossible model that worked

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-200 (Electric Charge), BB-NEW-552 (Circular Motion in B)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In 1913, Niels Bohr proposed a model of the hydrogen atom that violated every known law of physics. Electrons orbited the nucleus in fixed circular paths, like planets around a sun — but unlike planets, they could only occupy certain orbits, and they jumped between them instantaneously, emitting or absorbing a photon of exactly the right energy. Classical electrodynamics said an orbiting electron should radiate continuously and spiral into the nucleus in a fraction of a second. Bohr simply forbade it. The model was logically indefensible — Bohr's own mentor, Ernest Rutherford, called it "a most ingenious mixture of classical and quantum ideas." But it worked: it predicted the spectral lines of hydrogen with extraordinary accuracy. Bohr had traded consistency for results, and physics followed him. Einstein called it "the highest form of musicality in the sphere of thought." The Bohr model was wrong, but it pointed the way to the quantum mechanics of Heisenberg and Schrödinger — and Bohr himself became the philosophical centre of the revolution.

**Sources:** Pais, *Niels Bohr's Times* (1991); Heilbron & Kuhn, "The Genesis of the Bohr Atom," *Historical Studies in the Physical Sciences* (1969); Bohr, "On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules," *Philosophical Magazine* (1913).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-05 — Heisenberg on Heligoland: the matrix that rewrote physics

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-582 (Differentiation Power Rule)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In June 1925, Werner Heisenberg — 23 years old, suffering from severe hay fever — fled to the pollen-free island of Heligoland in the North Sea. There, in almost total isolation, he invented matrix mechanics: the first complete formulation of quantum theory. His central insight was that physics should describe only observable quantities — the frequencies and intensities of spectral lines, not the unobservable electron orbits. He replaced classical position and momentum with mathematical tables — matrices — whose multiplication was non-commutative: p × q ≠ q × p. "I had the feeling that, through the surface of atomic phenomena, I was looking at a strangely beautiful interior," he wrote. He returned to Göttingen, and within months, Max Born and Pascual Jordan had formalised his insight into the full mathematical apparatus of quantum mechanics. Independently, and almost simultaneously, Erwin Schrödinger developed wave mechanics — a completely different mathematical formalism that, as Schrödinger himself proved, was mathematically equivalent to Heisenberg's. Two roads, one destination.

**Sources:** Cassidy, *Uncertainty: The Life and Science of Werner Heisenberg* (1992); Heisenberg, *Physics and Beyond* (1971); van der Waerden, *Sources of Quantum Mechanics* (1967).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-06 — Schrödinger's cat and the equation that terrified its creator

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-580 (Differentiation), BB-NEW-586 (Trig Derivatives)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In 1926, Erwin Schrödinger published his wave equation — iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ — a partial differential equation that described matter as a wave spread through space. It was immediately recognised as a masterpiece. Einstein called it "the work of a true genius." But Schrödinger himself soon recoiled from what his own equation implied. The wavefunction ψ contained all the information about a particle, but its physical meaning was unclear. Max Born proposed the interpretation that shocked Schrödinger: ψ is a probability amplitude. The wave is not the particle; the wave is the probability of finding the particle. Schrödinger was horrified. He devised his famous thought experiment — a cat in a box, simultaneously alive and dead until observed — to expose what he saw as the absurdity of the Copenhagen interpretation. The cat was meant as a *reductio ad absurdum*. Instead, it became the most famous illustration of quantum weirdness. Schrödinger spent the rest of his career as a critic of the theory he had created.

**Sources:** Moore, *Schrödinger: Life and Thought* (1989); Schrödinger, "Die gegenwärtige Situation in der Quantenmechanik" (1935); Gribbin, *In Search of Schrödinger's Cat* (1984).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-07 — Dirac's silence

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-586 (Trig Derivatives), BB-NEW-600 (Integration)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

Paul Dirac was the strangest genius of the golden age. He spoke so rarely that his Cambridge colleagues invented a unit of conversation — the "dirac," one word per hour. When he published *The Principles of Quantum Mechanics* in 1930, it was so elegant that Einstein declared it "the most logically perfect presentation of quantum mechanics." In 1928, Dirac combined quantum mechanics with special relativity and derived an equation that predicted the existence of antimatter — the positron, discovered by Carl Anderson in 1932, confirming Dirac's prediction. It was, and remains, one of the greatest theoretical predictions in the history of physics. Dirac did not celebrate. When asked about his feelings on receiving the Nobel Prize in 1933, he considered the question and replied: "Well, I suppose it's better than not getting it." He was, by all accounts, incapable of small talk. When a student raised his hand after a lecture and said, "Professor Dirac, I don't understand the equation on the blackboard," Dirac stood silently for a full minute before the chairman prompted him: "Professor Dirac, would you care to answer the question?" Dirac replied: "That was not a question. It was a statement."

**Sources:** Farmelo, *The Strangest Man: The Hidden Life of Paul Dirac* (2009); Kragh, *Dirac: A Scientific Biography* (1990); Dirac, *The Principles of Quantum Mechanics* (1930).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-08 — The Solvay debates: Einstein vs Bohr

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-580 (Differentiation), BB-NEW-600 (Integration)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

At the 1927 Solvay Conference, Einstein and Bohr began a debate that would continue for the rest of their lives. Einstein would arrive at breakfast with a new thought experiment designed to show that quantum mechanics was incomplete — a hidden-variable loophole that Heisenberg's uncertainty principle could not close. Bohr would spend the day analysing it, and by dinner he would have found the flaw. This happened every day for a week. Einstein's most famous challenge — the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen paradox of 1935, arguing that quantum mechanics required "spooky action at a distance" — was answered by Bohr within months. The debates were philosophical at their core: Einstein believed the universe was deterministic, independent of observation; Bohr believed the universe was probabilistic, and the observer was inextricably part of the description. "God does not play dice," Einstein said. Bohr replied: "Einstein, stop telling God what to do." Neither man ever convinced the other. The debate remains unresolved in some interpretations of quantum mechanics to this day.

**Sources:** Pais, *Niels Bohr's Times* (1991); Bohr, "Discussion with Einstein on Epistemological Problems in Atomic Physics" (1949); Kumar, *Quantum: Einstein, Bohr, and the Great Debate* (2009).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-09 — The physicists' exodus

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-500 (Gravitation), BB-NEW-200 (Electricity)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

Between 1933 and 1941, the intellectual centre of physics moved from Europe to America. The Nazis dismissed Jewish scientists from German universities; Einstein left in 1933 and never returned. Born, Schrödinger, Pauli, Fermi, Szilard, Teller, Wigner, and Bethe all fled. Bohr escaped Denmark in 1943, ferried to Sweden in a fishing boat, then flown to Britain in the bomb bay of a Mosquito aircraft — he passed out from oxygen deprivation during the flight and nearly died. The refugees brought quantum mechanics, nuclear physics, and the theoretical tools that would build the atomic bomb to America. The Manhattan Project was staffed almost entirely by European-born physicists, many of whom had been forced out of their home countries by the regime they were now helping to defeat. The irony was not lost on them: the Nazis had driven away the very people who would ensure their defeat.

**Sources:** Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986); Medawar & Pyke, *Hitler's Gift: The True Story of the Scientists Expelled by the Nazi Regime* (2001); Moore, *Schrödinger: Life and Thought* (1989).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-10 — The letter that started the Manhattan Project

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-500 (Gravitation), BB-NEW-274 (Carnot / Energy)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

On 2 August 1939, Albert Einstein signed a letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt warning that Nazi Germany might be developing "extremely powerful bombs of a new type" based on nuclear fission. The letter was actually drafted by Leo Szilard and Eugene Wigner — Einstein's role was to lend his name, the most famous in science, to ensure the letter would be read. It worked. Roosevelt authorised the Advisory Committee on Uranium, which evolved into the Manhattan Project — a $2 billion effort employing 130,000 people across secret sites at Los Alamos, Oak Ridge, and Hanford. The bomb was tested at Trinity on 16 July 1945. Einstein, who was a pacifist and had played no further role in the project, learned of the Hiroshima bombing from a radio broadcast. He spent the rest of his life advocating for nuclear disarmament, calling the letter "the one great mistake of my life." The man whose equation made the bomb possible had only signed a letter — and spent his remaining decade trying to undo what that signature had set in motion.

**Sources:** Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986); Isaacson, *Einstein* (2007); Lanouette, *Genius in the Shadows: A Biography of Leo Szilard* (1992).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-11 — Oppenheimer: "I am become Death"

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-200 (Electric Charge), BB-NEW-500 (Gravitation)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

J. Robert Oppenheimer was an unlikely choice to run a military weapons laboratory. He was a theoretical physicist with no administrative experience, a left-wing intellectual with friends in the Communist Party, and a man who had once admitted to trying to strangle a colleague during a breakdown at Cambridge. But General Leslie Groves, the military head of the Manhattan Project, saw something in him: "He's a genius. A real genius." Oppenheimer built Los Alamos from scratch on a New Mexico mesa, assembling the greatest concentration of scientific talent in history — Fermi, Bethe, Feynman, von Neumann, Teller, Bohr, and dozens of others. On 16 July 1945, at 5:29 AM, the Trinity test detonated the world's first atomic bomb. As the mushroom cloud rose, Oppenheimer recalled a line from the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." He later told President Truman, "I feel I have blood on my hands." Truman replied, "The blood is on my hands. Let me worry about that." Oppenheimer's security clearance was revoked in 1954 in a humiliating hearing, driven by his opposition to the hydrogen bomb. The man who built the bomb was exiled from the government it had made supreme.

**Sources:** Bird & Sherwin, *American Prometheus* (2005); Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986); Monk, *Inside the Centre: The Life of J. Robert Oppenheimer* (2012).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-12 — Lise Meitner: the woman who explained fission and was erased

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-500 (Newton's Law), BB-NEW-260 (Temperature/Energy)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In December 1938, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in Berlin bombarded uranium with neutrons and detected barium in the products — the atom had split. Hahn, a chemist, could not explain how. He wrote to his former collaborator Lise Meitner, an Austrian-born Jewish physicist who had fled to Sweden months earlier to escape the Nazis. Meitner, working with her nephew Otto Frisch, calculated that the uranium nucleus was behaving like a liquid drop that had been stretched until it snapped — releasing energy according to Einstein's E = mc². She was the first to understand what had happened: nuclear fission. Hahn published the results in 1939 without Meitner's name on the paper — partly to avoid Nazi scrutiny, partly because he was already downplaying her role. He received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery. Meitner was not even mentioned in the citation. Einstein called her "the German Marie Curie." The element meitnerium, number 109, now carries her name — but the Nobel committee never corrected its omission.

**Sources:** Sime, *Lise Meitner: A Life in Physics* (1996); Rife, *Lise Meitner and the Dawn of the Nuclear Age* (1999); Frisch, *What Little I Remember* (1979).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-13 — Fermi's pile: the first nuclear reactor

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-274 (Carnot), BB-NEW-526 (Radiation)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

On 2 December 1942, beneath the bleachers of a disused squash court at the University of Chicago, Enrico Fermi and his team built the world's first nuclear reactor — Chicago Pile-1. It was a crude stack of graphite bricks and uranium pellets, held together by nothing but gravity, shaped roughly like a doorknob, and monitored by a team of physicists holding neutron counters. At 3:25 PM, Fermi ordered the control rods withdrawn. The neutron count rose, stabilised, and held — the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in human history. Fermi let it run for 28 minutes, then ordered it shut down. The team opened a bottle of Chianti and drank from paper cups. Arthur Compton sent a coded telegram to James Conant at the Pentagon: "The Italian navigator has landed in the New World." Conant replied: "How were the natives?" Compton: "Very friendly." Less than three years later, the bombs built on this principle destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Fermi died of stomach cancer in 1954, aged 53. The squash court was demolished in 1957.

**Sources:** Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986); Segrè, *Enrico Fermi, Physicist* (1970); Fermi's collected papers.

## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-14 — Turing: the man who invented the future in a shed

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-582 (Differentiation / Mathematics)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

In 1936, Alan Turing — 24 years old, a fellow at King's College, Cambridge — published a paper titled "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem." In it, he imagined a machine: an infinite tape divided into squares, a read-write head that could move left or right, and a finite set of internal states determining its behaviour based on the symbol it read. This "Turing machine" was not a physical device; it was a thought experiment. But it proved that a single machine, given the right instructions, could compute anything that is computable. The paper founded theoretical computer science. During the war, Turing worked at Bletchley Park, where he designed the electromechanical Bombe that cracked the German Enigma code — work that likely shortened the war by two years and saved millions of lives. After the war, he designed the ACE, one of the first stored-program computers, and wrote the first programming manual. In 1952, he was prosecuted for homosexuality and forced to undergo chemical castration. He died in 1954, aged 41, from cyanide poisoning — a half-eaten apple by his bedside. The British government issued an official apology in 2009. He was posthumously pardoned in 2013.

**Sources:** Hodges, *Alan Turing: The Enigma* (1983); Turing, "On Computable Numbers" (1936); Copeland, *The Essential Turing* (2004).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-15 — Von Neumann: the smartest man in the room, every room

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-582 (Differentiation), BB-NEW-600 (Integration)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

John von Neumann was born in Budapest in 1903, the son of a Jewish banker ennobled by the Austro-Hungarian Empire. By age six, he could divide eight-digit numbers in his head. By eight, he had read all 44 volumes of his father's world history library. At the same 1927 Solvay Conference immortalised in the photograph, he was 23 years old — the youngest person there. Over the next three decades, he made foundational contributions to quantum mechanics (the mathematical formulation that proved Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave mechanics were equivalent), game theory (the minimax theorem), nuclear weapons design (the implosion mechanism of the plutonium bomb), and computing (the stored-program architecture that bears his name — every computer you have ever used is a von Neumann machine). He worked on the Manhattan Project during the day and invented game theory at night. Colleagues described his mind as a searchlight: he could illuminate any problem, in any field, faster than the experts who had spent their careers on it. He died of cancer in 1957, aged 53, having shaped more fields of human knowledge than any other 20th-century scientist.

**Sources:** Macrae, *John von Neumann* (1992); Halmos, "The Legend of John von Neumann," *American Mathematical Monthly* (1973); Aspray, *John von Neumann and the Origins of Modern Computing* (1990).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-16 — The women of the golden age

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-200 (Electricity), BB-NEW-526 (Radiation)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

The golden age was dominated in the popular imagination by men, but women drove key breakthroughs. Marie Curie, the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences (physics 1903, chemistry 1911), personally carried tubes of radium in her pockets during WWI to power mobile X-ray units at the front — the radiation poisoning eventually killed her. Lise Meitner explained nuclear fission but was erased from the Nobel. Emmy Noether, described by Einstein as "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced since the higher education of women began," proved the theorem linking symmetry to conservation laws — Noether's theorem — which underpins all of modern physics. Maria Goeppert-Mayer developed the nuclear shell model and won the Nobel Prize in 1963 — only the second woman to do so in physics, after Curie. Chien-Shiung Wu designed the experiment that proved parity violation in 1956 — her male colleagues won the Nobel; she did not. These women worked in institutions that often refused them paid positions, titles, and recognition. Their discoveries survived the slights; their names, too often, did not.

**Sources:** Sime, *Lise Meitner: A Life in Physics* (1996); Byers & Williams, *Out of the Shadows: Contributions of Twentieth-Century Women to Physics* (2006); Dick, *Emmy Noether: A Tribute to Her Life and Work* (1981).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-17 — Bletchley Park: the cottage industry that cracked the unbreakable

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-114 (Number Theory / Cryptography foundations)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

In 1939, the German military encoded its communications using the Enigma machine — a device with rotors, plugboards, and 159 quintillion possible settings, widely believed to be unbreakable. Britain's response was Bletchley Park, a Victorian mansion in Buckinghamshire converted into a codebreaking factory. At its peak, 9,000 people worked there in shifts, 75% of them women. Turing designed the Bombe, an electromechanical machine that tested millions of rotor settings per hour. But the real mathematical breakthrough came from the Polish Cipher Bureau, which had been reading Enigma since 1932 and shared its methods with Britain and France in July 1939 — five weeks before the invasion of Poland. Bletchley also broke the Lorenz cipher, used for Hitler's personal communications, using Colossus — the world's first programmable electronic digital computer, designed by Tommy Flowers, a Post Office engineer. The entire operation was kept secret until 1974. Turing never told his family what he had done.

**Sources:** Singh, *The Code Book* (1999); Hodges, *Alan Turing: The Enigma* (1983); Gannon, *Colossus: Bletchley Park's Greatest Secret* (2006).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-18 — The Trinity Test: the moment the world changed

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-500 (Gravitation), BB-NEW-527 (Stefan-Boltzmann)

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

At 5:29:45 AM on 16 July 1945, in the Jornada del Muerto desert of New Mexico — the "Journey of the Dead Man" — the first atomic bomb detonated. The fireball reached 300 metres in diameter and a temperature of several million degrees — hotter than the surface of the Sun. The light was visible 290 kilometres away. A blind girl in Albuquerque saw it. The shock wave shattered windows 160 kilometres distant. The mushroom cloud rose to 12 kilometres. The steel tower on which the bomb had been mounted was vaporised, leaving a crater of radioactive glass — trinitite — that still glitters in the desert today. Fermi, standing 16 kilometres from ground zero, dropped strips of paper and measured how far the shock wave moved them — he calculated the yield at approximately 10 kilotons of TNT. His estimate, made with paper scraps and a ruler, was accurate to within 20%. Oppenheimer later testified that a line from the Bhagavad Gita crossed his mind. Kenneth Bainbridge, the test director, turned to Oppenheimer and said simply: "Now we are all sons of bitches."

**Sources:** Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986); Szasz, *The Day the Sun Rose Twice* (1984); Bird & Sherwin, *American Prometheus* (2005).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-19 — Feynman at Los Alamos: safecracking and bongo drums

**buildsOn:** BB-NEW-580 (Differentiation), BB-NEW-600 (Integration)

**Kind:** snippet (discovery)

**Content:**

Richard Feynman arrived at Los Alamos in 1943, aged 24, a freshly minted PhD from Princeton. While the senior physicists wrestled with implosion dynamics and neutron diffusion, Feynman amused himself by cracking the safes containing the atomic secrets. He learned that the default combinations had never been changed from the factory settings, and that most people used birthdays or mathematical constants. He left notes inside the safes: "Guess who?" and "I was here." The security office was not amused. Feynman also played bongo drums in the desert, taught himself to pick locks, and performed calculations that were essential to the design of the implosion lens. After the war, he declined offers from Princeton and Harvard to join Cornell — and later Caltech — where he rebuilt quantum electrodynamics from scratch, won the Nobel Prize in 1965, and delivered the lectures that became the most famous introductory physics course ever taught. His Los Alamos stories, collected in *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*, capture the irreverent, joyful, relentlessly curious spirit of a generation that built doomsday weapons and laughed in the desert.

**Sources:** Feynman, *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985); Gleick, *Genius* (1992); Hoddeson et al., *Critical Assembly* (1993).


## SNIPPET-GOLDEN-20 — The lamp goes out

**buildsOn:** All preceding Snippets in this batch

**Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

Between 1954 and 1962, the golden generation lost its core. Turing in 1954 (cyanide, age 41). Einstein in 1955 (aortic aneurysm, age 76 — he refused surgery, saying "I want to go when I want. It is tasteless to prolong life artificially"). Von Neumann in 1957 (cancer, age 53). Fermi in 1954 (stomach cancer, age 53). Bohr in 1962 (heart failure, age 77). Schrödinger in 1961 (tuberculosis, age 73). The era that had begun with Planck's desperate quantisation in 1900 — and had produced relativity, quantum mechanics, nuclear energy, and the digital computer — was over. The next generation, the students of these giants, would carry the work forward: Feynman, Gell-Mann, Weinberg, Glashow, Salam. But the founding cohort, the ones who had dismantled the classical universe and built a new one from the rubble of two world wars, had left the stage. In 1962, Niels Bohr — the last of the Solvay '27 front row — recorded a final interview. Asked what he hoped future physicists would remember, he said: "That we were part of a community that tried to understand nature. That we talked to each other. That we corrected each other. That we never stopped asking." It was as good an epitaph as any generation could hope for.

**Sources:** Pais, *Niels Bohr's Times* (1991); Isaacson, *Einstein* (2007); Gleick, *Genius* (1992); the collected recorded interviews of the American Institute of Physics oral history project.
