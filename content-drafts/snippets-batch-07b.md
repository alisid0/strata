## SNIPPET-TAYLOR-TRINITY — The physicist who cracked a state secret with a ruler

**buildsOn:** BB 1008 (The homogeneity principle)

**Subject:** physics | **Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

In 1947, the US government released photographs of the Trinity nuclear test — the first atomic bomb detonation — with the time stamp and length scale carefully blacked out. They released them believing no useful information could be extracted. The British physicist G.I. Taylor looked at the photos and, using nothing but dimensional analysis and a ruler, estimated the bomb's yield to within about 20% of the classified value. His reasoning: the radius R of the fireball depends only on the energy E released, the time t since detonation, and the density of the surrounding air. By dimensional homogeneity, the only possible combination is R^5 = (E t^2)/rho. He measured the fireball radius from the published photos at a known frame interval, plugged in the density of air, and solved for E. When he published his estimate in 1950, the US Atomic Energy Commission was blindsided — a man with a ruler and a principle from first-year physics had reverse-engineered their most closely guarded secret. The homogeneity principle isn't just an academic filter; it can extract truth from noise when almost nothing is given.

**Sources:** Taylor, *The Formation of a Blast Wave by a Very Intense Explosion* (1950); Batchelor, *The Life and Legacy of G.I. Taylor* (1996).

## SNIPPET-GALILEO-PENDULUM — The chandelier that started modern physics

**buildsOn:** BB 1009 (Dimensional analysis)

**Subject:** physics | **Kind:** snippet (era)

**Content:**

In 1583, a 19-year-old medical student named Galileo Galilei was sitting in the Cathedral of Pisa during a dull sermon when he noticed a chandelier swinging overhead on its long chain. Using the only clock he had — his own pulse — he timed the swings and discovered something that contradicted common sense: the chandelier took the same time to complete each swing whether it was moving in a wide arc or barely moving at all. The period didn't depend on the amplitude. He raced home and repeated the experiment with pendulums of different lengths, confirming that only the length mattered. This observation — that the period of a pendulum depends on sqrt(L/g) and nothing else — is arguably the first quantitative law in physics discovered by systematic observation rather than philosophical reasoning. Galileo never built a pendulum clock himself (that fell to Christiaan Huygens in 1656), but he spent his final years, blind and under house arrest, dictating designs for one to his son. The chandelier in Pisa Cathedral still hangs there — though the one swinging that day was probably not the one you see now.

**Sources:** Drake, *Galileo at Work* (1978); Heilbron, *Galileo* (2010).
