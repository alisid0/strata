# Snippets — Measuring the universe (build on BB 87 / Card 87)

Five snippets tied to the SI-units board: scale from the proton to the quasar, and the story of how the base units were defined.

## SNIPPET-QUASAR — Quasars: a galaxy's blazing heart
**buildsOn:** Card 87
**Kind:** snippet (era)
**Content:**
A quasar is the core of a distant galaxy where a supermassive black hole (often billions of times the Sun's mass) is actively feeding. Gas spiralling inward piles into a disk and is heated to millions of degrees, and the light it pours out can outshine all the hundreds of billions of stars in its galaxy put together. That brilliance is why we can spot them from the far edge of the observable universe — the most distant are over 13 billion light-years away. Their light left before the Sun or the Earth existed, so to look at a quasar is to look at the young universe as it was. When the first ones turned up in the early 1960s they looked like faint stars, earning the name "quasi-stellar" before anyone realised each pinprick was the blazing heart of an entire galaxy.

## SNIPPET-GRAND-K — Le Grand K: the kilogram that lost weight
**buildsOn:** Card 87
**Kind:** snippet (era)
**Content:**
For 130 years the kilogram wasn't a number, it was an object: a golf-ball-sized cylinder of platinum-iridium locked in a vault outside Paris, called Le Grand K. Every kilogram on Earth was calibrated against it. The trouble came when it was compared to its official copies — it had quietly drifted by about 50 micrograms over a century, and nobody knows exactly why. A unit that defines mass cannot itself be losing mass. So in 2019 the kilogram was cut loose from the cylinder and redefined by fixing Planck's constant, a permanent feature of nature. Le Grand K still sits in its vault, but the definition no longer depends on it.

## SNIPPET-METRE — The metre: from the Earth to the speed of light
**buildsOn:** Card 87
**Kind:** snippet (era)
**Content:**
When France defined the metre in the 1790s, they tied it to the planet — one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, surveyed over six years by men dragging instruments across a country mid-revolution. Later it became a platinum bar kept in Paris. Since 1983 it has been the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The definition climbed from the size of the Earth, to a metal bar, to a constant of the universe, and each step made it more precise and more reproducible. Anyone with the right kit can now recreate a perfect metre without ever visiting Paris.

## SNIPPET-SECOND — The atomic second
**buildsOn:** Card 87
**Kind:** snippet (era)
**Content:**
A second used to be a slice of the day: 1/86,400 of one Earth rotation. But the Earth is a sloppy clock — winds, tides, and its molten core nudge its spin by milliseconds. So since 1967 the second has been 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a caesium-133 atom, which never varies. The best clocks built on this would lose less than a second over the entire age of the universe. You lean on it constantly: GPS works by timing signals to billionths of a second, and without that precision your phone's location would wander off by kilometres within minutes.

## SNIPPET-PROTON — The proton end: how empty an atom is
**buildsOn:** Card 87
**Kind:** snippet (era)
**Content:**
On the small end of the scale sits the proton, about one femtometre across: a millionth of a billionth of a metre. At that size the real surprise is how empty everything is. An atom is almost entirely space — if its nucleus were a marble on the centre spot of a football stadium, the electrons would be a faint haze up in the stands, with nothing in between. The floor you're standing on is mostly void; what stops you falling through isn't matter touching matter but the electric repulsion between electron clouds. "Solid" is a sensation, not a statement about how full the space is.
