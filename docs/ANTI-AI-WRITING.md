# ANTI-AI-WRITING.md — breaking the machine voice

Research compiled 2026-07-04 from Lockhart (*The Mending of Broken Bones*),
the humanize-writing-skill dictionary, Wikipedia's "Signs of AI Writing," and
direct analysis of AI-generated Qubix BB drafts that still read as artificial
even after vocabulary cleanup.

---

## The real problem: not words — structure

Vocabulary cleanup (removing "delve," "landscape," "pivotal," etc.) fixes the
surface. But the text still *feels* AI because of deeper structural patterns.
These patterns are harder to name and harder to break — but they are the ones
that matter.

---

## 1. The "Every X has Y" template

**What it is:** A paragraph built as a series of parallel claims, each starting
with the same grammatical structure.

**AI example:**
> Every mark has a start and an end. Every crack stops somewhere. Every crease
> has edges.

**Why it's a tell:** Humans write one example and move on. AI writes three to
five examples in identical grammatical cages because the model has been trained
to be "comprehensive." It cannot stop at one.

**Lockhart counterexample:**
> So far, we have encountered only the fields ℚ and ℝ, the number systems
> arising from counting and measuring.

One example. Then a new thought. No catalogue.

**Fix:** Pick the strongest single example. Trust it. If a second example is
genuinely needed, give it a different grammatical shape — break the cage.

---

## 2. The three-act paragraph (setup → development → landing)

**What it is:** Every paragraph follows the same invisible template:
1. Opening claim or question
2. Development / elaboration
3. Tidy concluding sentence that "lands" the thought

**AI example:**
> A Euclidian line does not start and it does not stop. It extends forever in
> both directions. And we use it anyway — we have used it for two thousand years
> to build bridges, track the planets, and land on the moon.

**Why it's a tell:** Real humans don't land every paragraph. They let thoughts
hang. They end mid-gesture. They jump to the next thing without a handoff.

**Lockhart counterexample:**
> So the natural thing to do would be to enlarge and extend ℝ to include such
> desirable entities. It turns out to be enough to throw in any one of them, say
> √−1, and that will produce the rest.

Reads like someone thinking, not someone performing.

**Fix:** End some paragraphs abruptly. End others with a question. Never end
with a sentence that could be a tweet.

---

## 3. The hidden tricolon (beyond adjectives)

**What it is:** Groups of three that aren't obvious adjective lists — three
consecutive sentences with identical rhythm, three examples in a row, three
clauses that land with the same weight.

**AI example:**
> No width. No roughness. No grain of sand. And no endpoints.

**Why it's a tell:** The rhythm is too perfect. Human writing has stumble. AI
writing marches.

**Lockhart counterexample:**
> It is certainly not my intention to go into great detail here, but it might be
> amusing to see a few examples of just how wide a generalization we have made.
> How weird can a number system get and still obey our nine demands?

Uneven. A long sentence, then a question. No march.

**Fix:** Vary sentence length deliberately. Put a 3-word sentence next to a
25-word sentence. Break rhythm whenever it starts to feel musical.

---

## 4. The "and yet" pivot

**What it is:** A paragraph that builds toward a contrast, then pivots with
"and yet," "but," or "however" to deliver a reveal.

**AI example:**
> A Euclidian line has never existed outside a human mind. And yet it works.

**Why it's a tell:** AI defaults to contrast structures because they feel
"insightful." Real humans state the tension without announcing it.

**Lockhart counterexample:**
> The trouble with things like notation and nomenclature is that once they catch
> on and become entrenched, they are very hard to dislodge — even in light of
> new understanding that makes them obsolete.

The tension is embedded in the observation. No "and yet" flag.

**Fix:** State both sides of the tension in one sentence, or let the contrast be
implied. Kill the pivot word.

---

## 5. Editorializing (the AI can't stop telling you it's important)

**What it is:** Sentences that tell the reader how to feel about the content
rather than letting the content speak.

**AI patterns:**
- "Here is the uncomfortable part."
- "This is the leap."
- "That is the point."
- "And that idea turns out to be more useful than anything you could hold in
  your hand."

**Why it's a tell:** Real humans trust the material. AI doesn't — it keeps
nudging you: "this matters," "this is the key," "here's why this is profound."

**Lockhart counterexample:**
> The status of our real number system ℝ is now reduced to that of a mere
> example, or instance, of the larger field concept.

He just states it. He doesn't tell you it's profound. He assumes you'll notice.

**Fix:** Delete every sentence that tells the reader what to think. If the idea
is good, it doesn't need a cheerleader. If it needs a cheerleader, the idea
isn't there yet.

---

## 6. The uniform paragraph

**What it is:** Every paragraph is 3-5 sentences, every paragraph has a topic
sentence, every paragraph reaches a conclusion. The page looks like a brick wall.

**Why it's a tell:** Human writing has one-sentence paragraphs. It has
seven-sentence paragraphs. It has paragraphs that are just a question.

**Lockhart:** His paragraphs range from 1 sentence to 8. Some end with a
question. Some are just a single thought dropped on the page.

**Fix:** Vary paragraph length. Put a one-liner between two dense paragraphs.
Let the shape of the page breathe.

---

## 7. The rhetorical question with immediate answer

**What it is:** "What does this mean? It means..." — the AI's favorite
transition device.

**Lockhart does ask questions — but differently:**
> How weird can a number system get and still obey our nine demands?

He asks and then *works through the answer over paragraphs*, not in the next
sentence. The question is genuine, not a setup.

**Fix:** If you ask a question, let the answer take time. Or don't ask — just
state the thing.

---

## 8. The "No X. No Y. No Z." pattern

**What it is:** Short negative declarations stacked for dramatic effect. A
subtype of the tricolon but with a distinctive AI fingerprint.

**AI example:**
> No width. No roughness. No grain of sand.

**Why it's a tell:** This is a speechwriter's device (think JFK: "not because
they are easy, but because they are hard"). AI learned it from political
rhetoric and applies it everywhere.

**Fix:** One strong statement. Or break the triplet into different structures.
Or don't use negative declarations at all — describe what something IS.

## The Lockhart voice — what to do instead

After studying ~120 lines of Lockhart's prose, here are the positive patterns:

1. **Ask genuine questions and work through them.** Not rhetorical setups.
2. **Use "we" naturally.** "So we have opened the barn door fairly wide..."
3. **Acknowledge limits.** "It is certainly not my intention to go into great
   detail here..."
4. **Use humor.** "much to their surprise and delight (not to mention
   mystification and horror)"
5. **Vary everything.** Sentence length, paragraph length, rhetorical mode.
6. **Trust the reader.** Don't tell them what's important. Just show them.
7. **Casual connectors.** "So," "Well," "As it happens," "In any event,"
   "Of course," "Luckily" — not "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Consequently."
8. **End mid-thought sometimes.** Let the next section pick it up, or don't.
9. **One concrete thing at a time.** Lockhart doesn't catalogue. He picks one
   example and works it.
10. **Write like you're at a blackboard with one other person.** Not like
    you're delivering a TED talk.

## Quick self-check (before submitting any BB draft)

Read the draft aloud. If any of these are true, rewrite:

- [ ] Three or more consecutive sentences start with the same word
- [ ] Three or more consecutive sentences have the same rhythm/length
- [ ] Any paragraph ends with a sentence that could be a tweet
- [ ] Any sentence tells the reader what to think or feel
- [ ] The word "and yet," "but here's the thing," or "this is the key" appears
- [ ] Any paragraph has exactly 3 sentences
- [ ] More than 2 em dashes in the entire piece
- [ ] Any sentence begins with "Every," "No," "Not," "Here is," or "This is"
  more than once
- [ ] The piece can't be read aloud without sounding rehearsed
