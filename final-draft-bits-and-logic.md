# Final Draft: Bits and Logic Gates

## BB 1132 - The Bit

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** binary-state

**Floor 0:** At the lowest level, a computer does not begin with words, pictures, or apps. It begins with a very small question.

**Floor 1:** Is electricity flowing through this tiny part of the circuit, or is it not?

**Floor 2:** If the path is open and no current flows, the computer treats that as **0**. If the path is closed and current can flow, it treats that as **1**.

**Floor 3:** That single choice, between **off** and **on**, is called a **bit**.

**Floor 4:** Every message, image, video, website, and program is built from these bits. Modern computing starts with billions of tiny switches being turned off and on at incredible speed.

## BB 1133 - The Math of Yes and No

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** boolean-logic

**Floor 0:** People often say computers run on 0s and 1s, which can make it sound as if the machine is doing advanced mathematics from the very beginning.

**Floor 1:** But at the bottom level, 0 and 1 are not really numbers in the usual sense. They are states.

**Floor 2:** **1** means yes, true, or on. **0** means no, false, or off.

**Floor 3:** In the 1800s, long before electronic computers existed, George Boole showed that reasoning could be handled through simple true-or-false statements.

**Floor 4:** His system, now called **Boolean logic**, uses rules such as **AND**, **OR**, and **NOT** to combine simple yes-or-no answers into more complex decisions.

---

## BB 1134 - The AND Gate

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** logic-gates

**Floor 0:** A computer turns Boolean logic into something physical by building circuits that follow those rules.

**Floor 1:** Imagine a wire carrying electricity to a lightbulb. Now place two switches on that same wire, one after the other.

**Floor 2:** For the bulb to turn on, electricity must pass through both switches.

**Floor 3:** If the first switch is closed **and** the second switch is closed, the path is complete. The bulb lights up.

**Floor 4:** If either switch is open, the path is broken. The bulb stays dark.

**Floor 5:** That is an **AND gate**. It only gives a yes answer when both inputs are yes.

---

## BB 1135 - The OR Gate

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** logic-gates

**Floor 0:** An **OR gate** uses a different arrangement.

**Floor 1:** Instead of placing two switches on one straight path, split the wire into two separate paths that meet again before the lightbulb.

**Floor 2:** Put one switch on the top path and one switch on the bottom path.

**Floor 3:** Now electricity only needs one open route. If the top switch is closed, the bulb turns on. If the bottom switch is closed, the bulb also turns on.

**Floor 4:** The bulb stays dark only when both switches are open.

**Floor 5:** That is an **OR gate**. It gives a yes answer when at least one input is yes.

---

## BB 1136 - A Water Model for Circuits

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** circuit-analogy

**Floor 0:** Electricity is hard to see, so it can help to borrow a simpler picture: water moving through pipes.

**Floor 1:** The comparison is not perfect, but it is useful. A wire can be imagined as a pipe, a switch as a valve, and a lightbulb as the place where flow produces a visible result.

**Floor 2:** A valve has two basic states. Open means water can flow. Closed means water stops.

**Floor 3:** That gives us the same basic pattern as a bit: flow or no flow, yes or no, 1 or 0.

---

## BB 1137 - The AND Pipe

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** logic-gates

**Floor 0:** The water model makes the AND rule easy to picture.

**Floor 1:** Start with one pipe leading to a faucet. Place two valves on the pipe, one after the other.

**Floor 2:** Water can reach the faucet only if both valves are open.

**Floor 3:** If the first valve is open but the second is closed, the water still stops. If the second valve is open but the first is closed, the water never reaches it.

**Floor 4:** Both valves must say yes before the faucet can run. That is the physical idea behind an AND gate.

---

## BB 1138 - The OR Pipe

**Subject:** computing | **Topic:** The Bit | **Concept:** logic-gates

**Floor 0:** The OR rule looks different because the pipe is arranged differently.

**Floor 1:** Split the main pipe into two branches, then join the branches again before the faucet.

**Floor 2:** Put one valve on each branch.

**Floor 3:** If either valve is open, water has a route to the faucet.

**Floor 4:** The faucet stays dry only when both valves are closed.

**Floor 5:** By changing the arrangement of the paths, we change the rule the system follows. That is the basic idea of a logic gate: a simple physical structure that produces a simple logical result.
