Integration from scratch

Everything here was built from one question: is integration just a complicated way of finding the area of shapes? It isn't — but the road to why is worth walking.

Every diagram is live. Drag the sliders.

1. The one idea

Straight edges are easy because nothing changes. A rectangle's height is the same all the way across, so area is one multiplication: height × width. Done.

Curves ruin that. The height is different at every single point, so there is no single number to multiply by. This is the entire problem, and integration is the entire solution:

The whole idea

Chop the region into slices so thin that the quantity is effectively constant across each one. Multiply. Add them all up. Then shrink the slices to nothing.

That's it. Turn one changing thing into infinitely many unchanging things. Everything below is either a demonstration that this works, a shortcut for doing it quickly, or an example of it being used on something that isn't a shape at all.

A picture to keep in your pocket

There's a version of this with no curves at all: bars for each day's pocket money, and a jar for the running total. Adding the bars fills the jar; subtracting two jar readings recovers one day. Section 7 is that picture in full. Until then, every diagram below is the same idea with the days made thinner and thinner.

Three things to try:

  • Start at 1 rectangle (use the jump buttons). Hopeless — about 36% off. That's the "shapes with edges" answer, and it fails because the height isn't constant.
  • Jump 20 → 40 → 80. Watch the error column roughly halve each time you double. That pattern is the whole game, and section 4 proves it.
  • Switch inscribed vs circumscribed. One is always too small, one always too big, and they squeeze toward the same number from both sides. That squeeze is the definition of the integral.

Midpoint is the surprise. Each rectangle overshoots on one side of its strip and undershoots on the other, and the errors largely cancel. It's accurate to four decimals by about 40 strips. That cancellation is a smooth-curve gift: on sharp corners or jumps, midpoint can still miss badly.

The middle card is not a memorised constant. It is the antiderivative of √(1−x²) evaluated at the ends — the shortcut from section 5, used here as the referee for the rectangles.

Why does inscribed always come out under the true area?

Because on each strip the inscribed rectangle takes the lowest height anywhere in that strip. The curve is at or above that height across the whole strip, so the rectangle can never contain more area than the true region. Every strip is short, so the total is short.

If the rectangles never fill the region, how can the answer ever be exact?

No individual sum is exact — each one is genuinely too small. The integral isn't any one of those sums; it's the number they close in on. The definition contains a limit, and the limit is exact even though no member of the sequence is.

Where does 1.5708 come from without memorising π/2?

An antiderivative of √(1−x²) is F(x) = (x√(1−x²) + arcsin x)/2. Then F(1) = (0 + π/2)/2 = π/4 and F(−1) = (0 − π/2)/2 = −π/4, so F(1) − F(−1) = π/2. Same answer the rectangles are chasing, found in one evaluation.

3. Why it converges

Chopping a curve into rectangles leaves little slivers between each rectangle's flat top and the curve above it — roughly triangles. The natural worry is that adding more rectangles means more slivers, so the error should pile up. It doesn't, and here's why.

The sliver is roughly a triangle, so its area is about ½ · w · Δh. Halve the strip width and two things shrink at once: w halves, and Δh halves too, because you're now sampling a shorter piece of the curve.

Each sliver shrinks by a factor of 4, but you only gain 2× as many of them. Four beats two, so the total loses. The error is racing to zero faster than the slivers can accumulate.

Why does Δh halve when the width halves?

Over a short stretch, a smooth curve looks like a straight line with some slope m. Rise equals slope times run, so Δh ≈ m·w. Halve the run and you halve the rise. This is exactly why the sliver — a product of the two — drops by four.

4. The algebra

The picture above is convincing, but it can be proved in four lines with no calculus at all — just monotonicity and one cancellation.

Setup. Take f decreasing on [a, b], cut into n strips of width w = (b − a)/n, with cut points x₀ = a, x₁, …, xₙ = b. The inscribed rectangle on strip i takes the lower height, f(xi+1).

Step one — trap each strip. Since f is decreasing, its value on strip i never leaves the band between f(xi+1) and f(xi). So the error on that strip is at most the band height times the width:

Ei ≤ w · [ f(xi) − f(xi+1) ]no calculus — just "the curve stays inside the band"

Step two — sum, and watch it collapse. Add these up and write the sum out in full:

[f(x₀) − f(x₁)] + [f(x₁) − f(x₂)] + … + [f(xn−1) − f(xₙ)]every interior term appears twice with opposite signs

Everything in the middle cancels. This is a telescoping sum, and it collapses to just the two endpoints: f(a) − f(b). Therefore:

E ≤ (b − a) · [ f(a) − f(b) ] / n  =  K / nK is fixed by the problem, not by n

The result. The error is bounded by a constant over n. Double the rectangles, halve the error ceiling. As n → ∞, the error goes to zero. That's the convergence, proved.

The intuition behind the collapse

The strips' errors are not independent — each one's height drop is where the next one starts. Stacked end to end, all n drops add up to the total drop of the function, which is a fixed number however finely you chop. You're dividing one fixed budget among n strips.

For your semicircle, apply the bound to the rising half [−1, 0] and the falling half [0, 1] separately — the curve is not monotone on the whole interval. Each half has height change 1 and width 1, so each contributes at most 1/nhalf; together the total variation is 2 and the span is 2, giving E ≤ 4/n for n strips across the full diameter. Loose but honest — and note the argument never needed f to be differentiable, which matters here because the semicircle's slope is infinite at the ends.

What if the curve wiggles up and down instead of just decreasing?

Split it into monotone pieces and apply the same argument to each. In general, replace f(a) − f(b) with the total variation V — the sum of all rises and falls. The bound becomes E ≤ (b−a)·V/n. Still 1/n, still zero in the limit. The semicircle paragraph above is exactly that: two monotone pieces, V = 2.

5. The shortcut

Everything so far tells you what the answer is. It's a terrible way to actually get it. Nobody computes integrals by adding rectangles — there's a shortcut, and it's a shocking one.

Imagine sweeping left to right across a curve, keeping a running total of the area so far. Call it A. How fast is A growing? Nudge one tiny step right and you tack on a sliver as tall as the curve and as thin as your step. So the rate A grows at is just the height of the curve right there.

Which means A is a function whose rate of change is f. That's exactly what an antiderivative is. Area and slopes turn out to be the same problem seen from two ends.

Worked example: the area under x²

The hard way. Chop [0, b] into n strips of width b/n. Using right edges, strip i has height (ib/n)², so the sum is

Σ (ib/n)² · (b/n) = (b³/n³) · n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 = (b³/3)(1 + 3/2n + 1/2n²)using the sum-of-squares identity

Let n → ∞ and the junk terms vanish, leaving b³/3. Notice the leading junk term is 3/2n — there's the 1/n error again, this time visible in the algebra rather than trusted from a bound.

The easy way. Ask what has derivative x². Since d/dx(x³) = 3x², the answer is x³/3. Then just evaluate at the ends:

∫0b x² dx = [ x³/3 ]0b = b³/3one line, no identities, no limit

At b = 2 that's 8/3 ≈ 2.667. Drag the slider to 2.00 and both left cards read 2.6667.

Why is there a "+ C" on indefinite integrals?

Because constants have zero slope. x³/3, x³/3 + 7 and x³/3 − 400 all have derivative x², so all are valid running totals — they just start the jar at different amounts. For a definite integral it never matters: you subtract two values and the constant cancels.

6. Slope without fear

Slope has a frightening name and a boring meaning: how much you get per step. Five kilometres per hour is a slope. Thirty pounds an hour is a slope. Anything phrased as "this per that" is a slope.

The best proof that it isn't intimidating: you read one through your windscreen every day. Your car has two dials.

Your odometer is the running total. Your speedometer is its slope. You've been reading a derivative your whole driving life without anyone calling it that.

The orange line is the tangent, and that word does a lot of unnecessary intimidating. All it means is: if you froze your speed right now and held it, this is the path the odometer would follow. Its steepness is your speed.

Now read the two panels in opposite directions:

  • Area under the speed curve → distance. That's integration.
  • Steepness of the distance curve → speed. That's differentiation.

"The running total's slope equals the curve's height" is a sentence you'd find obvious about a car: the faster you go, the faster the odometer climbs. Of course it does. The parabola was the same sentence in an unfamiliar accent.

Is a steep curve the same as a curve that bends sharply?

No — and this trips people up. Slope is steepness, not bendiness. A straight line can be violently steep and never bend at all. How fast the slope itself changes is a separate thing, the second derivative, which measures curvature.

Two operations, both learned at the age of seven. Adding up the daily bars gives the jar — that's integration. Subtracting two neighbouring jar totals gives back one day's money — that's differentiation. Obviously they undo each other: if you add £8 to the jar and then ask what changed, the answer is £8. Now the payoff. How much did you collect from day 3 through day 6? You could add: 2 + 8 + 4 + 6 = 20. Or read the jar twice — £28 on day 6, £8 on day 2 — and subtract. Also 20, with no adding.

Everything else on this page is this picture with the days made infinitely thin. That is the only difference. Calculus isn't a new idea; it's this idea with the steps shrunk to zero.

Each disc is a cylinder, and cylinder volume is easy: πr² × thickness. At position x the radius is √(1−x²), so r² = 1 − x². Stack and shrink:

V = ∫−11 π(1 − x²) dx = π[ x − x³/3 ]−11 = 4π/3the sphere volume formula, derived in two lines

Archimedes considered this his finest result and had it carved on his tombstone. Notice what changed in the machinery: nothing. You still chopped, multiplied, added and shrank. The only difference is you multiplied by πr² instead of a height.

A note on the disc count

Switch to inscribed radius and the "discs drawn" card falls below the slider value. That's not a bug in the drawing — the inscribed radius at the two poles is exactly zero, so those discs have no volume and nothing to render. Midpoint radii avoid it, and converge faster besides.

Signed area — when the curve goes below

Everything so far stayed above the axis. Real curves don't. Below the axis the "height" is negative, so each slice contributes a negative amount. The integral is then a signed total: area above counts positive, area below counts negative.

Ordinary area

How much paint to cover the region — always positive.

Definite integral

Net signed total — downs cancel ups. Distance vs displacement.

That's why ∫02π sin x dx = 0: equal lobes above and below. If you wanted paint, you'd integrate |sin x| instead. Same rectangles; the sign on the height is the only new rule.

The same trick in three other costumes

Work. Pulling a spring, the force grows as you stretch: F = kx. Over a tiny move the force is effectively constant, so the work is F·dx. Add them up: W = ∫0d kx dx = kd²/2.

Average value. The average of n numbers is their sum over n. Let the numbers become a continuum and the sum becomes an integral: (1/(b−a)) ∫ab f dx. That's how average speed comes out of the odometer trip.

Probability. A bell curve isn't measuring area, it's measuring chance. The height is a density: the chance of landing in a tiny window is p(x)·dx. Add the windows and P(a < X < b) = ∫ab p(x) dx. And ∫−∞∞ p = 1, because something must happen.

What actually is "dx"?

It's the width of a slice, after the shrinking. In the sum it's a real number w; in the integral it's the fossil of that width, telling you what you're slicing along. It also carries units, which is why ∫ power dt comes out in kilowatt-hours and not kilowatts.

9. Where it's used

The pattern never changes: whenever something varies continuously and you need the total, that's an integral. Rates to totals, densities to amounts. Reality varies continuously, so integration is everywhere — mostly hidden inside instruments and software nobody thinks of as doing calculus.

11. What's left

You have the concept, not yet the craft. Plenty of people pass calculus exams without the understanding on this page, but there is real machinery on top of it.

  • Techniques — substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions. Plumbing for finding antiderivatives. Tedious, learnable, increasingly done by software.
  • Numerical integration — because most real integrals have no closed form at all. There is no formula for ∫e−x². So computers go back to your rectangles, tuned up: Simpson's rule, Gaussian quadrature, Monte Carlo. Your first slider is a crude version of what production engineering code actually runs.
  • Multiple dimensions — double and triple integrals, slicing volumes and fields instead of lines.
  • Differential equations — where integration stops being about measuring and starts being about prediction. This is where it earns its keep in science.
  • Measure theory — the rigorous rebuild, for curves so badly behaved that the rectangle argument breaks.
If you remember one thing

Slice it thin enough that nothing changes across a slice. Multiply. Add. Shrink. Everything else is notation.
