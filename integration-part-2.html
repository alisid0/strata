Integration, algebraically

2. Doing one the hard way

Take f(x) = x² between 0 and b. We will do exactly what the train workshop did, but with letters, so the answer comes out as a formula rather than a number.

Cut it up. Divide the stretch from 0 to b into n strips. Each is b/n wide. Taking the height at the right-hand edge, strip number i reaches up to (ib/n)², so its area is that height times the width.

Sn = Σ (ib/n)² · (b/n)one strip, then all of them added

Pull out everything that is not i. Neither b nor n changes as you move from strip to strip, so they come out of the sum and leave only the squares behind.

Sn = (b³/n³) · Σ i²the sum of 1² + 2² + … + n²

Use the one fact you have to look up. The squares from 1 to n add to a known expression. This is the only outside ingredient in the whole derivation.

Σ i² = n(n+1)(2n+1) / 6true for every whole number n, provable by induction

Put it together and tidy. Multiplying out and dividing through by n³ gives a form where the dependence on n is obvious.

Sn = (b³/3)(1 + 3/2n + 1/2n²)every term with an n in it shrinks as n grows

Now look at what happens as n gets large. The bracket has a 1 in it that stays put, and two other terms that both fall towards zero. Drag the slider and watch them go.

3. The pattern

That derivation was a lot of work for one function. Doing it again for x³ would need the sum of cubes, and for x⁴ the sum of fourth powers, and so on for ever. Nobody does this. They do it a few times, notice the pattern, and prove it once in general.

The rule writes itself. Raise the power by one, then divide by the new power.

∫ xn dx = xn+1 / (n+1)for every n except −1, where dividing by n+1 would mean dividing by zero

This is the power rule, and between it and the two rules in section 5 you can integrate any polynomial you are likely to meet.

4. The shortcut

So far every answer has started from 0. Real questions rarely do, and there is no need to sum anything to handle the general case.

Part 1 had a jar. Money went in, the jar recorded the running total, and subtracting two jar readings gave back what arrived between them. Write F(x) for the running total of a function f, meaning the amount accumulated up to x. Then the amount accumulated between a and b is simply the jar at b minus the jar at a.

∫ba f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)where F is any function whose rate of growth is f

The functions in the right-hand column of the table above are exactly these running totals. x³/3 is the running total of x², and it grows at a rate of x². A function used this way is called an antiderivative of f.

Worked example. To integrate x² from 1 to 3, take the running total x³/3, read it at both ends, and subtract.

∫31 x² dx = [ x³/3 ]13 = 27/3 − 1/3 = 26/3the square brackets just mean "evaluate at the top, subtract the bottom"

Two summations, an induction proof and a limit, all replaced by one subtraction. This is why nobody integrates by adding strips unless they have to. It should also look suspicious, because nothing so far explains why undoing a rate of growth ought to tell you anything about adding up strips. Section 5 shows why it does.

Why the constant does not matter

Adding a fixed number to a running total does not change how fast it grows, so x³/3, x³/3 + 7 and x³/3 − 400 are all valid antiderivatives of x². They just start the jar with different amounts already in it. When you subtract one reading from the other the starting amount cancels, which is why it never affects a definite integral. It is written as + C when no limits are given.

5. Why the shortcut works

Section 4 asked you to believe something odd. Find a function whose rate of growth is f, read it at both ends, subtract, and out falls a total. There is no obvious reason why undoing a rate of change should tell you anything about adding up strips. This section shows why it does, in two halves, and neither half needs anything you have not already done.

Take f(x) = x² throughout, and write A(x) for the running total accumulated up to x. From section 2 that total is x³/3.

1What one thin strip adds

Push x from 3 to 3.1 and the jar gains a single thin strip. The running total reads 9 at x = 3 and 9.930333 at x = 3.1. How much did the jar gain, to four decimal places?

2Turn it into a rate

3Squeeze the window

4Recognise the number

The growth rates came out as 9.303, then 9.030. They are closing in on something. What is f(3), that is, 3²?

Answer step 3 first.

That is the first half, and it is worth stating plainly. The reason it happens is that a narrow strip is almost a rectangle of height f(x) and width h, so the jar gains about f(x)·h, and dividing by h leaves f(x). The narrower the strip, the flatter its top and the better that is.

A(x+h) − A(x) ≈ f(x) · h  ⇒   [ A(x+h) − A(x) ] / h → f(x)the running total grows at a rate equal to the function itself

Now the second half. You have one running total, A. Section 4 claimed that any function with the right rate of growth will do, which is a stronger claim and needs its own argument.

5Two functions, one rate

Suppose somebody hands you a different function F, and it also grows at rate f at every single point, exactly as A does. What can you conclude about F and A?

6Pin down the fixed amount

So A(x) = F(x) + C for some fixed C. Take F(x) = x³/3 and start totalling at a = 1. At the very start nothing has accumulated, so A(1) = 0. Given that F(1) = 1/3, what must C be? Give it to four decimal places.

Answer step 5 first.

7Collect the prize

You now know the running total exactly: A(x) = x³/3 − 1/3. The total accumulated from 1 to 2 is just the jar reading at 2. Work out A(2), to four decimal places.

Answer step 6 first.

Look at what you just wrote down. A(2) = F(2) − F(1), and you never chose F for any reason other than that its rate of growth was right. That is the whole shortcut, derived rather than asserted, and it explains the loose end from section 4: the fixed amount C cancels because it appears in both readings and gets subtracted away.

The argument in four lines

A thin strip is nearly a rectangle, so the running total grows at the rate of the function: A′ = f.
Any F with F′ = f therefore grows exactly as A does, so it can differ only by a fixed amount.
The running total starts at nothing, which fixes that amount at −F(a).
So the total from a to b is F(b) − F(a).

And where the power rule comes from

One formula is still unexplained. The power rule works because x³/3 really does grow at rate x², and that can be checked with school algebra and no calculus at all. Expand a small step and see what survives.

(x + h)³ − x³ = 3x²h + 3xh² + h³multiply out and the x³ terms cancel
÷ h  →  3x² + 3xh + h²  →   3x²every term after the first still carries an h, so all of them vanish

So x³ grows at rate 3x², and dividing by 3 gives a function growing at rate x², which is x³/3. The same expansion works for any power: multiply out (x+h)n and the only term without a spare h in it is n·xn−1·h. That is where the n comes down and the power drops by one, and running it backwards is exactly the rule from section 3: raise the power, divide by the new one.

6. Rules and practice

Two more rules and you have everything needed for polynomials. Both are obvious once stated, and both follow from the fact that an integral is a sum.

∫ c · f(x) dx = c ∫ f(x) dxa constant multiplier can be moved outside
∫ [ f(x) + g(x) ] dx = ∫ f(x) dx + ∫ g(x) dxa sum can be split up and done piece by piece

The first says that doubling every strip doubles the total, which needs no defence. The second says you can add two quantities before totalling or after, and get the same answer, which needs no defence either.

Worked example. For 2x + 3, split the sum, move each constant outside, and use the power rule on what is left. Remember that 3 means 3x⁰, so its running total is 3x.

∫40 (2x + 3) dx = [ x² + 3x ]04 = (16 + 12) − 0 = 28two power rules and a subtraction

Where that leaves us

You can now integrate any polynomial without drawing anything. Raise the power by one and divide by the new power, handle constants and sums by splitting them off, then read the running total at both ends and subtract. Everything harder than a polynomial, which means fractions, roots, exponentials, sines and the rest, is a matter of knowing more running totals, and those are looked up in a table or worked out with techniques built on these same rules.

Worth keeping in view: none of this replaced what part 1 did. The strips are still what an integral is. The algebra is a way of avoiding having to add them up one at a time, and it only works when someone has already found a running total for the function in front of you. When nobody has, and for most functions that arise in practice nobody has, you go back to cutting the stretch into pieces and adding, exactly as you did on the train.
