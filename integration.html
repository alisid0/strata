Integration

An introduction for anyone who has never met integration, or has met it and come away none the wiser. It starts with the area of a rectangle and ends with working out how far a train travelled. Nothing is assumed beyond multiplying and adding.

Curves are a bit more complex. The height is different at every point along the way, so there is no single number to multiply by. Whichever height you pick is correct at one place and wrong everywhere else, which is why all three answers miss.

But notice what went wrong. The height only causes trouble because it changes across the width you are multiplying by. Over a narrow enough strip it barely changes at all. So cut the shape into narrow strips, treat each one as a rectangle, and add them up:

Every time you cut the strips in half, the leftover gaps get smaller and the total gets closer. Nothing here is new: each strip is worked out with the same multiplication you did on the very first shape. The only change is that you do it many times over and add up the results.

The whole idea

Cut the shape into strips narrow enough that the height barely changes across any one of them. Work out each strip as a rectangle, add them all up, then make the strips narrower still. That is integration, and everything that follows is either a demonstration that it works, a faster way to do it, or an example of it being used on something that is not a shape at all.

The next section is exactly this, in a setting with no curves and nothing that needs to shrink.

There are two questions worth asking about that jar. Neither needs anything beyond primary school arithmetic, although both have formal names that make them sound harder than they are.

Going one way

On Sunday you got £3, on Monday £5 and on Tuesday £2. How much is in the jar at the end of Tuesday?

£

You added the days up to get the jar, which is exactly what you did in section 1 when you added the strips up to get the area. That operation is called integration. The second question runs in the opposite direction.

Going the other way

Now suppose the daily amounts have been lost and the jar is all you have left. It held £18 at the end of Wednesday and £22 at the end of Thursday. How much did you get on Thursday?

£

You subtracted two jar readings to recover a single day, and that operation is called differentiation. It should be clear enough why the two undo one another. If you put £8 into the jar and then ask how much the jar changed by, the answer has to be £8. There is nothing to prove.

Both operations are available to you whenever you want the total for a stretch of several days, and one of them turns out to be a good deal less work than the other.

Two routes, one answer

How much did you collect from Tuesday to Friday?

£

Once you have the answer, compare how you could have got it.

The short way never looked at a single day's money. It read the jar twice and took the difference, turning four additions into one subtraction. Written down, that is the shortcut:

∫ba f = F(b) − F(a)the jar at the end, minus the jar at the start

The symbols are new but nothing else is. f is the daily money, F is the jar, and a and b are the two days you read it on.

The short version

The bars are the curve. The jar is the area underneath it.
Filling the jar from the bars is integration.
Getting one bar back out of the jar is differentiation.
For a run of several bars, read the jar twice and subtract rather than adding them up.

The rest of the page is this same picture with the days made thinner and thinner. Nothing else changes. Calculus is not a new idea sitting on top of this one. It is this one, with the steps shrunk to nothing.

Each rectangle is drawn tall enough to cover the highest point the curve reaches within its own strip, so the whole staircase sits over the semicircle and the total always comes out too big. Two things to try:

  • Start at 1 rectangle (use the jump buttons). A single rectangle gives 2.00 against a true area of 1.57, so it is about 27% over. That is the straight-edge answer applied to a shape that is not straight, and it fails for exactly the reason section 1 gave.
  • Jump 20 → 40 → 80. Each time you double the number of rectangles, the overshoot roughly halves: 0.081, then 0.043, then 0.023. That relationship is what makes the method work, and section 4 shows why it holds.

The overshoot never quite reaches zero at any particular number of rectangles, but you can make it as small as you like by using enough of them. The number all these totals are closing in on is what we call the integral.

The middle card is not a number we looked up. It is the jar trick from section 2 applied to this curve: find the running total F, read it at both ends, subtract. It is there so you have something to check the rectangles against.

Why is the total always too big?

Because on each strip the rectangle is given the highest height the curve reaches anywhere in that strip. The curve is at or below that height across the whole strip, so the rectangle always covers the region beneath it and a little more besides. Every strip overshoots, so the total overshoots.

If every total is too big, how can the answer ever be exact?

No individual sum is exact, because every one of them is genuinely too big. The integral is not any one of those sums. It is the number they close in on. The definition contains a limit, and the limit is exact even though no member of the sequence is.

Where does 1.5708 come from without memorising π/2?

An antiderivative of √(1−x²) is F(x) = (x√(1−x²) + arcsin x)/2. Then F(1) = (0 + π/2)/2 = π/4 and F(−1) = (0 − π/2)/2 = −π/4, so F(1) − F(−1) = π/2. That is the same number the rectangles are working towards, but here it took a single calculation.

4. Why it converges

Because each rectangle is tall enough to cover the highest point of its strip, a thin wedge of it pokes out above the curve. Those wedges are roughly triangular, and together they are exactly the overshoot you saw in section 3. You might reasonably worry that using more rectangles means more wedges, so the error would build up rather than shrink. It does not, and this section explains why.

Each wedge is roughly a triangle, so its area is about ½ · w · Δh, where w is the width of the strip and Δh is how far the curve falls across it. If you halve the strip width, two things shrink at the same time. The width w halves, and so does Δh, because a narrower strip covers a shorter piece of curve and the curve has less room to fall.

Because both w and Δh halve, each wedge shrinks to a quarter of its previous area, while the number of wedges only doubles. Shrinking by four while multiplying by two leaves you with half the total error. So every time you halve the strip width, the error halves as well, and it goes to zero.

Why does Δh halve when the width halves?

Over a short stretch, a smooth curve is close to a straight line with some slope m. For a straight line, the change in height is the slope times the run, so Δh ≈ m·w. Halve the run and the height change halves with it. Since the wedge's area depends on both the width and that height change, halving the width brings both down, and the area drops to a quarter.

5. Workshop: how far did the train go?

An old locomotive pulls out of a station onto a rising gradient. It is heavy and builds speed slowly, leaving at 20 km/h and reaching only 80 km/h an hour later. You are sitting in the cab watching the speed the whole way, so you know how fast it was going at every moment. What you want to know is how far it went.

Here is the whole journey. The line is the speed at every moment of that hour.

The journey

Speed against time. The train starts at 20 km per hour and rises to 80 km per hour after one hour, slowly at first and more steeply later. It passes 50 km per hour at about 41 minutes.

The question is simple to ask and surprisingly awkward to answer. How far did it go?

1When the speed holds still

Start with a case that gives no trouble. A train holds a steady 50 km/h for half an hour. How far does it go?

2The obvious guess

Our train is not so obliging. It starts at 20 km/h and ends at 80 km/h. The natural thing to try is to split the difference: take the average of those two speeds and treat the train as though it held that average for the whole hour. What distance does that give?

3Why that guess is too generous

It does not cross that line until about 41 minutes in, so for roughly two thirds of the hour the train was travelling slower than 50 km/h, and only near the end was it faster. Every reading is stale the moment you take it, because the train keeps accelerating after you look. The obvious fix is to look more often, so each reading has less time to go out of date. Take four readings, one every quarter hour: 20, 26, 38 and 56 km/h, each held for 0.25 hours. What total do you get now?

So the train covered 42 km, and its average speed over the hour was 42 km/h, not the 50 km/h you would get by averaging the two ends. The difference is the whole difficulty in one number. Averaging the ends treats the slow start and the fast finish as though the train spent equal time in each, when in truth it laboured below 50 km/h for about forty of the sixty minutes.

What you just did

You could not use distance = speed × time on the journey as a whole, because there was no single speed to put in it. So you chopped the hour into stretches short enough that the speed barely moved during any one of them, used the formula on each stretch where it did apply, and added the results. Then you made the stretches shorter until the answer stopped changing. That is integration, and it is the same move as cutting a curved shape into strips: find the places where the awkward thing holds still, solve those, and add.

One loose end worth knowing about. Every one of your totals came out too low, because each reading went stale as the train accelerated. Had you instead taken each reading at the end of its stretch, every total would have come out too high. Four readings that way give 50 km. So without knowing the answer at all, you could have said the distance was somewhere between 35 and 50 km, and narrowed that gap as far as you liked by reading more often.

Where that leaves us

Cut the shape into strips narrow enough that the height barely changes across any one of them. Work each strip out as a rectangle, the way you did with the very first shape on this page. Add them up, then make the strips narrower still. Section 2 showed those same two moves in whole pounds, building a total up and then taking one back apart. Section 4 showed that the error really does shrink away rather than piling up, and section 5 showed that none of it was ever about area in the first place.

Everything else in calculus is machinery built on top of that: quicker ways to find the running total, ways of proving it behaves for badly behaved curves, and the same slicing applied to volumes, forces and probabilities rather than areas. The machinery gets large. The idea does not get any bigger than this.
