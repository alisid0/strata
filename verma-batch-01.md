## BB-NEW-01 — Why we need standard units

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** measurement | **Concept:** standard-units; CGPM | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB 01]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You can't meaningfully say how heavy an elephant is without agreeing on what "heavy" means. A number alone means nothing — it needs a unit attached, and that unit must mean the same thing to everyone who uses it.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>An elephant is 200 times the mass of a goat. That statement works because both are compared to the same invisible standard — a unit mass. Without that standard, "200" is just a number with no physical meaning. All measurement works this way: a physical quantity is expressed as a numerical value times a unit. "2 hours" means two times the standard unit called an hour.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>A <strong>unit</strong> is a fixed, internationally agreed quantity of a physical dimension used as a reference for measurement. The numerical value of any measurement is the ratio of the measured quantity to this reference.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The international body that decides units is the CGPM — the General Conference on Weights and Measures. It meets periodically and any change to standard units is communicated worldwide. This ensures that a metre in Delhi is the same length as a metre in Tokyo. Before such bodies existed, every region had its own units — cubits, feet, stones — which made trade and science across borders nearly impossible.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing an elephant and a goat balanced on opposite sides of a giant scale, with a standard 1 kg block in the centre as the reference. Chalk style on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-02 — Fundamental and derived quantities

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** measurement | **Concept:** fundamental-quantities; derived-quantities; base-units | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-01]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Not all quantities need their own independently defined unit. If you define length, area comes free. If you define length and time, speed comes free. Most quantities can be built from a small set of foundation pieces.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Make a square with each side equal to the unit length. That square's area is the unit of area — it wasn't defined separately. If a particle covers one unit of length in one unit of time, we call that a unit of speed. Speed was never independently defined either; it emerged from length and time.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p><strong>Fundamental quantities</strong> (also called base quantities) are a set of mutually independent physical quantities from which all others can be derived by multiplication and division. There are exactly seven of them. Their units are <strong>fundamental units</strong>. All other quantities are <strong>derived quantities</strong> and carry <strong>derived units</strong>.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The choice of which quantities are fundamental is partly conventional. Length, mass, and time are the three most familiar. But one could alternatively take speed and time as fundamental and derive length from them. Different systems of units exist precisely because different choices of base quantities can be made. The SI system settled on seven: length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, and luminous intensity.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing the seven base quantity symbols (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd) arranged in a circle, with arrows radiating outward to derived quantities like area, speed, force, energy. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-03 — The seven pillars of SI

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** measurement | **Concept:** SI-units; base-units; prefixes | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-02]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every measurement in science rests on just seven foundation blocks. The entire edifice of physics — from the mass of an electron to the distance of a quasar — is expressed in combinations of these seven units.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>The seven SI base units are: the metre (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, the second (s) for time, the ampere (A) for electric current, the kelvin (K) for temperature, the mole (mol) for amount of substance, and the candela (cd) for luminous intensity. Two supplementary units exist for angles: the radian (rad) for plane angle and the steradian (sr) for solid angle.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>Since May 2019, all seven SI base units are defined in terms of fixed numerical values assigned to universal physical constants — not by physical artefacts. The metre is defined by fixing the speed of light at exactly 299,792,458 m/s. The kilogram is defined by fixing Planck's constant at exactly 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. The second is defined by fixing the caesium hyperfine transition frequency. These definitions are invariable and universally available.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Because physical quantities vary across enormous ranges — from the size of a proton (10⁻¹⁵ m) to the distance of a quasar (10²⁶ m) — SI uses standard prefixes: kilo (10³), mega (10⁶), giga (10⁹) on the large side, and milli (10⁻³), micro (10⁻⁶), nano (10⁻⁹) on the small side. A nanometre is a billionth of a metre; a gigawatt is a billion watts. The prefix carries the scale, the base unit carries the kind of quantity.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing seven labelled pillars (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd) supporting a roof labelled "SI", with prefix labels (nano, micro, milli, kilo, mega, giga) as horizontal rungs connecting quantities of different scales. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-04 — Dimensions: the fingerprint of a physical quantity

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** measurement | **Concept:** dimensions; dimensional-formula; MLT | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-03]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Every physical quantity carries a hidden code written in the alphabet of base quantities. Speed is always length divided by time. Force is always mass times length divided by time squared. This code — the quantity's dimensions — does not depend on units and never changes.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Force equals mass times acceleration. Acceleration is velocity divided by time. Velocity is length divided by time. Chaining these together: force = mass × (length/time) / time = mass × length × time⁻². So force has dimensions of 1 in mass, 1 in length, and −2 in time — written compactly as MLT⁻². This is called the <strong>dimensional formula</strong> of force.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>dimension</strong> of a physical quantity in a given base quantity is the exponent to which that base quantity is raised in the expression of the quantity. The <strong>dimensional formula</strong> is the product of the base quantities raised to their respective exponents: [quantity] = Mᵃ Lᵇ Tᶜ Iᵈ Kᵉ molᶠ cdᵍ. A quantity with all exponents zero is <strong>dimensionless</strong>.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Dimensions are not units. Speed can be expressed in m/s, km/h, or miles per hour — its numerical value changes but its dimensions (LT⁻¹) remain identical. A dimensionless quantity like an angle or a pure number has no dimensions regardless of the unit system. Knowing a quantity's dimensions lets you check whether equations make physical sense without doing any arithmetic at all.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing the chain: force → mass × acceleration → mass × velocity/time → mass × length/time² → MLT⁻². Each step is an arrow with a brief annotation. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-05 — The homogeneity principle

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** measurement | **Concept:** dimensional-homogeneity; equation-checking | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-04]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You cannot add a speed to a force. You cannot subtract an electric current from a temperature. Every term in a physically meaningful equation must be the same kind of thing — the same dimensions. This simple rule catches errors before any calculation is done.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Take the kinematics equation: x = ut + ½at². The left side x is a length, dimension L. The first term ut is velocity × time = (L/T) × T = L. The second term ½at²: the ½ is dimensionless, acceleration is L/T², multiplied by T² gives L. All three terms have the same dimension, L. The equation passes the test — it may be correct.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>principle of homogeneity of dimensions</strong> states that in any valid physical equation, every term must have identical dimensions. An equation that fails this test is necessarily wrong. An equation that passes it may be correct, but dimensional homogeneity alone is not a guarantee — numerical factors must still be verified.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>This principle is a fast filter. If a student writes F = mv (force equals mass times velocity), a quick dimensional check reveals: [F] = MLT⁻², but [mv] = M × LT⁻¹ = MLT⁻¹. The dimensions do not match, so the equation is impossible. The correct form, F = ma, gives MLT⁻² on both sides. Dimensional analysis cannot tell you the ½ in ½at², but it can tell you that at² belongs there and at does not.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing two sides of a balance scale: left side labelled [F] = MLT⁻², right side labelled [ma] = MLT⁻² — balanced. Below, a crossed-out unbalanced scale: [F] = MLT⁻² vs [mv] = MLT⁻¹. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-06 — Order of magnitude

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** measurement | **Concept:** order-of-magnitude; powers-of-ten; estimation | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB-NEW-03]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Sometimes you don't need an exact number. You need to know whether something is roughly a thousand, a million, or a billion. Knowing the right ballpark — the order of magnitude — is often enough to catch nonsense and make sense of the world.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>The mass of an electron is about 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg. The mass of our galaxy is about 2.2 × 10⁴¹ kg. Saying "an electron is very light and a galaxy is very heavy" is vague. Saying the galaxy is about 10⁷² times heavier — a 1 followed by 72 zeros — pins it down. The exponent, 72, is the order-of-magnitude difference between them.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>order of magnitude</strong> of a quantity is the power of ten closest to its numerical value when expressed in a consistent unit. If a value is N = a × 10ᵇ where 0.5 ≤ a < 5, the order of magnitude is 10ᵇ. If a ≥ 5, it rounds to 10ᵇ⁺¹. Orders of magnitude compress an enormous range — the 10⁶¹ span from subatomic to cosmic scales — into about 60 manageable steps.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Physicists use order-of-magnitude estimates constantly. When Enrico Fermi asked his students "how many piano tuners are there in Chicago?", he wasn't looking for an exact answer — he was training them to break an unfamiliar problem into estimable pieces, multiply rough numbers, and land in the right ballpark. A Fermi estimate that's off by a factor of 100 may still rule out a thousand impossible answers.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing a logarithmic scale from 10⁻³⁵ to 10⁴¹, with labelled markers: electron mass, proton size, human height, Earth diameter, galaxy mass. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-07 — The structure of the world

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** foundations | **Concept:** scale-of-universe; hierarchy-of-matter | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [BB 01, BB-NEW-06]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Reality is arranged in layers, each about a million times larger or smaller than the next. The same laws of physics that hold atoms together also govern the motion of galaxies — but they play out very differently at each rung of this ladder.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Start at the human scale, roughly 1 metre. Go down: a grain of sand is about 10⁻³ m (a thousand times smaller). A cell is 10⁻⁵ m. An atom is 10⁻¹⁰ m. A proton is 10⁻¹⁵ m. Quarks, as far as we know, are below 10⁻¹⁸ m. Go up: a city is 10⁴ m. Earth is 10⁷ m. The solar system is 10¹³ m. The Milky Way is 10²¹ m. The observable universe is about 10²⁶ m.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The physical world is hierarchically structured: subatomic particles form atoms, atoms form molecules, molecules form condensed matter, matter forms planets and stars, stars form galaxies, galaxies form clusters and superclusters. At each level, new phenomena emerge that do not exist at the level below. Temperature is meaningless for a single atom but dominates the behaviour of a gas.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Physics operates at every rung simultaneously. Quantum mechanics governs the subatomic, classical mechanics governs the everyday, general relativity governs the cosmic. A complete description of nature requires all of them, and one of the great unsolved problems in physics is that they do not yet fit together into a single framework. The hunt for a unified theory is, in part, the hunt for the rungs that connect them.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing a vertical ladder with rungs labelled: quarks → protons → atoms → molecules → cells → humans → Earth → solar system → galaxy → universe. Each rung has the approximate size in metres in pale yellow chalk. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-08 — Adding vectors: the triangle rule

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** vectors | **Concept:** vector-addition; triangle-rule; parallelogram-law | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 21]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>You can't add vectors by adding their numbers. A force of 3 N east plus a force of 4 N north is not 7 N. The two arrows point in different directions, and the real combined effect is a single diagonal arrow — shorter than the sum of the two lengths.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>To add two vectors, draw the first arrow. Then draw the second arrow starting from the tip of the first, keeping its length and direction exactly as they were. The vector from the very start of the first arrow to the very tip of the second arrow is the <strong>sum</strong>. This is the <strong>triangle rule</strong> of vector addition. Alternatively, draw both arrows starting from the same point, complete the parallelogram they form, and the diagonal through that common point is the sum — the <strong>parallelogram law</strong>, which is the same rule stated differently.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>If a⃗ and b⃗ are two vectors, their sum a⃗ + b⃗ is obtained by placing the tail of b⃗ at the head of a⃗ and drawing the vector from the tail of a⃗ to the head of b⃗. Vector addition is commutative (a⃗ + b⃗ = b⃗ + a⃗) and associative ((a⃗ + b⃗) + c⃗ = a⃗ + (b⃗ + c⃗)). The magnitude of the sum depends on the angle between the vectors and is given by the law of cosines.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>If two vectors point in the same direction, their magnitudes add directly. If they point in opposite directions, the magnitudes subtract. At 90°, the magnitude of the sum is √(a² + b²), by Pythagoras. At any other angle, the law of cosines applies. This is why rowing with the current is fastest, rowing against it is slowest, and rowing across it gives the diagonal — the same vector addition from Card 22, now formalised.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing two vectors a and b drawn tip-to-tail, with the resultant vector drawn from the start of a to the tip of b. Below, the parallelogram construction: both vectors from the same origin, with the diagonal highlighted. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-09 — The dot product: when two vectors multiply to a scalar

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** vectors | **Concept:** dot-product; scalar-product; work | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 21, BB-NEW-08]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Multiply two vectors and sometimes you want a single number back, not another arrow. How much of one vector lies along the direction of another? The dot product answers that question.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Push a box across the floor. If you push straight forward, all your effort moves the box. If you push downward at an angle, some of your force is wasted pressing the box into the ground. The part of your force that actually moves the box forward is the force times the cosine of the angle between your push and the direction of motion. This is exactly what the dot product computes.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>dot product</strong> (or scalar product) of two vectors a⃗ and b⃗ is a⃗ · b⃗ = |a||b| cos θ, where θ is the angle between them. The result is a scalar, not a vector. If the vectors are parallel (θ = 0), cos 0 = 1, and a⃗ · b⃗ = |a||b| — full multiplication. If they are perpendicular (θ = 90°), cos 90° = 0, and the dot product is zero. In component form: a⃗ · b⃗ = aₓbₓ + aᵧbᵧ + a𝓏b𝓏.</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>The dot product appears everywhere in physics. Work is the dot product of force and displacement: W = F⃗ · d⃗. Power is the dot product of force and velocity. Magnetic flux is the dot product of the magnetic field and the area vector. Each time, the dot product picks out exactly the component that matters — the part of one vector aligned with the other — and discards the rest.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing two vectors a⃗ and b⃗ with angle θ between them. A dashed line drops from the tip of a⃗ onto b⃗, showing the projection |a|cosθ. The formula a⃗·b⃗ = |a||b|cosθ appears below. Chalk on dark green board.

## BB-NEW-10 — The cross product: when two vectors multiply to a new vector

**Subject:** physics | **Topic:** vectors | **Concept:** cross-product; vector-product; torque | **Ground:** g0 | **Builds on:** [Card 21, BB-NEW-08, BB-NEW-09]

**Floor 0 (Idea):**
<p>Sometimes multiplying two vectors doesn't give you a number — it gives you a third vector, pointing in a direction neither of the original two pointed. This is the cross product, and it captures the physics of twisting and spinning.</p>

**Floor 1 (Concrete):**
<p>Turn a wrench to loosen a bolt. You apply a force at the end of the handle, at some distance from the bolt. If you pull directly away from the bolt, nothing turns. If you push at a right angle to the handle, you get maximum turning effect. The twisting power — the <strong>torque</strong> — depends on both the force and the distance, but also on the angle between them. The cross product bundles all three: magnitude, direction, and angle, into one resulting vector.</p>

**Floor 2 (Definition):**
<p>The <strong>cross product</strong> (or vector product) of two vectors a⃗ and b⃗ is a⃗ × b⃗ = |a||b| sin θ n̂, where n̂ is a unit vector perpendicular to both a⃗ and b⃗, with direction given by the right-hand rule. The magnitude is |a||b| sin θ. If the vectors are parallel (θ = 0), the cross product is zero. If they are perpendicular, the magnitude is |a||b|. Unlike the dot product, the cross product is <strong>anti-commutative</strong>: a⃗ × b⃗ = −(b⃗ × a⃗).</p>

**Floor 3 (In action):**
<p>Torque is the cross product of position and force: τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗. Angular momentum is the cross product of position and momentum: L⃗ = r⃗ × p⃗. The force on a moving charge in a magnetic field is the cross product: F⃗ = q(v⃗ × B⃗). In every case, the cross product produces a vector perpendicular to the plane of the two inputs — the axis about which the twisting or spinning occurs.</p>

**Image prompt:** A chalk diagram showing two vectors a⃗ and b⃗ with angle θ, and a third vector a⃗×b⃗ emerging perpendicular to the plane of the page. A right-hand rule illustration: fingers curling from a⃗ to b⃗, thumb pointing in the direction of the cross product. Chalk on dark green board.
